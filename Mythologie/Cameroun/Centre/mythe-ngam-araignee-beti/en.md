# Ngam, the Spider Who Went to Call God to Account

A Beti myth (Ewondo, Eton and their neighbours), Centre Region, Cameroon. Version 1, 16 October 2026, written from the research file `recherche.md` (same folder). It brings together entries B2 and B3 of the Centre inventory, with their echoes among the Bafia (F6) and the Sanaga peoples (S3).

> This story is a legend from oral tradition. The griot tells it as a legend. Divination with the trapdoor spider, however, is a real practice, described by Ewondo authors, ethnologists and colonial administrators: at every step, the griot tells you what belongs to the tale and what belongs to history.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. The hole at the foot of the house

Listen.

In the old days of the Ewondo country, before a man set out on a journey, before he took a wife, before he cleared a stretch of forest or avenged an insult, he did not decide alone. He went to see someone. Not the chief: the Beti had no king. Not a priest: the God of the sky received few offerings. He went to see a man who knew how to speak to a hole in the ground.

These are not my words. An Ewondo priest, Father Théodore Tsala, wrote them in 1955 in his great dictionary of the language: "In times past, the Ewondo undertook nothing serious without first consulting the oracle."

Picture the scene as Tsala describes it. Somewhere near the houses, a round hole in the beaten earth, barely wider than a thumb. Around that hole, a tiny courtyard has been laid out, "less than a square metre, bounded by small logs." When evening comes, the man who knows sets small sticks inside that miniature yard. They are called *avol*. Each one has its own name, according to its shape and colour: *nkōg*, *nkul ovol*, *ntōngō ovol*, *mfim ovol*, *mbë-nda ovol*, *mkpada*, *mbeb ovol*, and more besides. Then he covers them carefully with banana leaves. And he goes off to sleep.

In the night, something comes out of the hole.

Something large, dark and hairy, that lives only in the dark. Eight thick legs, a heavy body, a slow gait. The creature searches for a way out of the little yard built around its home, and as it searches, it knocks the sticks about. In the morning, the man comes back. He lifts the leaves. He looks at where the *avol* have fallen, which one has moved, which one has stayed put, which one lies across another. And in the disorder left by the night, he reads the future.

The Ewondo call this creature *ngam*. It is the mygale, the great ground spider. And the word *ngam* came to mean far more than an animal. Tsala says so: the spider is "the most important" of all the means of questioning the future, the one "which, moreover, has given its name to every kind of divination." *Ngam* is the spider. *Ngam* is the oracle. *Ngam* is the very act of seeking what is hidden. And the one who consults it, Tsala calls *mod ngam*, "the man of the ngam": the diviner.

So a question arises, the question a child always asks: why her? Why this frightening creature that lives underground and only comes out at night? Why should it be the spider, and not the elephant, not the leopard, not the eagle, that knows what people do not?

The Beti have an answer. It fits inside a story. A story of insolence, of a trial, of divine anger and of a sky that moves away. A story in which a small creature dares what no one else dares: to call God to account.

But before we hear it, we must know whom the spider dared to address.

### II. The stake that holds people up

The Beti of the Centre's forests, Ewondo, Eton, Bënë, Manguissa and their kin, lived in lineages. The *mvog*, "the people of", the descendants of one ancestor. No throne above them, no capital. Elders, families bound together by marriage, villages scattered through the forest, and a great freedom that the first Europeans mistook for disorder.

Above this world there was a God. The sources give him several names: Zamba, Zama, Ntondobe. The research note attached to one version of our myth adds Zobe and Zambe. The names change from village to village, from one era to the next. But one of them is worth pausing over, because it says everything about how the Beti thought of God.

*Ntondobe*. Tsala, once again, breaks it down: *ntondo bod*, "support of people." And the word *ntondo*, in his dictionary, has a very concrete meaning: it is the stake, the prop, the pole. It is the stick you plant beside a climbing plant too weak to stand on its own. His example is the stake that holds up a yam vine.

Stay with that image. In a land of farmers, God is not first of all a king on a throne. He is the stake planted beside the human stem, the thing that keeps people upright. A doctoral thesis defended in 2022 at the University of Yaoundé I, by the historian Alice Mathilde Ekani Zinga, gives the same translation, "Support of people", following the anthropologist Anselme Nana, and adds another name: *Medan Yobo*, "he who dwells beyond the vault of the firmament." The world, these sources say, has two storeys: *Yob ai Si*, Sky and Earth. God above. People below.

Between the two, there is no emptiness. The great ethnologist Philippe Laburthe-Tolra, who spent years among the Beti, described a universe filled with spirits, the *minkug*, which Ekani Zinga's thesis summarises after him: the little spirits of the forest, the mountains and the waters; the dead who wander around the village and protect their descendants; the animal guardians of a person or a clan. And in that list there is one line, a single one, that concerns us: "the spirit of divination that takes flesh in the mygale."

So this is the setting. A God who is a stake, up there, beyond the vault. People below, with their illnesses, their mourning, their disabilities. And in the earth, beneath the houses, a spider that shelters a spirit.

Now, people suffer. They are born blind. They are born without a leg. They bury their children. And if God is the support of people, then the question comes on its own, the oldest question in the world: why does the stake let the plant fall?

The Beti handed that question to the spider.

### III. The trial of Nden-Bobo

It is 23 January 1963, in Tala, in Eton country, some sixty kilometres north-west of Yaoundé and only about ten kilometres from the Sanaga River. In the presbytery of the Catholic mission, two researchers, Gaspard and Françoise Towo-Atangana, have organised an evening gathering. There are women, catechists, tales, sung stories, dance songs. And there is a mvet player.

The mvet is the harp-zither of the Beti, Bulu and Fang peoples: a stalk of raffia bamboo a metre or more long, whose bark is peeled into thin strips that become strings, raised on a central bridge, with gourds attached to make the sound resonate. The mvet player, the Towo-Atangana write, is a wanderer, an artist who can keep a village awake for whole nights, "a sorcerer at times, a prophet at others."

It is past midnight. The catechists have led their flock home. Someone asks the player for a piece of his own choosing, one whose age he can vouch for. And he announces the story of Nden-Bobo. He warns them: he will only tell a slice of it, because a whole night would barely be enough.

He introduces himself: "I, Vincent Mesi, of Tala, of the village of Elik Mesi-Ada, I am the one telling you the story called Weaver-Spider."

What follows is the legend, as Vincent Mesi sang it that night, and as the Towo-Atangana transcribed it in Eton and translated it into French in the journal *Africa* in 1966. I tell it following their translation, and when I quote, the words are theirs, rendered here in English.

First, the name. *Nden-Bobo*. The Towo-Atangana explain it: in Eton, *nden* means the spider's web, and *lebob* means the spider. Nden-Bobo is the Weaver-Spider, the one who spins. And the singer keeps giving her a title, like a refrain: *Monyan a Ngama*, "Sister of Mygale." The spider who weaves is the sister of the spider who divines.

One day, Vincent Mesi tells us, God threw a feast in the sky. The singer, performing before Christians, says it was for his Son's communion. God wrote out his invitations. To Weaver-Spider, sister of Mygale. To Ekul-ngaha, the little yellow ant you find drowned in gourds of palm wine. And it was Monitor Lizard who served as secretary.

But Weaver-Spider stayed at home. So God threw down his string. The Towo-Atangana explain the detail in a note: you often see spiders in houses lowering themselves from the roof on a thread; that is the string God throws them from the sky, or the one they spin out themselves to launch up to him. In the morning, Weaver-Spider unwound her thread. *Kpap kpap kpap nding*. Up she went.

Up there, God welcomed her: come and eat, come and drink, my Son has made his communion. And Weaver-Spider stood rooted to the spot, "her head held high." They called her to the food. She did not move. They called her to the drink. She did not move. So God asked her what she wanted. And the little creature answered, before the master of the sky:

"My dear friend, Lord God, you are not good at all."

God asked why. She demanded a trial. A meeting was set for the next day, at a crossroads of nine paths. Remember that number. The Towo-Atangana note that nine is sacred among the Beti; their traditions say God gave his commandments to the first man in the form of nine bundles.

In the morning, Weaver-Spider was waiting at the crossroads. God arrived at ten o'clock. She scolded him at once: "Do you see what time you arrive? Only at ten o'clock!"

Then she sat down and watched the roads.

The first to pass walked with great difficulty, crushed by an enormous hernia, which the singer swells to the point of farce. The spider called out to him. The man looked at her sideways and replied: "It is not you who did this to me, but the Lord God." She turned to the sky: "There is a charge I write down against God." And to the lizard: "Little Monitor, keep writing."

A cripple went by, dragging himself along. The same answer. A blind man went by. The spider provoked him and he insulted her: "With your little back like a trapper's, your little shoulders like a snuffbox, your little feet like a gravedigger's." Then, like the others: it is not you, it is the Lord God. Men went by carrying a corpse. A man went by who had only one leg. And every time, the same sentence fell like a drumbeat: it is not you who did this to me, it is the Lord God.

And the spider launched her great accusation, the one the singer repeats as the heart of the tale:

"How is it that you create people, and it is you again who kills them?"

Listen closely to that sentence. A small creature, at the crossroads of nine paths, with a lizard for a court clerk, puts the Creator on trial on behalf of the disabled and the dead. The Towo-Atangana report that a friend to whom they showed the manuscript called it "the Eton version of the Book of Job." They add that Nden-Bobo raises the problem of suffering "in a tone as rebellious as that of the righteous man of the Bible."

And God? God does not answer. The singer says it plainly: "Then God fell silent." Then he pronounced the sentence:

"You will be unable to die on the ground, but only hanging in the air."

And Vincent Mesi, the singer, calls on his audience as witnesses: "I have never seen Weaver-Spider lying in a grave, but always hanging in the air!" The Towo-Atangana explain: if a dead spider stays hooked to its web, it is because God denied it burial for its defiance. For a people who dug their dead tombs shaped like caves, where "no clod of earth was to touch the dead", to be denied a grave is a terrible punishment.

The feast never took place, because of the trial. And the singer ends with a twist: the food meant for the spider is the food people eat; the wine meant for her is the wine we drink today. Then he sets down his mvet: "I have told you about Weaver-Spider. I must rest now. I ask only for a glass of wine."

Notice what has happened. The tale is funny: the hernia reaching up to the sky, God running late, the blind man's insults, the refrain about wine bringing "carelessness" and "tall tales." And the tale is grave: it dares to tell God that he is not good. Both at once. This is how peoples say dangerous things: laughing.

### IV. Abobo Ndene and the monitor lizard

Fifty-four years later, on 23 September 2017, in Yaoundé. A woman of fifty-five tells a story in Ewondo. Her name is Elisabeth Belomo. She is a catechist and a leader of Ekang women. Divine Che Neba, of the University of Yaoundé I, takes notes for a large international database of myths, *Our Mythical Childhood*, hosted by the University of Warsaw. The entry, reviewed by other academics from Yaoundé and Australia, summarises the story in English. It is through that summary, and only through it, that I know this version: the Ewondo transcription has not been published.

Listen to what she tells. This is a legend.

There were once two inseparable friends, who shared their joys and their sorrows. One was a spider, Abobo Ndene. The other was a monitor lizard, Otene Nka'a.

Stop on the spider's name. Abobo Ndene. Remember Nden-Bobo, in Tala, in 1963, where *nden* was the web and *bobo* the spider. The same syllables, turned around. The entry does not make the connection, and I am the one making it, but it rings in the ear: it is the same creature, with the same name, passed from mouth to mouth across half a century. And the lizard, who in 1963 held the clerk's pen, is still there in 2017, now the friend who argues back.

One day, coming home from the fields, the two friends quarrelled. About what? About the greatest of questions: where do good and evil come from? The spider said: God is responsible. The lizard said: people are. There was no one to settle it. A month went by with no agreement.

Then the spider had an idea. Let us go and ask those who suffer. She stretched her web across a path, at a crossroads, and they waited for passers-by.

The first was blind. He cursed his condition and said it was Zamba, the Almighty, who knew why he had made him so. The spider was satisfied.

The second was mute. He could say nothing. He pointed a finger at the sky.

The third carried his hernia over his shoulder. The two friends burst out laughing. And the man told them that it was the Lord Ntondobe who had given him this burden, and he asked them: and what is yours?

You recognise the crossroads, the disabled, the hernia, the answer that blames the sky. The pieces of the 1963 tale are all there, arranged differently.

The spider was convinced: God controls everything. But the lizard still doubted. Zamba is just, he said, it is people who do not understand. And since no one down here could decide, he pushed his friend to do the unthinkable: climb to the sky and ask the Creator himself.

The spider set off. The journey lasted ten years.

Ten years to reach the place of God, which the English entry calls paradise. And when she finally arrived, Ntondobe did not open his arms to her. He was annoyed by the intrusion. He judged reckless the presumption of this creature who had come to question him. And he passed his sentence: from now on the spider would live in caves beneath the earth, and she would die hanging from her web.

She came back down disappointed, knowing now that she was mortal. And the lizard's doubt? The entry does not say it was ever resolved. The question stays open. The myth does not give the answer: it hands you the question to keep.

But it gives something else. Listen to the ending, as the entry reports it. Because the spider was the only creature to have met the Almighty, she became associated with the knowledge of good and evil and, in Beti cosmogony, with clairvoyance. She was renamed Ngam. It is believed that Ngam sees events before they happen, and that traditional healers receive their inspiration from her to foretell the future of their patients. In Eton and Ewondo, the entry says, "to foresee the future" is *á bó Ngam*.

There. The circle closes. Why does the mygale know what people do not? Because she went up there. Because she looked God in the face. And why does she live in a hole? Because that is the price of her daring. Her punishment and her knowledge are one and the same. The one who wanted to know too much is condemned to live under the earth, and it is precisely there, under the earth, that people come to ask her what she knows.

### V. The day God went away

A year later, on 22 December 2018, in Soa, on the edge of Yaoundé. Another woman, Véronique Mengue, fifty-eight, a farmer, tells the story in Ewondo in her turn, and Divine Che Neba, with a research assistant, records it. Her version carries a title that is already an explanation: "Why the Spider Roams between the Earth and the Sky." I know it, too, through the English summary in the entry. It is a legend, and it turns everything upside down.

This time it is not the spider who makes the long journey. She goes to find Zobe, the supreme God, and brings him unwelcome news: people hate you, they blame you for their misfortunes. Zobe does not believe her. I created them, he says, I created the world, how could they hate me?

Since you do not believe me, the spider replies, follow me.

And God comes down. The story does not say how. It says only that they went towards the earth together.

At the edge of a swamp, they saw a blind man crossing a river clumsily. His blindness? The will of God, he said. Further on, they saw a disabled woman who moved by dragging herself along on her buttocks and her hands. Her condition? The will of God, she said too.

Picture God, invisible, standing beside the spider, hearing his own creature accuse him without knowing he is there.

The entry says simply that Zobe was uncomfortable. And he did what so many gods do in African myths, gods who are too close and find themselves accused: he moved away. He withdrew from the spider and from people, to live forever in the sky.

And the spider was punished. Zobe condemned her to spin webs without end to reach the sky. Webs that never get there. Look at a spider's thread floating between two branches, rising, snapping, starting again. That, says the tale, is what the spider has done ever since: she tries to reach the God who left because of her.

Now compare the three versions. In Tala in 1963, the spider climbs to the sky on her thread and God condemns her to die hanging in the air. In Yaoundé in 2017, she climbs for ten years, God condemns her to live underground, and she becomes the diviner. In Soa in 2018, it is God who comes down, and God who goes away. Underground or between sky and earth; a God who punishes or a God who withdraws. The endings contradict each other, and that is natural. There is no "true" version of a myth. Each storyteller, each village, each era pulls the thread in its own way. What does not change is the core: a spider, a sky, disabled people who blame God, and a sentence that fixes the spider's life forever.

One more thing: this is not the only myth the Beti tell about God's withdrawal. Other Beti stories explain differently why the Creator moved away from people. Those belong to other evenings around other fires. Here, it is the spider who speaks.

### VI. Breaking the mygale

Now leave the tale behind and go back to the hole at the foot of the house. Because what follows is no longer legend. It is history, attested by written sources.

The act of consulting has a name in Ewondo. Tsala writes it in his dictionary: *boe ngam*, "to question, to consult the oracle." And in another text by Tsala, quoted by the Towo-Atangana in 1966, he gives its literal meaning: *boe ngam* is "to break the mygale." "One breaks the mygale's shell to reach the *nkug* that animates it, and which must answer through it." You recognise the expression the 2017 entry translated as "to foresee the future": *á bó Ngam*. Two translations of the same verb: one names the gesture, breaking the shell; the other names the goal, seeing what is coming. Historians of the twenty-first century take up the same idea: the Senegalese historian Mouhamadou Nissire Sarr, in a 2020 article, writes that among the Beti the mygale "is only the instrument" of a *nkug*, a spirit, and calls the diviner *mbongám*, "oracle-maker."

Tsala also reports a different origin story. Listen to it; it is a tradition he introduces with "It is said":

A man, pursued by his enemies, hid in the hollow of a tree. His *nkug*, his guardian spirit, to better conceal his protégé's hiding place, turned into a spider and quickly spun its web across the mouth of the hollow. The enemies arrived, searched every corner, saw the unbroken web, and went to look further on. Back in his village, the fugitive told his story. Everyone concluded that spiders were inhabited by spirits, the *minkug*. They began to consult the mygale, "considered the queen of spiders." And, Tsala says, "ngam became the generic name for all divinations."

Two origin stories, then. In one, the spider knows because she has seen God. In the other, she knows because a spirit dwells in her. Both say the same thing at heart: the mygale is not a creature like the others. She is a doorway.

The written sources also show what became of the word. A history thesis from the University of Yaoundé I (Ekani Zinga, 2022), drawing on the Cameroonian theologian Jean-Claude Ekobena and on the author's own informants, lists the Beti *ngam*: the *ngam* of the spear, the *ngam* of the horn, the *ngam* of bamboo sticks, of cowries, of the mirror, of eggs, of the fly whisk, of knots, of the machete, of beer, of the kola nut. Every one of these techniques bears the spider's name. It is exactly what Tsala said: *ngam* became the name of all divinations, now told apart "only by qualifiers." As if, in the language itself, every question put to the invisible still passed through the mygale.

The diviner has several names in the sources: *mod ngam* in Tsala; *ngengam*, plural *mingengam*, in a 1936 colonial survey that the administrator Pierre Cournarie carried out with the help of the paramount chief of the Ewondo, Charles Atangana, and of the court assessors; *ngengan ngam*, "technician of divination", in Anselme Nana's classification, taken up in 2022. And Tsala, with rare honesty, left us a proverb that names the limits of it all: "the answers of oracles are like crossbow arrows: some rise, true to the mark, and others spin and fall back to no effect." Even the Ewondo knew the spider could be wrong.

What is this creature? According to Laburthe-Tolra, as cited by the anthropologist David Zeitlyn, the Beti mygale belongs to the genus *Hysterocrates*, a large ground-dwelling tarantula of Central Africa.

And the whole Centre knows her. Among the Bafia of the Mbam, she is *Gam*. In 1935 a young colonial administrator, André Ménard, described their ritual before the Society for Cameroonian Studies in Yaoundé: a burrow where the diviner installs the spider, a circle of palm-leaf ribs, rays drawn out from the hole, each one standing for a question, incised leaves bearing symbols that the spider moves during the night, a leaf blackened on one side to mean misfortune. He also noted the spider's name "in Yaoundé", *Ngam*, and stated that throughout Cameroon's forest zone, and along the strip of gallery forest running from Batouri to Dschang by way of Nanga-Eboko and Bafia, the spider had always been held sacred, "in constant contact with the spirits of the bush." Further north, among the Sanaga people of the Ngoro area, a University of Yaoundé I student, Albertine Mbena Mbarga, gathered, in interviews conducted notably in 2021, the testimony of a society where "some people will undertake nothing without first seeking the advice of diviners", above all those who consult "the gama spider." The Cameroonian anthropologist Mbonji Edjenguèlè, quoted in the same 2022 thesis, widens the circle: *Ngame* among the Bamum, *Ngam* among the Tikar, the Bamileke, the Beti-Bulu, and among the Duala the mygale lives "in a hole set apart from the community, at the edge of the compound."

Further still, outside the Centre, in the North-West, the Yamba have their own myth, and I give it to you for comparison, not to be confused with ours. A Yamba diviner, Pa Monday Kongnjo, told it around 1989 to the anthropologist Hermann Gufler: long ago, the spider spoke to people in their own language; one day it guided a young hunter down beneath the earth, to beings the diviner called gods; the old man of that underworld scolded it for revealing what it should have kept hidden, threw earth on its back, and took away its speech: from then on it could show things only "in a hidden way." See the resemblance to the 2017 version: a spider that goes too far towards the world of the gods, a sentence that pins it to the earth, and from that sentence divination is born. Two distant peoples, two languages, one shared intuition.

And near the Nigerian border, among the Mambila of Somié, David Zeitlyn, initiated as a diviner in 1986, has watched the *ŋgam dù*, the "divination of the earth", for forty years. In a 2025 essay he writes that the village centre had five regular diviners in the mid-1980s, three around 2015, two in 2025. But clients still come from far away, and now they phone.

### VII. What the spider says

So what does this myth do for the people who tell it?

First, it explains. It says why the mygale lives underground, why a dead spider hangs from its thread with no grave, why it spins towards the sky without ever reaching it. Scholars call this an aetiology: a story that explains something we can see.

Next, it founds. It gives the most important divination of the Ewondo country its patent of nobility. If people question the mygale, it is not out of blind superstition: it is because, in the story, she is the only creature who ever stood before God. The myth gives the diviner legitimacy, as other myths do for a chief or a clan.

But above all, it thinks. And that is where this small tale becomes great. For the question the spider asks, "if God is the support of people, why do people suffer?", is the question philosophers call the problem of evil. The Beti did not leave it to theologians. They put it in the mouth of a creature at the crossroads of nine paths, and made every misfortune in the world file past her: the hernia, the blindness, the severed leg, the corpse carried by.

And notice how the myth answers. It does not. In all three versions, God never explains himself. He falls silent, he punishes, or he leaves. The Towo-Atangana saw it clearly: the spider is punished not because she is wrong, but for her insolence, her overreaching, what the Greeks called *hubris*. And yet the tale proves her right on one point: she, the insolent one, becomes the keeper of knowledge. The myth says at once: you do not question God with impunity, and: whoever dares to question becomes the one others come to consult.

There is another, quieter lesson. The 2017 entry points it out: so many of the world's mythologies lay the origin of evil at a woman's door. Not here. The question of evil is raised by two animals debating, with witnesses, as in a courtroom. And two of the three storytellers we know of are women, a catechist and a farmer, who carry this story into the twenty-first century.

Should we see this tale as a Christian invention? The question is fair, and it must be asked. Vincent Mesi sang in a presbytery, before catechists, and spoke of Jesus's communion. Elisabeth Belomo is a catechist. In 1966 the Towo-Atangana gave their verdict: the tale's first author "was certainly Christian, but just as surely knew nothing of the story of Job." The setting of the feast is no doubt recent. But the core, the spider who climbs on her thread, accuses God and is left hanging, appears with three storytellers across more than half a century, and joins the spider divination that Tsala describes as a practice of "times past." No one can date this myth. What can be said is that it managed to welcome Christianity without losing its question.

As for the divination, it has weathered storms. It was often colonial administrators who described it, and not out of mere curiosity: Ménard was gathering notes for a customary code meant for the presiding judges of courts; Cournarie conducted his survey with court assessors. In those same years, the German administration had abolished the great Beti initiation rite, the *so*, in 1910 according to Tsala. The missions fought the diviners. The notes attached to Ménard's talk, published much later by his descendant, sum up the causes of decline: a lack of initiates, new ways of living, colonial justice, the Christian religion. Tsala himself, a Catholic priest, speaks of "so-called means of questioning the future." And yet the word survived. In 2022 a Yaoundé historian interviewed initiates from Nkol-Lekok, Etock and Konabeng, and *ngam* is still there, as the diagnosis that comes before fertility rites. Consulting the spider itself has become rare and discreet: I found no recent study describing a Beti session, and that must be said.

The objects have travelled too. The *avol* sticks, the incised leaves, the diviners' gourds were fragile, plant-made things, handed down from master to successor. Among the Bafia, Ménard had gathered a collection of divination leaves which his family announced it would hand over to the Musée du quai Branly in Paris at the end of 2013. Thousands of objects from Cameroon now sit in European museums, and since 2024 the Museum Fünf Kontinente in Munich, working with the National Museum of Cameroon, has been investigating the provenance of objects from the Fang-Beti region taken in colonial times. At the quai Branly, a drinking vessel from the Bamum kingdom bears spirals that the museum's notice reads as a "stylised evocation of the spider's web", recalling that spider divination is "a major practice in Cameroon." Nden-Bobo's web has reached as far as Paris.

So you who are listening, here is what I leave you.

The next time you see a spider lowering itself from the roof on its thread, think of the string God threw to Nden-Bobo. The next time you see a web stretched between two branches, think of the one who has been trying, ever since the day God went away, to reach the sky. And the next time someone tells you that you must not ask questions, remember that your ancestors entrusted the most dangerous of questions to the smallest of creatures, and made her their diviner.

For the ancient Beti did not bequeath you an answer. They bequeathed you the right to ask.

- **Timeline**:
  - 1910: the German administration abolishes the *so* initiation rite (according to Tsala 1955)
  - 1935-1936: first written descriptions of spider divination in the Centre (Ménard among the Bafia, talk of 13 December 1935; Cournarie for the Yaoundé district, 1936)
  - 1955: Father Théodore Tsala's Ewondo-French dictionary describes *ngam* (spider, divination, oracle) and its *avol* sticks
  - 23 January 1963: Vincent Mesi, mvet player, sings the trial of Nden-Bobo in Tala (published by G. and F. Towo-Atangana in 1966)
  - 23 September 2017: Elisabeth Belomo tells Abobo Ndene and Otene Nka'a in Yaoundé (OMC database, item 156)
  - 22 December 2018: Véronique Mengue tells "Why the Spider Roams between the Earth and the Sky" in Soa (OMC database, item 1286)
- **Notable quotations** (all attested; English renderings of French sources):
  - "In times past, the Ewondo undertook nothing serious without first consulting the oracle." (Th. Tsala, *Dictionnaire ewondo-français*, 1955, p. 415)
  - "The answers of oracles are like crossbow arrows: some rise, true to the mark, and others spin and fall back to no effect." (Ewondo proverb, Tsala's translation, 1955, p. 415)
  - "My dear friend, Lord God, / you are not good at all." (Vincent Mesi, 1963, Towo-Atangana translation, 1966)
  - "How is it that you create people, and it is you again who kills them?" (same)
  - "You will be unable to die on the ground, / but only hanging in the air." (same)
  - "The expression the Ewondo use for 'to consult an oracle' is indeed 'boe ngam', 'to break the mygale'." (Tsala, quoted by Towo-Atangana 1966)

- **Debates and uncertainties**:
  - The 2017 and 2018 versions are known only from English summaries; the Ewondo texts are unpublished.
  - The endings contradict each other (underground, hanging in the air, spinning towards the sky): these are variants, with no canonical version.
  - The Christian share: the setting (communion, paradise) is recent; the core is judged "authentically Beti" by the Towo-Atangana (1966). The myth cannot be dated.
  - The link between Nden-Bobo (1963) and Abobo Ndene (2017) is the griot's own inference, based on the Eton etymology given by the Towo-Atangana.
  - The meaning of Ntondobe: "support of people" according to Tsala (adopted here); "he who brings Man out" according to French Wikipedia, unsourced.
  - The name "Zobe" comes only from OMC entry 1286.
  - Is spider consultation still practised among the Beti? The word *ngam* is alive among traditional healers (Ekani Zinga 2022), but no recent study of a Beti session was found.
  - Origin of the divination: a relatively recent innovation according to Vansina (cited by Zeitlyn), shared across southern Cameroon; no date can be given.
  - Sources to consult in a library: P. Gebauer, *Spider Divination in the Cameroons* (Milwaukee Public Museum, 1964); Laburthe-Tolra 1985 in full; Cournarie 1936, pp. 36-39; L. Mallart-Guimerà, *Ni dos ni ventre* (1981); R. Leiderer 1982 (Bafia).
- **Associated legends** (oral tradition, not history):
  - Nden-Bobo's trial of God at the crossroads of nine paths (Tala, 1963)
  - Abobo Ndene's ten-year journey and transformation into Ngam the diviner (Yaoundé, 2017)
  - Zobe's withdrawal to the sky and the spider spinning without end (Soa, 2018)
  - The fugitive hidden in a tree, saved by his spirit turned spider (reported by Tsala)
  - Comparison outside the region: the Yamba myth of the spider deprived of speech (Pa Monday Kongnjo, via Gufler 1995)
