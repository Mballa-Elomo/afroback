# The Miengu, People of the Sawa Waters

Belief, myths and institutions of the Sawa peoples of the Cameroonian coast (Duala, Bakweri, Malimba, Isubu, Bakoko, Oroko, Batanga and their neighbours), the Wouri estuary and Mount Cameroon. Version 2, rewritten in depth on 2026-10-15 (the earlier version is archived in `archives/fr_v1.md`, the research file in `recherche.md`).

> This story speaks of a living belief. The griot tells it as a belief, and at every step he says what historians and anthropologists know, what they argue about, and what no one can settle. What is attested is not the existence of the miengu: it is the faith, the rites, the institutions and the memory of those who believe in them.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. The water is never empty

Listen.

There is a place on the coast of Cameroon where river and sea mingle for so long that no one can quite say where one ends and the other begins. The Wouri comes down from the interior, heavy and brown, laden with earth and leaves. It widens, it splits into creeks, it slides between the mangroves whose roots rise out of the mud like fingers. And then it meets the Atlantic. There, on the high ground of its banks, a city was built that bears the name of its first masters: Douala.

On a clear day, look west. You will see an immense mountain rising, a volcano that sometimes smokes, which the people here call Fako. It is Mount Cameroon. Its slopes run all the way down to the sea. On its flanks live the Bakweri. Along the rivers and creeks, between the mountain and the estuary, live their cousins: the Duala, the Isubu of Bimbia, the Malimba, the Bakoko, the Pongo, the Bodiman, the Oroko, and further south, towards Kribi, the Batanga. Today all of them are gathered under one name: the Sawa, the people of the coast.

Now come closer to the water's edge. Look at it carefully.

You see a current and a fisherman casting his net. But ask a grandmother in Deido, an old fisherman in Bonaberi, a Bakweri woman from the mountain villages, and they will tell you gently, without raising their voices: the water is never empty.

Beneath the surface, they say, there is a people.

The Duala call it *jengu*, and in the plural *miengu*. The Bakweri say *liengu*, plural *maengu*. Among the Bakoko you also hear the word *bisima*. The names change from one bank to the next, just as the accents do, but the belief runs the whole length of the coast. The miengu are water spirits. They live in the rivers and in the sea. They are said to be beautiful, half human, and when people try to describe them to a child, they usually end up saying: like mermaids.

But be careful. A griot who simply told you "the miengu are African mermaids" would be lying out of laziness. The miengu are not fairy-tale creatures to be admired from afar. For centuries they stood at the heart of these peoples' lives: fishing, canoe races, the healing of the sick, the initiation of girls, the balance of power between the great families. Even today, every year in Douala, a man goes down into the Wouri to carry the word of a whole people to them and come back with their answer. And in 2024 the whole world placed that rite on the list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity.

So I am not going to tell you a story about mermaids. I am going to tell you about a people of the coast, what they believed, what they made of that belief, and what it helped them live through. To understand the spirits of the water, you must first understand the people of the water.

### II. The people of the estuary

Who are they, these Sawa?

Their traditions say they descend from a single ancestor, Mbedi, son of Mbongo, who lived at Piti, a place inland, upriver. From Mbedi came the founders of the different coastal peoples. One of his sons, Ewale a Mbedi, is said to have left Piti and travelled down the river to the site of present-day Douala, which was then held by the Basaa or the Bakoko. Ewale is the ancestor whose name the Duala carry.

Listen to what two historians who spent years on Duala history, Ralph Austen and Jonathan Derrick, say about these accounts in a book published by Cambridge University Press in 1999. They compared the versions collected by Europeans between 1863 and 1905. The details vary: some say the ancestors left Piti because of a quarrel over a chicken or a canoe prow, others because of a war, others over a marriage. One version even says the ancestors "learned to fish" when they reached the estuary; another that they traded fish with the Bakoko. And the two historians make a remark that struck me: in these traditions there are almost no heroes. Neither Mbedi nor Ewale performs wonders. No giant, no miracle. The Duala origin traditions read more like a family chronicle than an epic.

Remember this, because it matters for what follows. Among the people of the estuary, the marvellous did not settle in the ancestors. It settled in the water.

How did they live? First, from the river. The anthropologist Georges Balandier, who studied early Duala society, notes that fishing was "considered a noble activity". Then, when European ships began to anchor in the estuary, the Duala became something else: middlemen. Austen and Derrick called one chapter of their book "From fishermen to middlemen". The Duala paddled up the rivers, the Wouri, the Abo, the Dibamba, the Mungo, to fetch the produce of the interior, and traded it for goods from Europe.

And I must tell you the whole truth, even when it hurts. For a long time, the main merchandise of that trade was human beings. Balandier writes it plainly: the Duala became the favoured intermediaries of a trade "dealing mainly in slaves". After the Atlantic slave trade ended, in the nineteenth century, commerce shifted to palm oil, palm kernels and ivory. But the Duala plantations around the town were still worked by captives. A griot who hid this to make the story prettier would be no griot. The people of the estuary were great, skilful and powerful, and they also took part in one of the greatest violences in history. Both things are true.

Around 1850, when Europeans begin describing Douala in detail, the town is not a unified kingdom. It is made of several "towns" perched on the high banks of the Wouri, dominated by two great lineages: Bell, at Bonanjo, and Akwa, at Bonambela. The Europeans call their heads "kings". Beside them stand other chiefs, such as those of Deido or Bonaberi. And the European observers noticed something curious, which Austen and Derrick report: they saw no formal political structure uniting all the Duala, but "ritual societies" exercised influence across lineages and settlements.

There they are, the invisible threads holding together a people without a single king. Balandier described them as a second system alongside kinship: ritual associations, the two main ones being, he says, the *ngondo* and the *jengu*. Their role, in his view, was to maintain the shared ideology and the cohesion of the whole Duala group. The Cameroonian historian Victor Julius Ngoh also lists, among the secret societies of the coast, the Ekongolo, the Losango and the Munji.

The Ngondo was the assembly of chiefs and notables. Sawa tradition, recorded in 1972 by Maurice Doumbé-Moulongo, says it already existed before the first missionaries arrived, that it was founded around 1830, and that its "father" is considered to be King Ngando a Kwa of the Akwa, who died in 1845. The Jengu was the society of the water spirits.

Do you begin to see? Among the Sawa, belief in the miengu was not a private matter of superstition. It was an institution. One of the pillars holding up the common house.

### III. The kingdom beneath the waters

So what are they, these miengu? Listen to what believers say, and always keep in mind that these are the words of believers, which I report faithfully, without making them my own and without mocking them.

They are said to live at the bottom of the rivers and the sea, in a world that resembles ours. They are said to be intermediaries between the living and the world of the ancestors. They are said to bring luck, rich catches and health, and also to punish. And they are described in many ways. A Bakweri journalist, Wose Yangange Martin, reports that the elders portray Liengu la Mwanja, the great lady of the sea, as a beautiful woman with an oval face, an enchanting smile, a gap between her front teeth, the "lucky gap" the coast holds to be a sign of beauty, and abundant hair. But in 2017, in Yaoundé, a Batanga storyteller from Kribi, Désiré Guifo, a teacher of African languages and cultures, told a researcher from the University of Yaoundé I, Daniel Nkemleke, that the jengu appears sometimes as a mermaid and sometimes as a "beautiful dark-skinned girl".

Even the face of the miengu has more than one form. I will tell you later why some portraits, with very light skin, look strangely like an image that came from Germany.

What is certain is that the miengu were bound up with everything coastal life depended on. Historians who summarised the early observations report that the jengu cult originally honoured the water spirits as the source of four blessings: crayfish and shrimp, the end of the rainy season in one of the wettest regions on earth, victory in the canoe races, and protection from epidemics. Food, weather, honour, health: everything that keeps a fishing people standing.

The canoe races, then. You must see them with the eyes of old. Canoes twenty to twenty-eight metres long, without a keel, hollowed from a single trunk, driven by forty or fifty paddlers striking the water together as they sing. At the prow, a carved and painted piece, the *tangué*, bearing the emblems of the house. Every great family had its canoe, its flag, its champions. To win was the honour of the lineage. The historian Jonathan Derrick, who studied Douala in the 1930s, reports two precious things: in earlier times, diviners read the future in the results of the races; and until the late 1930s, a family on Jebale Island, in the middle of the Wouri, claimed it could call the miengu to help the crews it favoured. Fifty men paddling, and, it was said, invisible hands pushing beneath the hull.

And fishing? Listen again to Désiré Guifo, the Batanga storyteller, who says he himself carries the jengu spirit. He tells of a woman whose husband was taken by the jengu into the sea, with her consent, for about two weeks. The man came back with plenty of fish and the promise of a good harvest. He says that twice a year initiates carry gifts by canoe to the middle of the sea, that an invisible hand receives them, and that the empty containers come back later. He says a jengu sometimes sends a letter that floats on the water without getting wet, which only an initiate can read. He also says that some children are born with the spirit and keep it even when they move to the city. These are the words of a believer, published by academics.

But the jengu is not only a benefactor. Daniel Nkemleke stresses that the water spirits correct social disorder, especially that of irresponsible men. The jengu, Guifo says, is a guardian of morals.

How do you speak to a people who live under water? You need a language. Among the Duala, the ethnologist Manga Bekombo, himself a son of this culture, explains that healers, the *nganga*, the "people of the remedy", use special languages depending on whom they address: the language of the spirits of the dead, the language of divination, the language of the oracle, and what he calls "the language of the Jingú", after the spirits who dwell in the water. He also describes the world in which these healers move: the *ndimsi*, that "other dimension of reality" beyond the reach of the senses and of common knowledge, the realm of the strange, of illness and of death, but also of those who know how to act within it to heal. The nganga, he says, gather in brotherhoods, the *losango*.

And how was the jengu society led? The summaries available to us speak of a master called the *ekale*, who traditionally wore a mask at every meeting, a practice that had almost died out by the mid-twentieth century. The earliest rites, it is said, took place in canoes on the Wouri: the officiant, dressed in raffia, offered food and drink to the spirits. These details come from modern summaries whose older sources I have not been able to read: I give them to you with caution.

Finally, there is healing. This may be where the miengu mattered most in people's lives. A German missionary, Carl Bender, who lived among the Duala and Bakweri in the early twentieth century, wrote that the water spirits were held in high esteem and credited with superhuman powers, and that "when the witch doctor and his means fail, the Liengu is called." When everything else has failed, people turn to the water. The Jesuit Éric de Rosny, who lived in Douala and was initiated by a nganga, devoted an entire text in 1973 to a young woman healed by this path: he called it "Claire's jengu".

And it is through illness, precisely, that the most extraordinary story begins. The story of the Bakweri girls who go into the water.

### IV. The girls who go into the water

Climb now onto the slopes of Fako, among the Bakweri.

In the mid-twentieth century an English anthropologist, Edwin Ardener, came to live among them. He learned their language, studied their history, and wrote pages about them that are still quoted all over the world. Fifty years before him, the missionary Carl Bender had already described the same rites. And what both of them describe is not a folk tale. It is an institution of women.

Ardener says that among the Bakweri, liengu is not a single rite but "a patchwork of several women's rites all of which are linked by the name LIENGU". And all of them begin the same way: with a crisis. A fit, a fall, a kind of trance that strikes mainly adolescent girls, but older women too.

Listen to the first, the one most bound to the water. It is called *liengu la ndiva*. *Ndiva* means "deep water".

A girl is sitting by the fire. Suddenly the sickness seizes her. She falls, and often, as she falls, she knocks over one of the three hearthstones that hold up the cooking pot. The women come running. One of them, who knows this liengu, bends over her and speaks to her, not in ordinary Bakweri, but in a secret tongue, the liengu language. Everyone watches. If the girl shows that she understands, there is no more doubt: the water has called her. A liengu doctor is sent for, who may be a man or a woman.

Then the seclusion begins. The girl lives apart, bound by taboos. An initiated woman becomes her sponsor. She teaches her the liengu language. She gives her a liengu name, a secret name. Bender tells us that during this time the girl sometimes sees spirits, and that at the sight of them she cries out "Iso, iso!", "We, we!", and the spirits vanish.

After several months the doctor returns. Men chosen for their strength lift the girl and carry her in turn to the stream, to the place where the water grows deep. The women sing the liengu songs. And there, the doctor pushes her into the water.

And while she is in the water, the whole company tries to catch a crab. A crab, yes. Because the crab, Ardener says, represents the water spirit.

When she steps out of the stream, Bender writes, the girl cries "Ena Linua o!", a short form of two beloved women's names, Enanga and Limunge. And the crowd answers in chorus: "Amba Ena Linua!", "Truly, Enanga Limunge!". From now on she is a familiar of the water spirits, one of the "liengu women". After another seclusion there is a feast, and from then on the men regard her as safe from any attack by the water spirits.

The second rite is called *liengu la mongbango*. Here the sign is not always a fall: sometimes the girl disappears into the bush, "as if attracted by spirits", Ardener writes. Her female relatives go looking for her, singing in the liengu language. Bender adds that they carry the liengu insignia, the *njola*, a piece of wickerwork, and that they call out "Mandone so njo mbembi njilo e?", "Who is it that hides in the bush?". When she is found, she is brought back to the seclusion house.

This rite lasts about a year. There is a feast eaten on the bare ground. Nine months later a sheep is killed, another feast is held, and the girl withdraws into the bush with her sponsor. Then comes the great day. She is dressed in fern fronds, *senge* or *njombi*, rubbed with red camwood powder, used to anoint bodies throughout the region. She is tied to the middle of a long rope. Women hold the rope in front, others behind, and so they cross the village. Outside her house the two groups pull, as in a tug of war, until the rope breaks. Then the girl falls. As if dead.

Silence.

They call her. Once. Twice. Nine times, in the liengu language. And at the call, she rises. She is dressed in new clothes. A few weeks later the doctor washes her in a stream, to show that she is free of the taboos she has kept.

Do you understand what you have just seen? A death and a rebirth. The girl who falls beneath the broken rope is not the same as the one who rises at the ninth voice.

The third rite, *liengu la vefea*, is shorter: a goat and a young cock are sacrificed, the sick woman drinks a medicine that makes her vomit, the same in every rite, and she keeps food taboos. According to Bender, this liengu protects against diseases of the head, the lungs and the internal organs. When a woman is gravely ill and hope is fading, her relatives dedicate her to liengu la vefea. At the start of the rite she lifts a low stool above her head, crying "Iso nge, bane fe na nja e?", "We are it, who are the others?".

And among the upper Bakweri, those who live furthest from the sea, Ardener reports an even simpler liengu: it was said that every daughter went through liengu at about eight to ten years old, "so that she be fertile". She wore the fern dress and lived apart for a while. Here liengu is no longer only healing. It is the passage from childhood to womanhood.

An institution where women held together. Keep those words. Because to understand why, among the Bakweri, it is mostly women who go into the water, you must listen to a myth. The myth of fire.

### V. The fire of Moto

Ardener published this myth as a Bakweri scholar, Charles Steane, had given it in 1929 to a British official, B. G. Stone. Ardener notes that it is a "male" version. Listen to it.

There were three beings who were always quarrelling: Moto, Ewaki and Mojili. One day they agreed to settle the matter by a test, to decide which of them would stay in the town and which would go to the bush. Each would light a fire in his house in the morning. In the evening, on returning from the farms, the one whose fire was still burning would be the favoured one.

Moto was more cunning than the others. He took big logs and arranged them with care. The others made do with small dry sticks, which blaze quickly and die quickly.

When evening came, they returned. At Ewaki's, cold ashes. At Mojili's, cold ashes. At Moto's, the fire was still burning.

So Moto stayed in the town, and he became Man. Ewaki went into the bush and became the Ape, and Eto, whom the story names at this point, became the Mouse. And Mojili was driven into the water, and became a water spirit.

That is the myth. It fits in a few sentences. But a griot does not stop at the sentences. He listens to what lies beneath them.

First, the words. *Moto*, Ardener explains, is the ordinary Bakweri word for "human being", man or woman. *Eto* is the rat, or mouse. *Ewaki*, the ape. And Mojili, also called Mojele, is a spirit to the coastal Bakweri, while inland the name is used to speak of the ape without naming it. Ardener thinks the word comes from the fishing peoples and probably meant, originally, the manatee, that large, peaceful mammal of the estuaries that so many sailors have taken, from afar, for a mermaid.

Next, the consequences. According to the belief Ardener records, Mojili is responsible for young girls becoming liengu, mermaids, who are dangerous to men. And the husbands of these mermaids are the rats. Mojili's name is so powerful that it must not be spoken in front of children under seven, lest they fall sick and die. The same goes for the name of the ape, for the apes of the forest are said to draw small children towards them. "Rites exist to control these manifestations," Ardener writes. You have just seen those rites: they are the liengu rites.

And now listen to the reading Ardener drew from it, which travelled around the world. The myth of Moto, he says, draws a boundary. On one side, Moto's village, the human world, ordered, the one men control. On the other, the bush and the water, the wild, where the ape, the rat and the water spirit live. For Bakweri men, women pose a problem: they belong to the village, but through their power to give life they touch that wild world men do not master. And the women themselves? Hear his words: "Bakweri women themselves bound their world as including the wild that Moto excluded. They go through rites by which they become LIENGU mermaid spirits, or spirits of the forest, generally in adolescence, and retain this feature of womanhood throughout their lives."

Do you understand what that means? What the men tell as a danger, the women inhabit as part of themselves. For them the world of water is not an outside threat. It is their territory.

Ardener points out a wonderful detail: in the myth, the three banished brothers also knew how to make fire. They were not beasts. They simply lost the test. "Although the men bound off 'mankind' from nature," he writes, "the women persist in overlapping into nature again." From this he drew a conclusion that changed the way anthropology is done: when researchers listen only to men, they hear only half the world. Women are not mute; it is the others who are deaf. From this Bakweri fieldwork came what is known as "muted group" theory.

And this division can be read even in kinship. Among the Bakweri, Ardener says, there is double descent. The father's lineage governs residence in the village, land, cattle and political office. The mother's lineage governs fertility, and its symbolic fertility bangle is kept on a woman's farm, outside the village fence. Outside, again. On the side of the wild, of bush and water.

His wife, the anthropologist Shirley Ardener, showed another face of this strength. Among the Bakweri, an insult to a woman's body, *titi ikoli*, brought out all the women of the village: with leaves tied around their waists, they surrounded the offender and demanded redress. In the 1956 customary court records she studied, there even appears, in the course of one case, a woman named Liengu: Liengu Ikome. The name of the water spirit, carried in daily life as a woman's name.

Listen finally to the great version, the one that makes the water and the mountain a couple. A Bakweri tradition, reported in the country's press, says that the master of the mountain, Efasa-Moto, half man and half stone, is the husband of Liengu la Mwanja, the great lady of the sea. By agreement, he chose the mountain, and she stayed in the ocean. He watches over Fako; she reigns over the waters. And when, during an eruption, a lava flow came down the mountain towards the sea, some said that the mountain god was going to join his wife.

The mountain and the sea. Man and woman. The village and the wild. The fire that does not go out, and the water that will not be enclosed. Among the Bakweri, the myth of the miengu is a way of thinking about the world in pairs, and of saying that neither half can do without the other.

### VI. The cross, the Kaiser and the mermaid

And then came those who wanted to empty the water of its inhabitants.

In 1843, Baptist missionaries from Britain and the Caribbean settled at Bimbia, among the Isubu. In 1845, Alfred Saker opened a mission in Douala. They wrote down the Duala language and translated the Bible. To them, the water-spirit societies, the sacrifices on the river, the women speaking the secret language of the mermaids, all belonged to a paganism to be fought. The summaries available to us say that the early missionaries largely failed to suppress the jengu cult, which was already well established by the early nineteenth century.

In 1884 the Duala kings signed a treaty with Germany. Cameroon became a German protectorate. Two years later the German authorities expelled the British Baptists and handed their churches over to the Basel Mission. The Duala faithful refused to be dispossessed, and in 1888 they founded their own church, the Native Baptist Church, one of the first independent African churches in the country. The Duala accepted Christianity, but refused to let anyone take the mastery of their house.

The Basel missionaries, for their part, watched and wrote: in 1904 one of them, Dinkelacker, published an article on "the losango, or secret societies of the Duala".

On the mountain, the Bakweri resisted in another way. In 1891 a German expedition led by Karl von Gravenreuth marched on Buea. It was driven back, and Gravenreuth was killed. Only in 1894 did the Germans take Buea and raze it. Chief Kuva Likenye would die in flight or in exile; the sources disagree. And Bakweri popular memory, reported in the press, adds its own reading: some elders say that Efasa-Moto, husband of the great lady of the sea, had helped his people win in 1891, and that the Bakweri lost afterwards because they betrayed his trust. This is not the history of the archives: it is a defeated people looking to its spirits for the meaning of its defeat.

And then colonisation settled into the calendar itself. The great canoe races, where the miengu were said to push the hulls, were now held, according to Jonathan Derrick, on 27 January, the Kaiser's birthday, and later, under the French, on 14 July and 11 November. The diviners who read the future in the races were replaced by a presiding Christian priest. By the 1930s most Duala were Christian.

So did the miengu disappear? No. They changed place. They passed into the shadows, into the night, into what the Duala call the *ndimsi*. The Jesuit Éric de Rosny, who came to Cameroon as a teacher, settled in a working-class neighbourhood of Douala and discovered, beside the world of day where he taught, a world of night where the nganga worked. The Church forbade its faithful to frequent them, "even though many nganga are Christians", note those who published his work. Rosny got his friend the healer Din out of prison, and Din initiated him before he died. He told the story of this initiation in *Les yeux de ma chèvre* (*The Eyes of My Goat*), in 1981: the title comes from the goat Din asked him to sacrifice. Years later the patriarchs of the Ngondo, the *Beyoum ba bato*, the "stump-men", admitted him into their brotherhood. Until his death he bore the Duala name Dibounjé.

But another figure also arrived by water, at the turn of the twentieth century. Her name is Mami Wata.

Listen closely, because the two are often confused. The art historian Henry Drewal, who has devoted decades to Mami Wata, explains that her name comes from pidgin, "Mother Water", a language born for trade. Mami Wata is a figure of money, exchange, seduction, cities and ports, honoured from West Africa to the Americas. And her most famous image, the light-skinned woman with long black hair and snakes coiled around her neck, has a precise origin: a circus poster printed in Hamburg in the 1880s for the animal showman Carl Hagenbeck. It depicted a snake charmer who performed under the name Maladamatjaute. The poster, Drewal says, reached West Africa soon after, probably carried by African sailors who had passed through Hamburg, and by around 1901 it was already being copied in sculpture.

Hamburg, the port of the German merchant ships. I cannot claim that the very light-skinned portraits of the miengu come from that poster. But when I see how much they resemble it, and how different they are from the "dark-skinned girl" of the Batanga storyteller, I ask myself the question. It is a griot's hypothesis, not a historian's certainty.

What is certain is that Drewal himself draws the distinction. He writes that beside Mami Wata there exists a vast, uncountable "school" of African water spirits, female and male, with their own local names and personalities, whose beliefs and practices may or may not be shared with those of Mami Wata. The miengu belong to that school. They have Sawa names, a secret language, societies, rites of passage, a place in the assembly of chiefs. The researcher Daniel Nkemleke, of the University of Yaoundé I, notes that the two beliefs partly overlap in Cameroon, but that Mami Wata seems more "hospitable" than the jengu. The jengu belongs here. It cannot be bought.

Today a new wave is blowing. Drewal observes that Pentecostal movements and other preachers turn Mami Wata into a figure of the devil, and the suspicion often spreads to all water spirits. If you have heard this, I do not ask you to choose a side. I only ask you to know where each word comes from. The word that demonises is young. The word that respected the water is old.

### VII. The diver of the Wouri

November 1949. The first United Nations visiting mission crosses French-administered Cameroon and leaves through Douala with nearly a hundred petitions. One of them is signed by the Ngondo. That year the Duala chiefs, led by Betote Akwa, revive their old assembly and celebrate it in public. In 1951 Betote Akwa even asks the French administration to make the date a public holiday. Refused.

The historian Ralph Austen studied this revival. For most historians of Cameroon, he says, it was an act of self-assertion against the colonial power. For him, it is also an almost perfect case of what is called "the invention of tradition": what the Duala celebrated, he writes, was not so much an institution of the past as the very act of producing a memory of that past. And with Jonathan Derrick he adds that if the modern Ngondo placed the jengu cult at its centre, it was because it reinterpreted what had made the Duala great: navigation, the river, the hierarchies born of trade. Do not take this as an insult. Every people invents its festivals from the materials of its past. The real question is what they keep alive.

For the Ngondo disturbed people. In December 1981, according to Austen, the governor of the Littoral province banned the annual festival; the regime regarded it as an unrecognised tribal association. Other sources date the ban as early as 1972. The silence lasted until 1991. That year the Ngondo was reborn, and the assembly changed its name: the *Ngond'a Duala* became the *Ngond'a Sawa*. All the peoples of the coast now have their place in it.

And here is the heart of the rite, as described in the file submitted to UNESCO. Every year, from September to the first Sunday of December, come the caravan, traditional wrestling, the fair, the beauty contest, the canoe race. On the eve, the summaries say, the jengu initiates hold a private ceremony on Jebale Island. A priestess, the *Mangon*, prepares the sacred vessel and the ritual dishes. Then, on the first Sunday of December, the crowd gathers on the banks of the Wouri, at Deido. A sacred canoe pulls away. A priest dives with a vessel holding the wishes and grievances of the whole community.

And time stretches.

Belief says he can stay down a very long time, for hours, and come out with his clothes dry. The press, for its part, has timed it: in 2019, Ndjangoua, head of the Ngondo heritage commission, stayed under water for eight minutes. Eight minutes in which thousands of people hold their breath with him. When he surfaces, he carries a message from the spirits. It is interpreted in a sacred hut, passed to the chiefs, then announced to all. That year the message asked parents and grandparents to guide the young better. It was raining, and people said the rain was joining the river as a blessing.

On 4 December 2024, in Asunción, Paraguay, UNESCO inscribed "Ngondo, worship of water oracles and associated cultural traditions among the Sawa" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The file says the Sawa see water as a nurturing mother and the home of their divinities. It also plans to replant mangroves and fight the silting of the river. From water spirits to ecology, the path is short.

### VIII. What the waters say

So what do the miengu tell you?

They tell you first that a people without a single king can stand tall. The Sawa had no common throne, but they had the water, and societies that bound the lineages together above their rivalries.

They tell you that nature is not an empty backdrop. The elders did not ask permission to exploit the river. They asked permission to live with it. The water was honoured before the net was cast, before the race, before the journey.

They tell you that women have always had their own language. While the men told of Moto's fire, Bakweri girls learned the secret language of the water, fell beneath the broken rope and rose again at the ninth voice.

They also tell you to look your history in the face. The people of the estuary were noble fishermen and skilful traders, and they took part in the trade in human beings. Belief in the miengu survived the missions, the Kaiser, the bans. No one is obliged to believe in it. But everyone can understand it.

Perhaps you live in Douala, in Buea, in Paris or in Houston, and you have never seen the diver of the Wouri. I, a griot, ask you neither to believe nor to fear. I ask you to remember that your ancestors looked at this river and saw a voice in it.

One day, go to the riverbank on the first Sunday of December. Watch the canoe pull away. Count the minutes with the crowd. And when the man comes up again, listen to the message he brings.

The water is never empty.

This is where you come from.

## Structured profile

### The Miengu (jengu, liengu, maengu), water spirits of the Sawa

- **Type of content**: belief and a body of oral-tradition myths, still practised (not a historical fact). Historical: the ritual societies (jengu, liengu), the rites described by ethnographers, the Ngondo and its history.
- **Peoples concerned**: Sawa of the Cameroonian coast: Duala, Bakweri, Isubu, Malimba, Bakoko, Pongo, Bodiman, Oroko, Batanga and neighbours.
- **Era**: precolonial (cult described as established by the early nineteenth century), colonial, independence, contemporary.
- **Region**: Central Africa (Wouri estuary, Mount Cameroon, Kribi coast).
- **Theme**: other (nature spirits, female initiation, healing, political and ritual institutions, heritage).

**Timeline (history of the belief and its institutions, distinct from the myth)**

- Around 1830: founding of the Ngondo assembly according to the tradition recorded by M. Doumbé-Moulongo (1972), whose "father" is said to be King Ngando a Kwa (d. 1845).
- 1843-1845: Baptist missions at Bimbia, then Douala (Alfred Saker, 1845); the already established jengu cult resists preaching.
- 1884: German-Duala treaty, German protectorate; 1888: founding of the Native Baptist Church; 1891: the Bakweri repel the Gravenreuth expedition at Buea (Buea razed in 1894).
- Early twentieth century: the missionary Carl Bender describes the liengu rites (published 1924); 1929: Charles Steane, a Bakweri scholar, gives the Moto myth to B. G. Stone.
- 1949: public revival of the Ngondo by the Duala chiefs (Betote Akwa); petition handed to the UN visiting mission.
- December 1981: festival banned by the governor of the Littoral (Austen 1992; other sources say 1972); 1991: revival, the Ngond'a Duala becomes the Ngond'a Sawa.
- 4 December 2024: "Ngondo, worship of water oracles" inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (decision 19.COM 7.B.28).

**Notable quotations (attested, sources checked)**

- "Bakweri women themselves bound their world as including the wild that Moto excluded. They go through rites by which they become LIENGU mermaid spirits, or spirits of the forest, generally in adolescence, and retain this feature of womanhood throughout their lives." Edwin Ardener, "Belief and the Problem of Women", 1972/1975 (extract reproduced on bakwerirama, 2007).
- "The maengu are held in high esteem among the Wakweli and are credited with superhuman powers. When the witch doctor and his means fail, the Liengu is called." Carl Bender, *Religious and ethical beliefs of African Negroes*, 1924 (quoted by Dibussi Tande, 2007).
- "The Ngondo's focus on the jengu water-spirit cult represents an instructive reinterpretation of the critical role played by riverain navigation, migration, and related social hierarchies in earlier Duala middleman experience." Ralph Austen and Jonathan Derrick, *Middlemen of the Cameroons Rivers*, 1999, pp. 2-3.
- The *ndimsi*, "that other dimension of reality beyond the immediate reach of the senses and of common knowledge" (our translation). Manga Bekombo, *Psychopathologie africaine*, 1997.
- No words of a jengu, a diver or an old prophecy are given as quotations: the Ngondo messages are known only through the press (for example, 2019).

**Debates and uncertainties**

- The Ngondo: an ancient institution (Sawa tradition, around 1830) or a tradition largely reinvented in 1949 (Austen)? Both are partly true: old ritual societies, modern public festival.
- Date of the ban: 1981 (Austen) or 1972 (French Wikipedia).
- Origin of the cult: "from the west, perhaps the Ijo" according to overviews, with no older source checked.
- Gender: cult mainly female among the Bakweri, mainly male among the Duala according to some overviews; openness to both sexes may be recent.
- Appearance: light skin and long hair (popular sources) or dark skin (Batanga, 2017); possible influence of the Mami Wata icon (the griot's hypothesis, not proven).
- The "four blessings" and the masked ekale come from modern overviews whose older sources were not read.
- Mojili as originally the manatee: Ardener's hypothesis.
