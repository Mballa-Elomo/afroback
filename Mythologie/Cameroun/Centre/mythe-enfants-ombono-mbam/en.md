# The Children of Ombono

The great genealogical story of the peoples of the Mbam (Gunu, Elip, Mmala, Yangben and Baca, known together as the Yambassa; the Maande, or Lemandé; the Bafia; the Yambetta; the Banen; the Mbure; the Sanaga), in the Mbam-et-Inoubou and Mbam-et-Kim divisions of Cameroon's Centre region. Version 1, 2026-10-16. The research file is in `recherche.md` (in French).

> This story gathers oral traditions. They are genealogies and founding legends, not established historical facts. They do not all say the same thing, and the griot has not melted them into one: each voice is returned to the people who carry it.

---

## The griot's story

### I. The land where people call each other "Région"

Listen.

In the middle of Cameroon there is a country where the forest cannot make up its mind. It advances, it retreats, it lets the savanna through in long tongues of tall grass, then it comes back to huddle along the rivers. People call it the Mbam, after a river that comes down from the north, broad and brown, and pours itself into the Sanaga. Along its banks, and along the banks of the Noun and the Inoubou, live peoples who do not speak the same language.

I mean exactly that: not the same language. The Bafia speak Rikpa. The Banen speak Tunen. Around Bokito and Ombessa you hear Gunu, Nulibie, Mmala, Nubaca. In the hills of Tchekos and Osimbe, people speak Nomaande. The linguist Virginia Boyd, who spent years working in these villages, puts it simply: most people, men and women alike, speak or at least understand one other Mbam language, and more commonly several.

And yet, when these people meet far from home, in Yaoundé, in Douala, in Paris, they recognise one another. They greet each other with a word I find magnificent. They call each other "Région". The president of the Mbam cultural festival, the patriarch Mouté à Bidias Camille, said it before ministers and kings gathered in Bafia in 2019: "our languages are different, our food is different, some of our social and ritual ways are different, but there is a constant middle ground within these differences that forms the bedrock of our Mbam identity, hence the way we recognise each other as 'Région'."

So ask yourself the question I asked myself. What makes peoples with different languages feel like brothers and sisters? Living side by side, of course. The markets, where you buy your pots from the next village. The marriages, which weave threads from one bank to the other. But there is something else, something older, which the elders tell in the evening and which the printed booklets of chiefs' enthronements still carry. There is a name.

That name is Ombono.

Some say Mbono. The Maande write it Ɔmbɔ́nɔ́, with those open vowels you hear at the back of the throat. The Elip sometimes say Ombona, the Baca Ambono. But the name keeps coming back, village after village, like a refrain. Boyd noticed it: many of the Mbam peoples call themselves "children of Ombono" and name one of his sons as their own ancestor.

And here is the most beautiful part of the matter, and the hardest. When you ask who Ombono was, every people answers. And no two answers are the same. For some, Ombono is a hunter. For others, a young woman, the only daughter of a runaway slave. For others still, a mother who had eleven children. Here a father, there a son.

A lazy griot would pick the prettiest of these stories and say: this is the truth. I will not. I am going to tell them one after another, each in the mouth of those who carry it. Listen to them the way you would listen to several elders sitting under the same tree, interrupting one another, contradicting one another, and yet all speaking of the same family.

Sit down. The first voice comes from the Maande hills.

### II. The hunter, the dog and the plum tree

In 1981, men of the Maande people, whom the administration calls Lemandé, wrote down the story of their origin. The text is called *La Création de la Famille de Ɔmbɔ́nɔ́*, "The Creation of the Family of Ombono". It bears the name of Ebaya Silas and his companions. It was never published, but Virginia Boyd read it and summarised it in her doctoral thesis, defended at Leiden University in 2015. This is what the Maande voice says.

Ombono was a hunter.

One day he sets out with his dog. He walks a long way, he crosses the grasslands, he follows the tracks of animals, and the hunt gives him nothing. He comes to a stretch of savanna called Osimbe. He is tired. He sees a tree, a plum tree, which the Maande language calls *buhétú*, and he sits down in its shade. He falls asleep.

When he wakes, fruit has fallen all around him. He gathers it up. At least he will not go home empty-handed. He carries the fruit back to his wife. Her name is Ekiíkí. She soaks the fruit and makes the evening meal with it.

Stop for a moment on that plum tree. The text does not name the species. But in Cameroon, when people say "plum", they first think of the safou, the purple fruit botanists call *Dacryodes edulis*, the "African plum" that grows in every village and is eaten roasted or boiled, with cassava, maize or plantain. If that is the tree, then Ekiíkí's gesture is the gesture of every mother in the Centre region: putting the plums in hot water to soften them. I offer you that as a guess, not as a certainty.

The next time, Ekiíkí goes hunting with her husband. They come to the plum tree. And there, together, they decide to build their house beneath it.

That is the whole founding. No war. No miracle. A failed hunt, a generous tree, a meal, and a woman who says: let us stay here.

But the Maande text adds a detail that changes everything. Ekiíkí was not Maande. She was a daughter of the Banen, the neighbouring people, the people of Ndikiniméki. And Ombono had not paid her bride price. He had not handed over the marriage compensation that a wife's family has every right to expect.

Understand what that means here. In the societies of the Mbam, marriage is not the business of two people. It is an alliance between two families, sealed with words, palm wine, kola nuts, and the bride price. Among the Yambassa, reports an archaeology student at the University of Yaoundé I who worked in Ombessa, the bride price used to be made of heads of large livestock, baskets of yams, and iron objects. An unpaid bride price is an open debt between two lineages.

So how did Ombono pay?

With his first son.

The first child of Ombono and Ekiíkí was named Benenyi. And Benenyi was sent to his mother's brothers, to the Banen, in place of the bride price. The firstborn handed back to his mother's family, to settle his father's debt.

Listen to what that tells us. It is not only the story of a couple. It is an explanation. Why are the Maande and the Banen so close, so close that the ethnologist Idelette Dugast, who studied the Banen from 1934 to 1956, noted that Banen traditions "very often bring us back to the Lemandé, whose stock seems indeed to be shared"? The Maande voice answers in its own way: because one of our first children went to live with them. Because there is Ombono's blood among the Banen, and Banen blood in the mother of all the Maande. The debt of a bride price became a bond of kinship.

The rest of the Maande text, Boyd says, is a long list. The children of Ombono and their descendants. And that list is a map of the Mbam. After Benenyi comes Omaŋa, then Kóono, ancestor of the Yambeta. Then Ɔmaándɛ́, ancestor of the Maande themselves, whose sons bear the names of the Maande villages: Osimbe, the savanna of the plum tree, Nyiambya, Anyangɛma, Béyéke and others. Then Ekiíki, who carries almost the same name as his mother, ancestor of the Bafia, with the villages of Bɔta and Muk on the road from Bafia to Bokito. Then Omendé, ancestor of the Yangben, from whom descend Balamba among the Elip, and Kefíke and Báká among the Gunu. Then Kala, another Yangben ancestor, from whom descend three Mmala villages: Kɔta, Yɔr and Bényi.

Do you see what this list does? It takes nearly all the peoples of the Mbam and seats them around a single hearth, that of a hunter and his Banen wife, under a plum tree. That is the Maande version. It is not the only one.

### III. The slave who loved his master's wife

Now let us cross over towards Bokito and Ombessa, to the Gunu. Their voice is entirely different.

In November 2013, a man named Sintsimé Crépin, from Ombessa, recorded the story of an elder, Boyomo Mouko Michel. Virginia Boyd published it two years later. This is what the Gunu voice says.

On the other side of the river there lived a great warrior. He was the chief of his tribe. And he had a slave named Kamba, son of Nnyole.

The chief had a wife, and that wife was beautiful. Kamba fell in love with her.

You know what that meant, in that world. A slave who raises his eyes to his master's wife, to the wife of a war chief, signs his own death. And Kamba was condemned to die.

So Kamba ran. He ran away with his wife, the story says, and Gunu tradition names that wife Molela. The text I hold does not say in so many words that Molela was the chief's wife for whom he had risked everything. That is the most natural reading, but I will not set it down as fact. What the Gunu voice affirms is that Kamba and Molela fled together, that they crossed the river, the Sanaga, and reached the land of the Banen, which was then, it says, thinly peopled.

They had only one child. A daughter.

Her name was Ombono.

Measure the reversal. Among the Maande, Ombono is a hunter who founds a home. Among the Gunu, Ombono is a girl, the only child of a fugitive couple, born far from her parents' land, on a bank where they had neither chief nor master. And from her, from that only daughter, five sons are born, five founders. Gunu, ancestor of the Gunu. Lemande, ancestor of the Maande. Iguigui, ancestor of the Bafia. Saasa, ancestor of the Sanaga. Zong, ancestor of the Elip.

The Gunu call these five peoples, Boyd reports, the five tribes "which crossed the river".

I want you to hear the strength of this story. In many traditions around the world, peoples like to give themselves royal ancestors, sons of chiefs, invincible heroes. Here, the Gunu voice places a slave at the beginning. A man without rank, condemned, with nothing on his side but his love and his legs to run. The line of five peoples begins with a transgression, an exile and a daughter. And that daughter, Ombono, becomes the mother of them all.

What does the story mean? In what I have been able to read, the elders do not explain it, and I will not put words in their mouths. But one thing is certain: the societies of the Mbam have long paid close attention to the line of mothers. In Ombessa, a University of Yaoundé I study recalls that a child used to be named with reference to its mother, and cites the historian Daniel Abwa, according to whom some sources say the founding ancestors of the great Mbam families were women. In the village of Yambassa, near Bokito, people still say that palm wine is best served by one's sister's son. In such a world, there is nothing strange about a woman being the root of five peoples.

### IV. The mother of eleven children

The third voice comes from Bafia, and more precisely from a group of Bafia clans called the Beké: Kiiki, Ribang, Mouko, Bitang.

On 31 January 2006 the village of Ribang enthroned a new chief, His Majesty Bol à Nwatsock Adamou. For the occasion, a booklet was printed, as is often done in Cameroon, with the history of the chiefdom. It opens with a caution I admire: "It is never easy to trace a history based on oral tradition." Then it says: "several concurring sources agree that the Ribang are the descendants of the ancestor NKE and of his wife MBONO. The story goes that MBONO had many children who settled throughout the greater Mbam."

Here Mbono is a woman, the wife of Nké. The mother.

How many children? Eleven, answers the Cameroonian historian Gabriel Maxime Dong Mougnol, of the University of Yaoundé I. In an article published in 2015, he reports a version collected as early as 1946 by a French administrator named Coquil, and he writes that it "perfectly corroborates the version drawn from oral tradition". I give it to you as it has been quoted.

Once there was a couple, Nké and his wife Mbono. Their first home lay in the north of today's Noun division, between the Noun river and the Mbam, somewhere between Massangam and Foumban. They had eleven children, and the eleven scattered.

Iring, the eldest, went to the Banen, and his descendants still live there.
Sibé founded the Lemandé group of Ossimb.
Sessé had his descendants among the Yambetta.
Ribaï fathered the Niambai group among the Lemandé.
Kiiki fathered the Kiiki-Ribang clan among the Bafia.
Zoh founded the Tchékos clan among the Lemandé.
Zong founded Bokaga and Assala among the Yambassa.
Kon founded the village of Kon among the Yambetta.
Bitang, his brother, fathered the Bitang clan among the Bafia.
Mouko is the ancestor claimed by the Mouko clan of Bafia.
And Bessassa has his descendants among the Ngoro.

Eleven names. Eleven directions. One mother.

Notice that this list does not match the Maande list, nor the Gunu one. Here Zong is a son of Mbono, there a grandson of Ombono. Here the Banen are the eldest of the family, whereas we will see in a moment that many Banen refuse that kinship. But notice the echoes too. Among the Yangben, Virginia Boyd recorded in 2013, from Kibassa Otoke, another list of Ombono's children: Koon, Kiiki, Muko, Bitang, Bongo, Omende, Kiyangaben. Kiiki, Muko, Bitang. The same three names as the Beké clans of Bafia. Two peoples who do not share a language, asked years apart, and the same trio of ancestors comes back.

And among the Yangben, Ombono is no longer a mother. He is a father, the father of those seven sons. Among the Baca of Bongo too: Bongo, they say, is the son of Ambono and the brother of Balamba, Omende, Yangben and Kiiki.

Now I must tell you about another name, one that hovers around Ombono like a faithful shadow. Ambassa.

Where does the word "Yambassa" come from, the name the Germans extended to four clans, Gunu, Elip, Mmala and Yangben? The people here say it themselves: from *bunya Ambassa*, "the descendants of Ambassa". A thesis from the Higher Teacher Training College in Yaoundé gives it in Gunu as *ba'a bouy'ambassa*, "those of Ambassa's house". The name of the country is a sentence about kinship.

But who was Ambassa? Here again the voices part ways. According to some, Boyd reports, Ambassa was Ombono's wife. According to others, his son. Among the Elip, the informant Esseba Ombessa Lambert makes Ambassa a grandson of Mbɔnɔ and the father of Olamba, ancestor of the Elip and the Gunu. An Elip document from 1988, by Abiadina Samba, makes Ombona the son of a certain Belibe and counts Ambassa among his grandchildren.

I will not choose. I will tell you what I see. Ombono and Ambassa are like two beams of the same house: nobody remembers any more which was laid first, but the roof rests on both.

And there is the hunt. Everywhere. The chief of Bongo, His Majesty Ntsomo Npong Pierre, told Boyd how the ancestor Bongo, after a hunt that brought no game, lost his way in the savanna of Buyok and finally settled at the place called Nda mpile, "the great oil palm", where he founded the village that bears his name. Among the Elip of Mana-Kanya, the ancestor Olouo found a child in the bush while hunting, adopted him, and that child, Killikoto, gave his name to a village. It is the pattern Boyd found in nearly all the Mbam stories: a hunter loses his way, finds a place rich in game or fruit, stops, founds, and the village takes his name. The lost hunter of Osimbe is not alone. He has brothers all over the region.

### V. Those who say: we are not of Ombono

An honest griot does not let you believe that everyone sings the same refrain. In the Mbam there are peoples and clans who say plainly: Ombono is not our ancestor. Their voices are part of the story too.

Let us begin with the Banen of Ndikiniméki.

A Banen man, Baléhen Jacques René, told Boyd an entirely different origin. There was a man named Biwoung, and he had four sons: Ganté, Onga, Munen and Bofia. The four left the region of Foumban, in Bamoun country. Ganté settled towards Bangangté and founded that people. Onga stopped near Tonga. Munen and Bofia crossed the Ndé river together, and stayed together for a while. Then their sons quarrelled. Bofia went south, to where the town of Bafia stands today. Munen stayed, with his two sons, Ndiki and Niméki. And the land took their names: Ndikiniméki.

A word about names, while we are here. In Tunen, the Banen language, the particle *ndik* or *ndek* means "those of". A history student at the University of Yaoundé I, Thomas Jules Moukoli, explains it in 2022: Ndik-Hok, those of Hok; Ndik-niméki, those of Niméki. Ndikiniméki therefore means, literally, "those of Niméki". And "Banen" is said to be the plural of Munen, a name which, according to the Banen tradition he reports, means the noble man.

Now this story of Biwoung is not new. Sixty years earlier, Idelette Dugast had collected another form of it among the Ndiki, and published it in 1955 in her monograph. Moukoli quotes it in full. Listen to it: it is one of the finest scenes of the Mbam.

There was a man who lived near the river Nomo, the Noun. His name was Bayessek. He had four sons: Munen, Nganite, Mutemp and Mofe. The father left the Nomo and climbed a mountain, which his sons called by his name, Omban wo Bayessek, "the sky of Bayessek". He made his sons work in his fields, he ordered them to turn the earth. And he cooked food for them: chickens, taro, groundnuts.

But when it came to sharing, the father gave Munen a cooked cockerel. To Mutemp he gave meat, and to Nganite as well. And to Mofe, a pot of frogs.

The last three grew angry, because they had not been given a cockerel. They argued, they ended up fighting, they wounded one another. So they parted, each going to live on his own. Nganite left the banks of the Nomo, climbed the mountain and became the father of all the Bangangté. Mutemp crossed the river and became the ancestor of all the Bamoun. Mofe went down the course of the Nomo and fathered the Bafia. And Munen stayed with his father, had many children, and called them the Banen. One of Munen's sons was named Ndiki.

Did you hear it? The father's name changed between the two stories, Bayessek here, Biwoung there. The sons' names changed too. But the shape is the same, sixty years apart: four brothers, one who founds the Bangangté, one who founds Bafia, Munen who stays at home. That is how an oral tradition lives: the names move, the frame holds.

And the cockerel? Moukoli questioned notables in Ndiki between 2018 and 2020. They explained to him that among the Banen the cockerel is the animal of justice, as the tortoise is among the Bafia. Someone who suffers repeated injustice can bury a cockerel while pronouncing curses, and give the guilty party nine days to confess. Giving the cockerel to Munen meant naming him heir, the one who would guide his brothers. The others refused it. And one family broke into four peoples.

The Banen are not the only ones. Among the Yambetta, a speaker of the language, Bolioki Léonard-Albert, recorded two opposing stories in two dialects of the same tongue. The people of Kon, Kon-Kidoun and Edop, who speak Nigii, call themselves descendants of Mbono, like the Gunu. But the people of Babetta, Bamoko, Bayomen and Bebis, who speak Nɛdɛk, tell how a certain Timin, fleeing a war in Bamoun country, reached the right bank of the Noun and settled there, and how his three sons, Pɛda, Onkon and Yomɛn, founded their three villages. These do not call themselves children of Mbono. They call themselves the true natives of Yambetta country.

And in Bafia itself there are two memories. For the Bafia are not a single block. Alongside the Beké, children of Nké and Mbono, there are the Bekpak, who see themselves as the first occupants and the owners of the Rikpa language. Their genealogy does not pass through Ombono. It begins with a couple, Nkpag and Wayag, according to a page published by the Mbam'Art association. A booklet from the Koro canton, printed in 2007 for the enthronement of its chief, tells that Kodoh à Mpwack and Binkirah à Wayack (after the "à" you can recognise the names of Nkpag and Wayag) had five children, Roum, Gouifé, Yakan, Ngam and Koro, whose names are today those of the great Bafia groups. And when the mother died, she handed Koro, the youngest, a hoe, with these words: "you are the granary of the whole family".

Notice the shape of those names: Koro à Binkira, Bol à Zom, Zom à Isseri. Among the Bafia, a person's name carries a parent's name along with it. You say who you are by saying whom you come from.

Among the Bekpak, another memory says they came from the Tikar plain around Bankim and are kin to the Bamoun and the Nso. And the Beké, the Bekpak say, are people who came from Yambassa country, whom they took in and who adopted their language. Do you understand what that means? In Bafia, two genealogies live side by side not because someone is wrong, but because two groups live side by side, and one of them remembers having welcomed the other.

There is even, among the Bekpak, a little naming story that resembles the quarrel over the cockerel. The village of Tsèkani, according to a Mbam'Art page, bears a name that means "what is it?", or rather "what is going on between you?". That is what the ancestor Koro is said to have thrown at two of his children, forever quarrelling, and above all, the text specifies, when the meals were shared out. The authors say themselves that several anecdotes circulate, and that they kept the one that seemed most likely to them.

### VI. The serpent underfoot, and the quarrel over the dish

Now that you have heard the voices, let us rise a little. Like a bird flying over the Mbam, let us look at the patterns that return from one story to the next. What I say here is my reading as a griot, resting on what researchers have noted; it is not a word of the elders.

First pattern: the river that must be crossed. The five tribes of the Gunu are those which "crossed the river". Munen and Bofia cross the Ndé. Timin settles on the bank of the Noun. And Virginia Boyd noticed that almost every people of the Mbam tells of a crossing. In one of the versions she reports, the people find themselves trapped on the bank of an immense river. In the morning, a bridge has appeared, as if by miracle. They begin to cross, rejoicing. But an old blind man, leaning on his spear as on a walking stick, stumbles and drives the point into the bridge. The bridge was an immense boa. Wounded, the serpent dives and vanishes, and the people are cut in two: those who have crossed, those who stayed behind.

If you have heard AFROBACK tell the story of Ngan Medza, the serpent of the Beti crossing, you recognise this scene. It does not belong to the Beti alone. In 2014 Boyd even heard, at Campo, on the edge of the ocean, an Iyassa man named Patrice Ipoua tell her that his people had left the Mbam and crossed the Sanaga on the back of a giant snake, and that those left behind were called the Isanaga. The direction of the crossing changes from people to people: among some Elip, they cross the Sanaga from south to north to reach the great savannas of the Mbam; among the Yangben, there are two crossings, one northward, the other back to the south; among the Nigii, it is the Noun that is crossed. The serpent-bridge is a story of the whole region, from the Sanaga to the Mbam. The river divides, and the story stitches together.

Second pattern: the lost hunter. Ombono under the plum tree. Bongo under the great palm. Olouo finding a child in the bush. Among the Mmala, a hunter and his dog come upon four people on a termite mound, and the village will be called Begni, "four". The hunter is the one who leaves the known world. He loses his way, and in losing it he finds. In this borderland between forest and savanna, where people hunt with traps, spears and bows in the tall grass, the hunter is the natural explorer.

Third pattern: the meal that divides. Munen's cockerel and Mofe's pot of frogs. Koro's children quarrelling when the food is shared. And among the Yambetta of Kon, this detail, gathered from Mboussi Ntafor: the Nigii are said to have separated from the Gunu over a dispute about the entrails of an antelope. Meat again. Sharing again. As if the elders wanted to say: a family does not break over great things. It breaks around the dish, when one believes he has received less than the other.

Fourth pattern: the debt of alliance. Benenyi given back to his Banen uncles to pay the bride price. Ekiíkí the Banen woman, mother of the Maande. The chief's wife loved by the slave Kamba. In these stories, it is women and marriages that carry people from one people to another. The borders between the peoples of the Mbam are borders of alliance, crossed by a wife or by a child.

And now that you hold these four patterns in mind, read the map again. The river, the hunt, the meal, the bride price. These are the four things that, in real life, make and unmake villages: people move, they settle where the land feeds them, they fight over an inheritance, they marry beyond their own walls. The story of Ombono, for its part, tells no miracle. It tells life, set in order.

### VII. What Ombono does today

There remains the question a young man once asked me: "So, griot, is all this true or not?"

I will answer in three steps.

First, what we do not know. No one can date Ombono. A patriarch of Ombessa says that Gunu lived "five hundred years before the colonisers came"; that is a way of saying "a very long time ago", not a date. A young archaeologist at the University of Yaoundé I, who studied the old pottery of Ombessa, places it, by comparison with other sites, in a long period running from the second millennium of our era to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and she herself calls for more precise dating. What we know for certain is that these genealogies were already being told when Idelette Dugast was studying the Banen between the 1930s and the 1950s, and when the administrator Coquil collected them in 1946.

Next, what is debated. Where did Ombono's family come from? From the north of the Noun, between Massangam and Foumban, according to Coquil's version as taken up by Dong Mougnol. From the south of Bamoun country, according to the historian Eldridge Mohammadou. From the Tikar plain, according to the Bekpak. From the east, crossing the Sanaga twice, according to what is attributed to the researcher Hans Wilhelm (1973) for the Elip and Yangben. And who belongs to the family? There the scholars argue as much as the brothers over the cockerel. The Cameroonian historian Paul Valentin Emog gives the Elip no place among Mbono's descendants. The anthropologist Philippe Laburthe-Tolra is said to have judged their descent from Mbono "purely fictitious" and to link them instead to a people the Germans called Bati, known in Manguissa traditions on the other side of the Sanaga. I have not been able to read his book myself; I report his view as others quote it.

There is even a voice that links Ombono to the Beti. A text by the Mbam'Art association, repeated by the online encyclopaedia, makes Mbono "a descendant of Nnanga, the mythical ancestor of the Beti". I found no scholarly work that confirms this connection. But there are real bridges: the same serpent-bridge on both sides of the river; the Manguissa, Emog says, who also call themselves descendants of Mbono; the Elip linked to the Bati of Manguissa tradition. Take the link with Nanga for what it is: a proposal of kinship, not an established genealogy.

And there is a linguists' hypothesis, broader still. A researcher, Rebecca Grollemund, suggested in 2012 that the starting point of the great migrations of Bantu-speaking peoples lay close to the Mbam. Virginia Boyd judges part of her argument poorly founded, but writes that the idea of a migration from this region is "not necessarily wrong". Keep it as an open question, nothing more. But what a question! The land of the children of Ombono might be one of the old gateways through which the peoples of Central Africa passed.

Finally, what the myth does. Because that is where everything becomes clear.

Virginia Boyd, after gathering all these voices, concludes with great wisdom: "While these histories are not direct evidence for a historical event of such a river-crossing, they do suggest that these traditions are shaped to forge an idea of common origin." She adds that the sense of kinship among these peoples may be "due either to a common origin or convergence". Perhaps both.

And the ethnologist Jean-Louis Siran, who worked for a long time among the Vute, neighbours of the Mbam peoples across the Mbam river, wrote a sentence that taught me a great deal. He says no one ever told him a myth. The Vute spoke to him of the ancestors, the *maap*, who live in the rocks along the river and whom people see "in the form of a snake", and whose permission you must ask before fishing. For them it was not a myth: it was life. And Siran gently mocks the scholars who always look for the original version, the first, the true one. The myth, he says, commenting on Dumézil, is "that part of the original text of which we do not have the text".

That is why I have not given you a single version of Ombono. It does not exist. There is no original text hidden somewhere. There are voices, and their sum is the myth.

What this myth does, the people of the Mbam still practise. Among the Bafia, when someone dies who has lived long and left descendants over three or four generations, the family holds the Dôm, one of the most prestigious of their funeral rites. The patriarch Mouté à Bidias Camille described it in 2020. All the descendants of an ancestor are gathered "so that all may know one another and keep up ties of brotherhood". Because, he says, "we remain brothers and sisters until the seventh generation". On the day of the Dôm, every lineage comes with an animal, each lineage head receives a knotted palm rib, and people share the heart and lungs of the animals, "blood and breath", in small pieces, five for the head of the family, two for each of the others. A Bafia proverb goes with the rite: living long always ends in a line of descendants.

Do you see the link? The Dôm does, at the scale of a family, what the story of Ombono does at the scale of a region: it gathers scattered people around a shared ancestor so that they recognise one another. And this time it does so around a fair sharing of the meal. Like an answer, perhaps, to Munen's cockerel.

The shadow must be told as well. These genealogies are not only songs of unity. They also serve to say who came first to a piece of land, who came later, who has the right to command. Many of the chiefdoms known in the Mbam today were created under German and then French colonial rule; a page by the Mbam'Art association says so bluntly about Tsèkani, where the old dean Nnomi à Zap, humiliated by a German officer, sent a younger man in his place. The 1946 version was collected by an administrator at the very moment the cantons were being redrawn. And the historian Dong Mougnol has studied how two "brother peoples", the Bitang and the Bassala, both children of Mbono, tore each other apart over land. Being children of the same ancestor never prevented quarrels. The stories themselves say so: look at the cockerel.

Nor was the real history of the Mbam gentle. At the end of the nineteenth century, the region lived through the warlike expansion of the Vute chiefdoms, which the historian Eldridge Mohammadou reconstructed as early as 1967, and then the German conquest. Whole groups were displaced: the Tsinga pushed south of the Sanaga, the Ngoro enslaved and scattered, Dugast reports, as quoted by Boyd. The genealogies of Ombono came through those storms. Sometimes they even helped people rebuild.

Objects travelled too. In the Ethnological Museum in Berlin lie pieces collected in the Mbam during the colonial period by the German Günter Tessmann: an ivory signal horn from the Bafia, a Bafia thumb piano made of raffia stems, a mirliton carved from a leopard bone, a small Yambassa bell made from the fruit of a borassus palm, Yambassa flutes. Their online records say nothing about the ancestors; they say only that these peoples had voices to call, to dance, to answer one another from hill to hill. And that some of those voices are kept far from here.

And yet the word holds. Since 1996, the Mbam'Art and Development association has run a festival where the "families" of the Mbam dance, where the kings of the Bamoun and the Nso come as brothers, where the people of the Mbam wear a rallying cloth. In the villages, children are still called Ombono, Ambassa, Kiiki. Among the hundreds of people who helped Virginia Boyd describe their languages, there was a man in Kananga named Ombono Athanase, and in Bualando an Ambassa. The ancestors are not in the books. They are on the school registers.

So, young person of the Mbam, or young person from anywhere, here is what I leave you.

You do not need your origin to fit into a single sentence to be proud of it. The children of Ombono have six, seven, ten ways of telling where they come from: a hunter under a plum tree, a slave in love, an only daughter, a mother of eleven children, four brothers angry over a cockerel. They do not agree. And that is exactly their wealth. They kept every voice instead of silencing the others.

If one day someone asks you where you come from, you can answer like a child of Ombono. I come from a river my ancestors crossed. I come from a tree under which someone decided to stay. I come from a woman who came from elsewhere, and from a debt that was paid with love rather than with goats. I come from brothers who quarrelled, and found each other again.

And when, far from home, you meet someone who says "Région", smile. Now you know what they mean.

## Structured sheet

### The Children of Ombono

- **Type of content**: a great genealogical and founding story from oral tradition, in several versions (genealogical myth, founding legends). The figures (Ombono, Ambassa, Kamba, Nké, Biwoung, Bayessek...) are ancestors of tradition, not historically attested.
- **Peoples concerned**: Yambassa (Gunu, Elip, Mmala, Yangben, Baca), Maande (Lemandé), Bafia (mainly the Beké clans), Yambetta (Nigii), Sanaga; the Banen, the Nɛdɛk Yambetta and the Bekpak Bafia have their own genealogies, presented as such.
- **Era**: pre-colonial (traditions that cannot be dated; recorded in writing since Dugast, 1930s-1950s, and the administrator Coquil, 1946)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Cameroon, Centre region: Mbam-et-Inoubou, Mbam-et-Kim)
- **Theme**: other (genealogical myth, origin and unity of the Mbam peoples)

**Timeline (history of the collection and context, distinct from the myth)**

- 1934-1956: Idelette Dugast studies the Banen; she records the story of Bayessek and his four sons (published in 1955, *Monographie de la tribu des Ndiki*, vol. I, pp. 56-57).
- 1946: the French administrator Coquil records a version of the origins of the Mbam peoples (Nké, Mbono and their eleven children), later used by G. M. Dong Mougnol (1998, 2015).
- 1981: unpublished Maande text *La Création de la Famille de Ɔmbɔ́nɔ́* (Ebaya Silas et al.).
- 1996: first Mbam'Art festival (association founded in 1994), a public place of memory for Mbam unity.
- 2013-2014: named recordings (Boyomo Mouko Michel, Kibassa Otoke, Chief Ntsomo Npong Pierre, Baléhen Jacques René, Bolioki Léonard-Albert...) gathered by V. L. Boyd; thesis published in 2015.

**Debates and uncertainties**

- **Ombono's sex and place**: hunter and husband (Maande), only daughter of Kamba (Gunu), wife of Nké and mother of eleven (Beké Bafia, Coquil-Dong Mougnol version), father of seven sons (Yangben), father of Bongo (Baca), son of Belibe or of Dugalagala (Elip). Ambassa is sometimes his wife, sometimes his son. Left open.
- **The lists of children contradict one another** (Kiiki is ancestor of the Bafia for the Maande, of the Maande for the Yangben; Kon/Kóono ancestor of the Yambeta or of the Gunu). There are agreements too (Kiiki, Muko, Bitang among the Yangben as among the Beké Bafia).
- **Who belongs to the family**: the Banen are included by Dong Mougnol (Iring the eldest), but their own stories (Biwoung in Boyd, Bayessek in Dugast) link them to the Bamoun and the Bangangté. The Elip: excluded by Emog; their descent judged "purely fictitious" by Laburthe-Tolra (quoted, not read), who links them to the Bati. The Nɛdɛk Yambetta and the Bekpak Bafia have their own ancestors.
- **Link to Nanga, ancestor of the Beti**: asserted by an association source (Mbam'Art, via French Wikipedia); no scholarly source read confirms it. Real but indirect bridges (shared serpent-bridge, the Manguissa and the Elip).
- **Geographical origin**: north of the Noun (Coquil, Dong Mougnol), south of Bamoun country (E. Mohammadou), Tikar plain (Bekpak), the east (Wilhelm, for Elip and Yangben), south of the Sanaga (Mbure). Egyptian or Sudanese hypotheses (the land of Punt) have no established basis.
- **Dating**: impossible. Ombessa pottery dated only by comparison (second millennium CE to the 18th-19th centuries).
- **The "Bantu cradle" in the Mbam** (Grollemund 2012): a linguist's hypothesis, partly contested by Boyd.
- **The plum tree**: identifying it with the safou (*Dacryodes edulis*) is the griot's guess; the text does not name the species.
- **Political uses**: genealogies drawn into land and chieftaincy disputes (the Bitang-Bassala conflict; a version collected by a colonial administrator in 1946; chiefdoms largely created under colonial rule).

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The hunter Ombono, his dog and the plum tree of Osimbe; the son Benenyi given to the Banen in place of the bride price (Maande).
- Kamba the slave, condemned for loving his master's wife, his flight with Molela across the Sanaga, their only daughter Ombono and the five tribes "which crossed the river" (Gunu).
- Nké, Mbono and their eleven children scattered across the Mbam (Beké Bafia, Coquil version).
- Bayessek and the cooked cockerel that split the Banen, Bamoun, Bangangté and Bafia (Banen, Dugast); Biwoung and his four sons (Banen, Baléhen).

- Timin, founder of the Nɛdɛk Yambetta; Nkpag and Wayag, ancestors of the Bekpak Bafia; Tsèkani, "what is going on between you?".
