# Ernest Ouandié

A schoolteacher who became the last historic leader of the UPC, chief of the armed resistance against France and the regime of Ahmadou Ahidjo, publicly executed in Bafoussam in 1971, and rehabilitated as a national hero of Cameroon in 1991.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

A little less than a century ago, in the highlands of western Cameroon, in Bamiléké country, a child is born. The year is 1924. The land then called French Cameroon is not even a colony in the full sense of the word: it is a mandate territory, wrested from Germany after the First World War and entrusted to France by the young League of Nations, with the promise, one day, of self-government. That promise, France would spend forty years betraying, in its own way.

About this boy's childhood, today's accounts sometimes disagree on the exact name of his village, on the names of his father and mother. What is known with certainty is that he grows up in this Bamiléké country, that he is sent to the regional school and then to the École primaire supérieure of Yaoundé, the best training the colonial system then offers a young Cameroonian, and that in November 1943 he leaves it with a certificate as a native schoolteacher (moniteur indigène). He becomes a teacher. Like Um Nyobè before him, like so many other figures of that generation, it is the colonizer's own school that will, without meaning to, shape the man who will one day rise against it. France hands him her tools so he can serve her administration. He will use them to fight what that administration does to his people.

For four years, from 1944 to 1948, he teaches, moving from post to post as colonial assignments dictate, in Edéa, in Dschang, in Douala. Then, in 1948, he takes the step that will decide the whole of his life: he joins the Union des populations du Cameroun, the UPC, a very young party, founded a few months earlier by Ruben Um Nyobè and his companions, and daring to demand what almost no one in Africa dares yet demand aloud in those years: immediate independence, and the reunification of the two Cameroons, the one administered by France and the one administered by Britain. In 1952, at the party's congress, he is elected vice-president. He is twenty-eight years old. At this point, he still believes the fight can be won through words, congresses, petitions to the United Nations.

But listen to what happens next, for this is where the history of the whole of Cameroon turns. On 13 July 1955, after riots in Douala violently repressed, the French administration purely and simply bans the UPC. The party no longer has the right to exist in the open. Its militants are hunted down, arrested, in some cases tortured, as Ouandié himself will experience. So the party does not die: it goes underground, it takes to the forests, it becomes a maquis. What France tried to smother through prohibition, she turned into a war. A war that, officially, will never bear that name in the history books of the time, but that will last fifteen years, kill tens of thousands, and remain largely hidden from the French public itself. This is what historians have spent decades documenting, archive after archive, testimony after testimony.

In 1956, Ouandié takes refuge in Kumba, across the administrative border, in the British-administered Southern Cameroons. That is not enough to protect him: in July 1957, under French pressure, the British authorities deport him to Khartoum, in Sudan. From there he moves on to Cairo, then to Conakry, in a Guinea only just independent, then to Accra, in Kwame Nkrumah's Ghana, that cradle of the emerging pan-African movement where so many figures of African liberation cross paths in those years. He is no longer merely a Cameroonian militant. In these years of exile, he becomes a man of the wider pan-African movement.

And meanwhile, at home, repression strikes down the movement's leaders, one after another. On 13 September 1958, Ruben Um Nyobè, the founder, the party's father figure, is killed by French forces in the forest of Sanaga-Maritime, where he had been hiding for years. On 1 January 1960, French Cameroon officially achieves independence, under the presidency of Ahmadou Ahidjo, a man chosen and firmly backed by France. For the UPC, this independence is an illusion: the flag has changed, but the French army continues operating on Cameroonian soil against the maquisards, and the new power in Yaoundé is, in their eyes, nothing but a relay of the former colonizer. Then, in the autumn of 1960, a French secret service agent poisons Félix-Roland Moumié with thallium; Moumié, who had succeeded Um Nyobè at the head of the UPC, dies in agony in Geneva on 4 November 1960.

Understand this moment well. Two founding leaders assassinated within two years, an independence that is one in name only, a war continuing under a new flag. It is into this void that Ernest Ouandié rises: he becomes president of the UPC. In July 1961, he does what few men would have the courage to do: he returns clandestinely to Cameroon, not to hide, but to personally take command of the armed struggle, to reorganize the national liberation army of Kamerun, to hold secret assemblies in the Bamiléké mountains and in the Moungo region to rebuild a general staff, a discipline, a doctrine. On 16 June 1964, in turn, Abel Kingué, the last of the historic companions still alive, dies in exile in Cairo. Ouandié finds himself alone. The last of the four great leaders the UPC had known at its founding. Ahidjo's government names him the nation's public enemy number one.

I must tell you what it means to hold out for ten years in the maquis. This is not a romantic legend of triumphant guerrilla warfare. These are men hunted through the mountains of the west and the hills of the Moungo, entire villages emptied by the army to cut the maquis off from any support, ranks thinning year after year, hunger, sickness, exhaustion. In 1964, as an example meant to terrorize the population, fifteen captured fighters are publicly executed in Douala, Bafoussam, and Edéa. Ouandié, for his part, carries on, year after year, as his companions fall, as his support dwindles, as the country he dreamed of liberating closes in around him.

Between 1965 and 1970, Bishop Albert Ndongmo, a Bamiléké bishop like him, attempts a mediation, it seems at the very request of the ruling power, between Ouandié and President Ahidjo. In 1970, exhausted, in search of shelter, Ouandié takes refuge with the bishop. Before leaving for Rome, the bishop sends him to hide with a catechist near Mbanga. The catechist refuses to take him in and alerts the police. Ouandié and his secretary flee, lose their way in unfamiliar terrain, try to hide under bridges and in banana plantations. On 19 August 1970, exhausted, thirsty, hungry, disoriented, he asks a passerby for help, who recognizes him. He then walks into the police post himself. So ends a decade of clandestine life: not in one last heroic fight, weapon in hand, but in the exhaustion of a hunted man, betrayed by the chance of an encounter.

What follows is a sham of justice. Interrogated for several months, transferred to Yaoundé, he appears on 28 December 1970 before the permanent military tribunal, alongside Bishop Ndongmo, now himself an accused man. His French lawyers, Jacques Vergès, Jean-Jacques de Félice, and Ralph Milner, are refused entry visas into Cameroon. Among the defenders who try, unsuccessfully, to represent him, is even a certain François Mitterrand, future president of the French Republic. None of them will be allowed to plead. Ouandié is tried without his own counsel. On 5 January 1971, he is sentenced to death.

On 15 January 1971, on the market square of Bafoussam, about forty thousand residents are gathered, ordered to attend what is meant to be an exemplary punishment. Ouandié is executed by a firing squad of Cameroonian soldiers, commanded by a French officer, alongside two companions, Gabriel Tabeu, known as Wambo le Courant, and Raphaël Fotsing. Several accounts report that he refuses a blindfold. After the first volley, while he lies wounded but still alive, the French officer commanding the squad steps forward and finishes him with a shot. As for his last words, the testimonies diverge, and I must tell you this honestly: no identical version circulates from one source to another. Some report that he cried out that others would carry on the fight, others that he declared history would judge, still others attribute to him a farewell message to his wife and children. These are not empty inventions, they are fragments of popular memory, passed down and sometimes reshaped over the years. But none of them is a signed record from the time. What is, however, beyond doubt, documented and cross-checked, is the scene itself: a man executed in public, before a crowd forced to witness it, to serve as a lesson to an entire people.

And then, silence. For twenty years, under the presidencies of Ahmadou Ahidjo and then Paul Biya, the name of Ernest Ouandié remains attached, in the official narrative, to that of a rebel, an outlaw. It is only in 1991, in a Cameroon then living through its years of democratic contestation, that the Cameroonian National Assembly votes, on 27 June, to recognize him as a national hero, formalized by Law No. 91/022 of 16 December 1991. Thirty years after independence, the very state that had him publicly shot rehabilitates him through a legal text. Even this rehabilitation remains fragile: in January 2012, UPC militants denounce the state of neglect of his grave in Bafoussam. A country's memory, you see, is never settled once and for all. It is built, contested, repaired, again and again.

So hold on to this. Ernest Ouandié did not begin his life as a hero wrapped in certainties. He began as a schoolteacher trained by the very school of those he would one day fight, as a man who first believed in congresses and motions before choosing clandestine life and arms, as the last survivor of a generation of leaders assassinated one by one, who held out for ten years in the mountains for a country that, officially free, kept hunting him down. He fell alone, betrayed by exhaustion, tried without defenders, executed as a spectacle before his own people, forced to watch. And it took two decades of silence before his own country dared put in writing that he had been a hero. This is what the word independence sometimes costs, when taken seriously all the way through. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Ernest Ouandié**

- **Era**: colonial then independence era (Cameroon under French trusteeship, then independent Cameroon under Ahmadou Ahidjo, 1924-1971)
- **Region**: Central Africa (western Cameroon, Bamiléké country; executed in Bafoussam)
- **Theme**: resistance / politics

**Timeline**

- 1924: born in a village in Bamiléké country, western Cameroon (sources disagree on the exact village name and on details of his family)
- November 1943: obtains a native schoolteacher's certificate from the École primaire supérieure of Yaoundé, becomes a teacher
- 1948: joins the UPC; elected the party's vice-president in 1952
- 13 July 1955: the UPC is banned by the French administration, the party goes underground and armed action begins
- 1958-1964: assassination of Ruben Um Nyobè (13 September 1958), poisoning of Félix-Roland Moumié (died 4 November 1960), death in exile of Abel Kingué (16 June 1964): in that order, Ouandié becomes leader and then the last surviving historic head of the UPC, leading the armed struggle since his clandestine return to Cameroon in July 1961
- 19 August 1970: captured near Mbanga after a decade in the maquis, following the failed mediation of Bishop Albert Ndongmo and the betrayal of a catechist
- 15 January 1971: publicly executed by a firing squad of Cameroonian soldiers commanded by a French officer, on the market square of Bafoussam, before about 40,000 people, alongside Gabriel Tabeu and Raphaël Fotsing
- 16 December 1991: rehabilitated and declared a national hero by Law No. 91/022 (following the Cameroonian National Assembly's vote of 27 June 1991)

**Notable quotes**

No words spoken by Ernest Ouandié at the moment of his execution are confirmed identically by a dated primary source (trial record, signed testimony contemporary with the events). Several versions circulate in the Cameroonian press and popular memory: according to some accounts, he cried out after the first volley that others would carry on the fight; according to others, his last words were an appeal to history to judge; according to still others, he asked that a message be passed to his wife and children. These three versions do not match word for word from one source to another: they are treated here as belonging to popular memory and oral tradition (see below), not as a verified quote.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The exact last words spoken facing the firing squad: three different versions circulate (the announcement that others would carry on the fight, an appeal to history to judge, a message to his wife and children), none of them backed by a dated and signed testimony from the time of the events. They belong to popular memory built after the fact, not to an established fact.
- The precise details of his family life (the exact name of his native village, the names of his parents and siblings, the first name and origin of his wife, the number and names of his children) vary noticeably from one memorial site to another (some mention a village called Bangou, others Badoumla or Ndumla in Haut-Nkam; some name a wife called Marthe, from Edéa). For lack of an academic source clearly reconciling them, these details are deliberately left out of the narrated tale here and flagged as uncertain rather than presented as facts.
- The account of a months-long interrogation involving mistreatment, reported notably by a fellow detainee, Albert Mukong, fits within a context of repression now well documented by historians of the Kamerun war, but the precise details of this episode rest on an individual testimony rather than on an independently cross-checked source.

**Related heroes**

- Ruben Um Nyobè, founder of the UPC, killed by French forces on 13 September 1958 in the forest of Sanaga-Maritime
- Félix-Roland Moumié, second leader of the UPC, poisoned with thallium by a French secret service agent, died in Geneva on 4 November 1960
- Abel Kingué, UPC vice-president alongside Ouandié, died in exile in Cairo on 16 June 1964
- Bishop Albert Ndongmo, Bamiléké bishop, mediator and later co-defendant in the 1970-1971 trial, sentenced to death and then pardoned in 1976
