# Martin Paul Samba (Mebenga m'Ebono)

Bulu chief and resistance fighter against German colonization, southern Cameroon, late nineteenth and early twentieth century.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

More than a century ago, in the south of what was then called Kamerun, a German colony seized through unequal treaties and military campaigns, a child is born in a small village of Bulu country, not far from Ebolowa. He is named Mebenga m'Ebono. The world gives him nothing from the start: his father dies before his birth, his mother shortly after. Orphaned, he is taken in by his people, then, still very young, sent to the coast, to Kribi, where he grows up in the orbit of the foreigners from Europe who, for a few years already, have been planting their flag on this land.

It is there that this child's fate first turns. He catches the attention of a German explorer, Kurt von Morgan, and in 1891 he is sent far from home, to Germany, to receive military training. There, he is baptized, given a new name: Martin-Paul Samba. He is barely fifteen years old. Imagine what that split is like: born in a Bulu village and finding yourself on the benches of a Prussian military academy, learning to march in step, to handle the weapons of an empire that has just settled among your own people.

I must tell you the whole truth, the one that flatters no one. When Samba returns to Kamerun in 1894, now a commissioned officer, he does not return as a resistance fighter. He returns in the service of the German colonial administration. For eight years, he accompanies the military expeditions of the feared Hans Dominik, one of the most brutal officers of German colonization in Central Africa, in so-called "pacification" campaigns waged against other Cameroonian communities. This is not a detail to be erased to make the story prettier. It is a fact: before being a hero of resistance, Martin Paul Samba wore the uniform of those who were subjugating his own continent. True African history is not made of straight lines and spotless statues. It is made of men caught in systems larger than themselves, who sometimes end up turning against the very system that shaped them.

Because that is exactly what happens. In 1902, Samba leaves the army. He settles in Ebolowa as a businessman. And there, in daily contact with his people, far from the staff rooms, he sees what the German colonial regime is really doing to his own. German Kamerun is no benevolent administration: it is a system of forced labor, corporal punishment, brutal requisitions, where Bulu women and children are not spared the harshness of colonial agents. What Samba had served from afar, he now sees up close, and something breaks in him.

In 1910, he becomes a chief within the Bulu hierarchy. A year later, the German administration elevates another man, Charles Atangana, to the rank of paramount chief of the Ewondo, a neighboring people. This choice alarms Samba: he sees in it a sign that the Germans intend to govern southern Kamerun by relying on peoples other than the Bulu, playing one people against another, that old method of colonial powers. It is from this point, between 1911 and 1912, that Samba turns for good. It is no longer about protesting. It is about preparing an uprising.

He makes secret contact with another chief, hundreds of kilometers away on the coast: Rudolf Duala Manga Bell, king of the Bell clan among the Duala, himself exasperated by German land expropriations in Douala. The two men devise a plan as risky as any: Samba would seek weapons from the French, settled in neighboring Equatorial Africa, while Manga Bell would seek support from the British, in neighboring Nigeria. For two years, in the shadows, Samba gathers supporters, trains Bulu warriors, wins the backing of other chiefs such as Madola in Grand Batanga and Edande Mbita, stockpiles weapons. Imagine the courage this demands: preparing, under the nose of a colonial administration whose methods and brutality he knows from the inside, an armed uprising, knowing exactly what awaits him if the plot is discovered.

Summer 1914 arrives, and with it, all of Europe slides toward war. Samba, pressed by events, writes to the French forces stationed in Brazzaville to announce his intention to rise up. This letter, the most dangerous he ever wrote, falls into German hands. On the first of August 1914, he is arrested and charged with high treason.

Not a week passes. On 8 August 1914, in Ebolowa, a German firing squad ends his life. Precision matters here, because history sometimes blends two fates that are not the same: on that very day, in Douala, at the other end of the country, the Germans hang Rudolf Duala Manga Bell and his adviser Ngosso Din, in a separate case, born of the Duala revolt against the expropriation of their lands. Two men, two cities, two stories of resistance, the same fateful date, the same German colonial hand striking at the very moment the First World War is about to shake its grip on Kamerun.

What has been told in Ebolowa ever since, popular tradition has carefully kept: that Samba, facing the firing squad, is said to have refused a blindfold, to have pulled out a white handkerchief and waved it while looking his executioners in the eye, to have declared he was not afraid of death, that they could kill him but they would never have Cameroon. I tell you this because this image has crossed generations and speaks a real truth about the memory a people keeps of its resistance fighters. But I must also be honest with you: no archive of the time, no identified and dated witness confirms this scene word for word. It is a legend, beautiful and dignified, passed down by word of mouth, not a transcript of an execution.

What is beyond doubt, however, is this: a man born in a Bulu village, trained by the colonial power itself to serve it, ended up turning everything he had learned against it, at the cost of his life, only a few years before that same German power lost Kamerun to Franco-British forces. He did not live long enough to see that defeat. But he had seen truly: this regime that seemed unshakable fell, and the name Germany had given him, Martin-Paul Samba, remained engraved, not as that of a loyal servant, but as that of a man who chose, at one point in his life, to stand with his own.

Even today, a statue watches over Ebolowa, from a hill overlooking the town. If you descend from this land, remember that a hero's path is not always straight from day one. There are men who begin on the wrong side of history and who find, one day, the courage to cross to the right one. Samba was one of them. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Martin Paul Samba (born Mebenga m'Ebono)**

- **Era**: colonial (German Kamerun, 1884-1916)
- **Region**: Central Africa (present-day southern Cameroon, Bulu country, Ebolowa region)
- **Theme**: resistance

**Timeline**

- c. 1874-1875: born as Mebenga m'Ebono near Ebolowa, Bulu people (sources waver between 1874 and 1875, orphaned of both parents very early)
- 1891: sent to Germany for military training on the initiative of German explorer Kurt von Morgan; baptized Martin-Paul Samba
- 1894-1902: officer in the service of the German colonial administration, takes part in Hans Dominik's "pacification" expeditions against other Cameroonian communities, before resigning in 1902 to settle as a businessman in Ebolowa
- 1910-1912: becomes a chief within the Bulu hierarchy (1910); the elevation of chief Charles Atangana as paramount chief of the Ewondo by the Germans (1911) fuels his concerns; he begins, together with Duala chief Rudolf Duala Manga Bell, secretly preparing an uprising and seeking French and British weapons
- 1 August 1914: arrested after the interception of a letter addressed to French forces in Brazzaville, charged with high treason
- 8 August 1914: executed by a German firing squad in Ebolowa (the same day, in Douala, in a separate case, the Germans execute by hanging Rudolf Duala Manga Bell and his adviser Ngosso Din)

**Notable quotes**

No quote from Martin Paul Samba has been authenticated by a dated primary source (trial archive, identified eyewitness testimony contemporary with the events). The phrase widely repeated in the Cameroonian press and popular memory, "I am not afraid of death, kill me but you will never have Cameroon," circulates on numerous Cameroonian websites but is always presented "according to legend," with no reference to a period document: it is treated here as belonging to oral tradition (see below), not as a verified quote.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The waving of a white handkerchief facing the firing squad, the refusal of a blindfold, and the phrase "I am not afraid of death, kill me but you will never have Cameroon": this set of elements is reported by numerous contemporary Cameroonian sources as a story passed down in Ebolowa's popular memory, never backed by a dated and signed testimony from the time of the events. It should be told as a legend carrying a strong symbolic truth about the courage attributed to Samba, never as an established fact on the same footing as the date of his execution.
- A Cameroonian documentary made about his life is itself titled "Martin Paul Samba, au-delà de la légende" ["Martin Paul Samba, Beyond the Legend"] (Waanda Stoudio), a sign that the construction of his collective memory in Cameroon readily goes beyond strictly documented facts.

**Related heroes**

- Rudolf Duala Manga Bell, king of the Bell clan among the Duala, executed the same day (8 August 1914) in Douala in a separate case of resistance to colonial expropriation
- Ngosso Din, adviser to Rudolf Duala Manga Bell, executed with him
- Charles (Karl) Atangana, paramount chief of the Ewondo appointed by the German administration: a figure linked by context and rivalry, to be treated separately for his own ambivalent trajectory between collaboration and colonial administration
- Queen Nzinga (Njinga a Mbande), Queen of Ndongo and Matamba, already covered in the Stories & Heroes pillar for the same theme of resistance to colonization
