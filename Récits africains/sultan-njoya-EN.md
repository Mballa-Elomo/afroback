# Sultan Njoya (Ibrahim Njoya)

Seventeenth king (mfon) of the Bamum kingdom, unifier and inventor of the Shümom writing system, western Cameroon, late nineteenth and early twentieth century.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

Long before the first European flags fluttered over the highlands of western Cameroon, an ancient kingdom already stood there, founded in 1394 by a chief named Ncharé Yen, and passed down since, king after king, across the high plateaus around Foumban. A kingdom with its own laws, its blacksmiths, its weavers, its court, its memory kept alive by the mouths of elders alone, for lack of a writing system of its own. It is into this world that, around 1860, the man who would become the seventeenth ruler of this line was born: Njoya, son of King Nsangu.

Njoya's childhood begins in blood and uncertainty. His father dies in battle against the neighboring Nso kingdom, sometime in the 1880s, while Njoya is still a child. His mother, Queen Njapdunke, holds the regency. But among the Bamum, a king cannot fully reign until his father's skull, lost on an enemy battlefield, has been brought back to the kingdom: a matter of honor and legitimacy. It was, it is said, German officers, then extending their influence over this part of Kamerun, who helped negotiate the skull's return. So it is that, from the very beginning, young Njoya's fate becomes entangled with that of the European powers, not as an enemy, but as a cautious partner.

That alone does not secure his throne. Between 1892 and 1895, civil war tears the kingdom apart. Njoya, still young, owes his victory to help from outside, from Sultan Oumarou of Banyo. Hold onto that detail: the future unifier of the Bamum kingdom first had to reach beyond his own borders to survive. Power, among the great African kings as everywhere else, never falls from the sky. It is won, often painfully, sometimes at the cost of uncomfortable alliances.

Once his throne is secure, Njoya rules as a skillful diplomat. The Bamum kingdom is by then part of German Kamerun, and for more than twenty years, relations between the young king and the German colonial administration remain remarkably cordial, at a time when so many other African kingdoms in the region were burning with armed resistance. In 1908, after a military expedition led jointly with German troops against the neighboring Nso kingdom, Njoya makes a spectacular gesture: he gives Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany, for his birthday, his own royal throne, the Mandu Yenu, carved from wood, inlaid with glass beads from Europe and cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean, guarded by carved figures of protective twins. He immediately has another made for himself. In return, the German emperor sends him a uniform of the Prussian Imperial Guard and a portrait, both still on display today at the Palace Museum in Foumban. The Mandu Yenu throne itself has rested for over a century in a museum in Berlin. It remains there today, at the heart of a debate all of Africa knows well: the restitution of works taken during colonization.

But what truly sets Njoya apart is not his diplomacy. It is what he carries in his mind. Around 1896, the young king tells his court of a dream: a teacher appeared to him in his sleep, showing him how to draw a hand on a wooden tablet, then wash the drawing away and drink the water that had cleaned it. Out of this dream comes a fixed idea: to give his people a writing system of their own, distinct from the Arabic script of Hausa traders and the Latin alphabet of Europeans. "If you draw many different things and give them names," he tells his advisers, "I will make a book that speaks without being heard." Those around him doubt, some mock: what is the point, they say, it will never work. Njoya persists. It takes five failed attempts, seven successive versions, more than twenty years of continuous work, between 1896 and 1918, to bring the project to completion: starting from a first set of nearly five hundred pictograms, he simplifies, year after year, down to a final syllabary of roughly eighty characters, named A ka u ku after its first four signs. An entirely new writing system, born from the mind of one man and his court, capable of recording the kingdom's laws as well as its love letters.

And Njoya does not stop there. He opens schools at the palace as early as 1898 to teach this script; by the late 1910s, some twenty schools are training several hundred students across the kingdom. He has maps of his territory drawn up, medical records written, court judgments recorded. He dictates to his scribes an entire chronicle of his people's history and customs, a text later translated into French by Pastor Henri Martin and published in 1952: for the first time, Bamum history is no longer carried solely by the fragile memory of elders, it is set down in black and white, by the Bamum themselves, in their own language and their own alphabet. In 1913, he even launches the construction of a printing press for this script. After seven years of work, the object is finally ready in 1920. It will never be used: pressure from the French colonial administration, now in charge of Cameroon, forces Njoya to destroy it before it ever prints a single page. Imagine the pain of that act: destroying with your own hands the result of seven years of labor, because the ruling power will not tolerate an African king printing his own books in his own script.

For the wind has turned. After the First World War, Germany loses Cameroon, and France takes its place. The new colonial masters do not see Njoya's intelligence as an asset but as a threat: a king with his own writing system, his own schools, his own administration, his own calendar, is too independent a king. Starting in 1920, the French administration methodically reduces the palace staff, places the provinces under direct control, dismantles the craft guilds. In 1931, France finally deposes Njoya and exiles him to Yaoundé, far from his kingdom, far from his people, far from Foumban. He dies there on 30 May 1933, at the age, according to the most widely repeated tradition, of sixty-six.

I must also tell you, to be honest all the way through, that Njoya was a ruler of his time and his culture, with several hundred wives: a visiting British officer counts six hundred in 1915, while other sources put the number, across his whole reign, at close to twelve hundred. His spiritual life, too, was restless: influenced early on by the Islam of Hausa and Fulbe traders, he later welcomed German Christian missionaries, before eventually shaping his own religion, blending Christian, Muslim, and traditional Bamum beliefs, and drawing closer to Islam again in his final years. A man who searches, who tries different paths, who refuses to settle on a single, well-marked road: that too was Njoya.

After his death, his writing system nearly vanished with him: by the 1930s, it was barely used by anyone but the sultan and his inner court. It took until 1985 for his own son and successor, Seidou Njimoluh Njoya, to reopen a school to pass it on again. Today, in Foumban, the palace Njoya rebuilt after a fire, completed in 1917, has become a museum where children still learn to trace the signs a king once dreamed. Thousands of documents written by his hand and those of his scribes have been digitized so they will never be lost again.

This is what a king can do, without cannon or resistance army: refuse to let his people's memory be stolen, and invent, with his own hands, a way to keep it forever. Njoya did not defeat colonization; it eventually broke him, dispossessed him, exiled him far from his own. But the writing system he dreamed up survived exile, survived neglect, survived nearly a century of silence. If you descend from that land, remember that inventing, passing on, writing your own history, is already a form of resistance. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Sultan Njoya (Ibrahim Njoya)**

- **Era**: colonial (Bamum kingdom under German Kamerun then French Cameroon, reign roughly 1886/1887 to 1931, deposed and exiled until his death in 1933)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Bamum kingdom, present-day West Region of Cameroon, capital Foumban)
- **Theme**: science (invention of a writing system); also connects to art (palace architecture) and politics (unification and modernization of the kingdom)

**Timeline**

- c. 1860: birth of Njoya, son of King Nsangu (birth date uncertain across sources, sometimes estimated later, which is not fully consistent with the widely repeated age of 66 at his death in 1933; his father dies in battle against the Nso during his childhood, regency held by his mother Queen Njapdunke)
- 1892-1895: civil war within the Bamum kingdom, won by Njoya with the support of Sultan Oumarou of Banyo, allowing him to secure his personal authority
- 1896-1918: design and progressive refinement of the Bamum script through seven successive versions (from nearly 500 pictograms to a final syllabary of about 80 signs, called "A ka u ku"); first palace schools opened as early as 1898 to teach it
- 1908: gives his royal throne, the Mandu Yenu, to Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany after a joint military campaign against the Nso kingdom (the throne is now held at the Ethnological Museum in Berlin); receives a Prussian military uniform in return
- 1913-1920: rebuilding of the Foumban palace after a fire (completed in 1917); construction of a printing press for the Bamum script, completed in 1920 but destroyed before use under pressure from the French colonial administration
- 1931: deposed and exiled to Yaoundé by the French administration; dies in exile on 30 May 1933

**Notable quotes**

- "If you draw many different things and give them names, I will make a book that speaks without being heard." This line is reported as Njoya's own words to his court when launching the writing project, as relayed in historical work on the invention of the Bamum script. It comes from the tradition surrounding the founding dream of the script (see legends below) rather than from a strictly dated archival document: it should be treated as an attributed saying with reasonable historical plausibility, not as a quote authenticated by an independent primary source.

The account above favors the details most cross-confirmed across independent sources (the detailed, academically sourced English Wikipedia account, checked against museum institutions for the throne episode).

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The origin of the writing project is presented, including by Njoya himself in the accounts he left behind, as born from a prophetic dream: a teacher is said to have appeared to him in his sleep to show him how to draw a hand on a wooden tablet, wash it away, then drink the water used to clean it. This story belongs to the register of a founding dream and the spiritual legitimation of a ruler, a recurring motif in African royal traditions: it should be told as the account Njoya himself passed down about the origin of his invention, not as historical proof of the actual mechanism by which the idea came to him.
