# Ngog Lituba, the Pierced Rock

Founding myth of the Basaa, Elog-Mpoo (Bakoko) and Bati peoples, Nyanon district, Sanaga-Maritime, Cameroon. Version 2, rewritten in depth on 2026-10-15 (the earlier French version is archived in `archives/fr_v1.md`, the research file is `recherche.md`). Quotations from French-language sources are translated here by AFROBACK.

> This story is a legend of oral tradition, or rather a cluster of legends. The griot tells them as legends, and at every step says what researchers have recorded, what historians debate, and what no one can settle.

---

## The Griot's Tale

### I. The Stone with a Mouth

Listen.

In the north of the Sanaga-Maritime, where the great forest of the Littoral begins to open up, where the trees draw back and give way to a savanna of tall grass, there stands a stone. Not a pebble you pick up by the roadside. A mass of rock rising above the grass, grey, bare, motionless for longer than anyone has counted. It lies in the district of Nyanon, at the heart of the land held by the Bati, a few hours' drive from Edéa.

And this stone has a mouth.

An opening runs through it, a passage cut into the rock, a cave that sinks into the belly of the mountain. That is why it is called Ngog Lituba. *Ngog*, in Basaa as in Mpoo, is the rock. *Lituba* is the hole, the piercing. The pierced rock.

But wait. Among our peoples, a name never has only one meaning. Pierre Titi Nwel, a sociologist at the University of Yaoundé and himself a *mbombog*, an initiate of his people's highest knowledge, gave the historian Prosper Molibok another reading: in the Mpoo language, he said, *lituba* points to refuge, to exile. Ngog Lituba would then also be the rock of refuge. The stone you hide in when everything is hunting you. And some initiates prefer yet another name, Ngog Lipondo, the holed rock, to say that no human hand ever dug that opening. Three names for a single stone. Remember them. They already hold the whole story I am about to tell you: an opening, a flight, a mystery.

For out of this mouth of stone, the elders say, came the first ancestors. The Basaa, the Elog-Mpoo whom outsiders call Bakoko, the Bati of the savanna: all of them call themselves children of this rock. *Bon ba Ngog Lituba*, the children of the pierced rock. The sociologist Titi Nwel wrote it as early as 1982: "It is commonplace today to say that all the Basaa trace their origin to the pierced rock called Ngog-Lituba."

A commonplace. But what a place! Think of what that means. Hundreds of thousands of people, scattered today from Edéa to Eséka, from Douala to the banks of the Nkam, from Yaoundé to Paris, all pointing to the same stone and saying: that is where we come from.

Before I tell you what happened inside that cave, I owe you honesty about the stone itself. You will read everywhere that the rock rises to fifteen hundred metres and that the cave opens at eight hundred. I have found no serious study that confirms it, and relief maps at the coordinates that circulate for the site show a country of hills only a few hundred metres high. You will also read that it is an old volcano, or the scar of a meteorite, and that human footprints are pressed into the stone. Those claims come from passionate defenders of the site, not from geologists: no published study has tested them. What we do know is that the ground beneath this part of Cameroon belongs to a very old belt of rock, folded more than five hundred million years ago, and that great masses of hard rock often break through the surface there once erosion has carried the rest away. The rock is still waiting for its geologist. So keep those figures and those volcanoes for what they are: rumours. The true mystery of Ngog Lituba is not its height. It is what people have placed inside it.

So come closer. What follows is the legend. Several legends, in fact, because a rock with a mouth is bound to have more than one voice.

### II. The Blind Children

The first voice I want you to hear is the one gathered by a son of the land, Joseph Mboui. In 1967, at the University of Bordeaux, he defended a thesis with a magnificent title: *Mbog Liaa, the Land of the Cave, or the Social Knowledge of the Basa People*. In it he wrote down a great story of origins as the elders carry it. That text became the reference. Sociologists quoted it, historians too, and a German researcher, Ilsemargret Luttmann, even noticed that it was endlessly reproduced as a historical source. I have not been able to read Mboui's thesis itself, which sleeps in libraries. But two Basaa scholars summarised and quoted it with care, Pierre Titi Nwel in 1982 and Prosper Molibok in 2024, and it is through their voices that his reaches us. Listen.

In the beginning, inside the cave of the rock, there were nine men. Nine men with their nine wives, and their children, one hundred and sixty-eight children in all by Mboui's count. They came out of the cave in order of birth, the firstborn first, then the next, then the next.

At their head stood a man and a woman. His name was Ngog, like the rock. Hers was Ki Wom. Ngog was gifted with a quick intelligence, and the whole group took him for its father.

And here is the detail that chills and illuminates everything else. According to the version Molibok reports from Mboui, everyone who lived around Ngog and Ki Wom was blind.

Blind. They saw nothing of the world around them. They did not know who their real parents were, nor where they came from, nor why they were there. Every day they gathered around Ngog and Ki Wom, who gave them food and drink, and then each went back to his tasks. They had lived like this for years. What kept them in that darkness, the story says, was a boundless respect, and a fear: the fear of seeking to know.

Stop for a moment on that image. A people fed, protected, sheltered in the belly of a stone, but unable to see. A people who have everything except knowledge of themselves.

Then came Mban.

Mban was one of the sons. The story gives him a strange nickname: the man of discord. Why? Because he asked the question no one dared to ask. According to Titi Nwel, Mban showed "an uncommon obstinacy in searching for his origins": he wanted to know the names of his fathers and mothers, and the names of their parents. According to Molibok, he saw so much honour and power gathered in the hands of a single man that his mind was troubled, and he went to Ngog to ask who he, Mban, really was.

Ngog did not answer at once. He sent Mban to call his brothers. When they arrived, he questioned the eldest, whose name was Mbog: where had Mban's idea come from? Mbog replied that he knew nothing about it. So Ngog sent everyone back to his place.

But Mban did not move.

He stood there, before the father of all, holding his question. And it was the woman who understood. Ki Wom took a new calabash, filled it with water, and came to him. She laid her right hand on Mban's head, poured the water over him, and spoke these words, as Titi Nwel quotes them from Mboui:

"Through your endurance, you will remain in your father's place and in mine. It is you who will harvest the work of hope, you will set things right on every side, you will bless someone in the assembly, according to his will and his deeds."

Listen carefully to who is speaking. Not Ngog, the father. Ki Wom, the mother. It is a woman who recognises the first leader, who consecrates him with water, who entrusts him with the word of blessing. Keep that in your heart; we will come back to it when we speak of the women of the Koo.

And see why Mban is chosen. Not because he is the eldest: the eldest is Mbog, and Mbog asked for nothing. Not because he is the strongest. Because he dared to seek. Because he had, as Titi Nwel puts it, "love for ancient things and the desire to reach the social knowledge of the people." The sociologist draws a rule from it: among the Basaa, the staff of command does not necessarily go to the firstborn, nor to chance, but to whoever proves worthy of it before all. That is the first law born in the cave. Power belongs to the one who seeks.

### III. The Three Doors

But Ki Wom's words were only a beginning. For Mban to see, he had to pass through the rock.

So Ngog took him and led him into the depths of Ngog Lituba, from door to door. Mboui's story, as Molibok summarises it, counts three doors. Listen to their names; they are as beautiful as passwords.

The first door is called Ngui. Strength. Standing before it, Ngog spoke to the spirits of the place: "Take back the blessings." And the spirits answered: "Duration, like the cave raised up." Then Mban spoke in his turn: "By the strength of my father, if it is granted that man shall not end, I give thanks." And in that instant, Mban recovered his sight. For the first time, he saw the world around him.

The second door is called U. Night. There it was the spirits of the cave who carried Mban away. The door of night gave him the power to transform all things by transforming himself, even diseases. Do not be surprised that night comes after sight. For the peoples of the forest, night is not merely the absence of day: it is the realm of the invisible, where the things the day does not show are decided. Whoever is to govern people must first know their night.

The third door is called Kel. Day. Ngog led Mban there, and Mban saw three young women, each holding a sheaf of grass. The sheaves rose onto his head, and the young women said to him: "We came to open for you the storehouses of life. It is you who will open, it is you who will close."

And when Mban turned around, Ngog was gone. The protecting father had returned to a secret place, where he dwells with the ancestors.

Strength, night, day. Do you see what this passage tells? It is an initiation. The young man leaves blindness through strength, learns the night, and returns to the day to serve the living. Titi Nwel wrote it plainly: Mban is "the first and the model of the *bambombog*", of all those initiates who, ever since, have carried the burden of knowledge among the Basaa. And notice this detail: the grass laid on Mban's head recalls that one of the great branches of Basaa initiation is called, according to the same Titi Nwel, *mbog mabuy*, the mbog of the tufts of grass. I offer that connection as my own, not as the scholars'. But it is hard not to see it.

There is still more in Mboui's story. Each of the nine men of the cave carries a name that means something. According to the table he drew up, Ngog means duration, eternity. Mbog, the multiplying of people. Njel, the boundaries, the edges of the land. Bias, the one who heals diseases. Buwe, the one who stays awake for the land. The other names speak of endurance, of the one who keeps the words and deeds of people, of the one who runs good or bad errands, of the one who does anything at all. Molibok rightly sees in them the portrait of an entire society: time, number, territory, the healer, the watchman, the initiate and the uninitiated. The cave shelters not only ancestors. It shelters every function a people needs to stand upright.

But a people that stands upright grows. And a people that grows must one day divide. In Mboui's story as Titi Nwel summarises it, Mban governed the whole group until the day his brothers, seeing how many descendants they had, left his domain one after another to settle elsewhere with their children. For, the text says, and this is a sentence you should learn by heart, "one stump cannot command two stumps." These stumps are called *maten* in Basaa. As they grew, they became the clans, each one autonomous, each with its land, all bound by the memory of the same cave.

Then came the time of misfortune. The story speaks of a man named Modè. He broke every law and every taboo, and sowed war across the whole land. Seized by panic, several branches of the lineages crossed the Sanaga towards the east. The evildoer was finally defeated and died in chains. Then a descendant of Mban, named Manal, rose to restore order. With the help of the clan leaders, the *bambombog*, he created the institutions that would govern Basaa country until the colonisers arrived.

This Modè does not appear only in Mboui. Elog-Mpoo informants call him Mode Sop, and the historian Molibok, drawing on the Mpoo scholar Cosme Dikoumé, describes him as a fearsome, bloodthirsty warrior who had placed himself outside the common law to impose his own, and who drove his opponents onto the roads. Two traditions that meet: the memory of a time when the law was broken by a single man, and the people had to flee before rebuilding.

Remember the lesson of this first great story. At Ngog Lituba, order does not fall ready-made from the sky. It is born from a question asked by a blind man, it is consecrated by a woman, it is handed on from door to door, it is shattered by a tyrant, and it is rebuilt by a reformer. It is the story of a law that has to be earned.

### IV. The Other Voices of the Rock

You think you know the story now? Wait. A pierced rock has many mouths, and every clan, every storyteller, every generation has made its voice heard there. Listen to them. It is in their differences that you understand what this place means.

Listen first to old Pierre Nyobe. On 7 March 2018, in Edéa, this seventy-year-old man, a village notable and a farmer, told the story of the rock in Basaa into the microphone of researchers from the University of Yaoundé I. The scholar Divine Che Neba published it in a large university database devoted to the myths of the world. And in his mouth, it is no longer Ngog who lives in the cave. It is God.

In the beginning, says Pierre Nyobe, Nyambe, the living God, created the forces, the spirits, the first human couple and the forest. In that forest he made Ngog Lituba, the pierced rock, near Nyanon, not far from the Sanaga. And Nyambe lived with the people, in a tunnel inside the rock. In those days there was neither sickness nor death. When people grew old, they went to sit under a small tree called Singue for nine days and nine nights, and came out young again. Nyambe had made that tree to show his love for his people.

But people changed. They began to believe they owed everything to their own intelligence. They forgot their creator and turned to the tree instead of to the one who had planted it. So Nyambe summoned them, told them he was leaving since they now followed their own paths, and withdrew into the sky. He declared Ngog Lituba cursed. That day, sickness and death entered the world. And ever since, the storyteller says, at Ngog Lituba joy is always mixed with sorrow.

This story of a God who lived among people and then withdrew is found elsewhere in Basaa memory, under another name for God: Hilolombi. The priest Francesco Amato noted it as early as 1967, and the Basaa theologian Simon-Victor Tonyé, later Archbishop of Yaoundé, took it up in his 1981 thesis: "Long ago, at the beginning of the ages, Hilolombi lived with people and gave them wise counsel." Then man resisted him, disobeyed him, hardened his heart, and Hilolombi vanished, "so that no one knew any longer where exactly he was." Since then, the text says, man has been searching for him everywhere. Hilolombi, according to the historian Molibok, means "the elder of elders." The Basaa also name God Nyambe, Nwet, Batupeg or Bayemlikok, and the Elog-Mpoo call him Nyolè or Ololome.

Do you see? In this version, the rock is not only the cradle of the ancestors. It is the place of paradise lost. The place where humankind lived close to God, and lost him through pride.

There is also a cosmogony older still, reported by the Basaa historian Jean-Marcel Eugène Wognou and taken up by Molibok, which does not even mention the cave but the birth of the world. In the beginning, it says, there was nothingness, drawn as a circle. Inside the circle, a triangle. And from their meeting came something smaller: the child. The great stick, the triangle, the small stick. Man, woman, child. Another version, which Molibok heard from a *mbombog* in Nyanon, places these two forces inside an egg, in the midst of chaos, and says that "order is born of disorder." The world, in this way of thinking, begins on the day a woman's body opens to give life. Measure the greatness of that idea: at the origin of everything there is no war and no king. There is a birth.

Now listen to the voice most widely heard today, the one you may have heard as a child. It speaks neither of God nor of the blind. It speaks of flight.

Long, long ago, the elders say, some ancestors were being hunted. Pursued by an enemy that some tellings describe as men with hidden faces, hooded. Exhausted, they found the opening in the rock and took refuge inside. And during the night, a spider wove its web across the entrance. When the pursuers reached the cave, they saw the web unbroken and told themselves that no one could have passed that way. They turned back. The thread of a spider had saved a people.

This version was written down by an author named Nyoumea in a book published in Douala in 1978, *Ngok-Lituba, peuples et histoires*, which Molibok quotes. It is said to explain why the spider is held to be the protecting animal of this people. And it gives full meaning to the second name of the place: the rock of refuge. Notice one thing, though. This spider does not appear in the great story gathered by Mboui, the one of the nine men and the three doors. Two memories live side by side: one tells of a birth and an initiation, the other of a flight and a rescue. They do not contradict each other. They answer two different questions: where does our law come from, and why are we still here.

Listen, finally, to the genealogies, for that is where the voices argue most. As early as 1950, an author named Louis-Marie Mpouka published in *Les Cahiers d'Outre-Mer* a version in which the ancestor Elolombi leaves the rock at the command of Nyambe, marries Kimaga, and fathers Bipoupoum, who in turn fathers first Mpoo, ancestor of the Bakoko, then Tendje, ancestor of the Basaa. In this version, Mpoo is the eldest. But the genealogy that Molibok calls the most widely shared today tells something else: the ancestor Nnanga, who found refuge at the rock, fathers Ngé; Ngé fathers Mban; and Mban has three sons, Nsaa, ancestor of the Basaa, Nnanga known as Mpoo, ancestor of the Elog-Mpoo, and Nti, ancestor of the Bati. Hence the old name by which these peoples recognised one another as brothers: Mban-Ngé-Nnanga. Wognou, for his part, makes Ngog the real ancestor, at the head of the line.

And each has reasons to be the eldest. Bati notables say they are: they are the only group never to have left the savanna of the rock, and they stayed behind, they say, to guard it. They even have their own name for it, Mben Mben. The great ethnologist Philippe Laburthe-Tolra, on the other hand, linked the Bati more closely to the neighbouring peoples of the Mbam, because of their language. The debate is open.

Even the name of the Basaa has its legend. According to an oral tradition reported by the theologian Antoine Babe in 1977, two brothers, Nsaa and Mpoo, went hunting and killed a snake. When it came to sharing, Nsaa took nearly everything and left only a small piece to his brother. Mpoo, offended, gave him this name: Nsaa, the one who grabs it all. It is a folk etymology, told with a cousin's grin, and I give it to you as such. But it says something true: these peoples know each other well enough to tease each other like brothers.

What should you keep from all these voices? This: there is no single myth of Ngog Lituba. There is a place, and around that place, stories that answer one another. The cave of the blind who learn to see. The paradise where God lived among people. The egg of chaos from which order emerges. The refuge saved by a spider. And genealogies competing for the honour of being the eldest. All of them point to the same stone.

### V. The World the Mbog Held Together

To understand why this rock matters so much, you have to understand the world its children built. For what Mban received in the cave was not a throne. It was knowledge. And that knowledge has a name: the Mbog.

The Mbog is hard to translate. Basaa authors speak of it as the ordered universe and the law that holds it together. The historian Molibok writes that it embraces "grasses, stones, trees, the sky, the earth, the moon, the sun, animals and people." The Basaa thinker Théodore Mayi-Matip, in *L'univers de la parole*, says the Mbog demands "the permanent acquisition of negative and positive knowledge of the universe if one wishes to understand it and to manage creation well under the authority of Hilolombi." Among the Elog-Mpoo, the same initiate is called *mpê-pêê*, and the Mpoo scholar Cosme Dikoumé explained in 1978 that the word comes from *pêê*, the universe: the mpê-pêê is the one who knows the things of the universe, a scholar, a sage who knows the history of his clan and of the neighbouring clans, geography, eloquence, medicine.

Picture a society without a king. No palace, no capital, no sovereign above all the Basaa. Researchers all agree: this people lived in autonomous clans, bound by descent through the father's line, and one did not marry inside one's own clan. The *mbombog* Ngijol Likeng, of the Ndog Tjog clan, explained it to Titi Nwel in 1976: there was no mbombog above the whole Basaa people; the bambombog of a region met in assembly to set common laws. The first European observers spoke of an "acephalous" society, a society without a head. Titi Nwel answered them in 1978, before a gathering of researchers in Yaoundé: every lineage there was governed, and "firmly governed."

But who could become a mbombog? Listen to the portrait a Basaa author drew in 1935, S. P. Njock Bot, quoted by Titi Nwel: "The first quality of such a man is to be just towards everyone. To become a mbombok, one must never steal, pick quarrels, commit crimes or practise witchcraft. Every mbombok must know how to speak well and wisely, and must know all genealogical, legal and religious matters." You recognise Mban, don't you? The one who wants to know, the one who keeps memory, the one who sets things right on every side.

The mbombog was not alone. Around him, other societies of initiates each held a share of the world. Ngijol Likeng summed them up in a sentence I find perfect: "Nge renders justice by putting to death; Um blesses; Mbok sows harmony and concord."

The Ngé was the armed arm. It prepared the defence when an enemy attacked, and it punished those who broke the order from within: sorcerers, thieves, hardened wrongdoers. The *nge-nge* danced at night, in costume, in the village square, to purify it. Some bambombog today put it simply: they were our gendarmes.

The Um is linked to the rainbow. According to Molibok, its name comes from *nyum*, the rainbow, which the Basaa see as a bridge thrown between two banks, between the world above and the world below, an umbilical cord between the visible and the invisible. The *mbombog* Nkoth Bisseck wrote in 1997 that the priests of the Um were "more particularly specialised in shaping and transmitting oral tradition, in social criticism and metaphysical speculation", and that they sang this knowledge. At night, when a village suffered a misfortune or a quarrel, the initiates carried the Um through the houses, and people heard a rumbling, now muffled, now roaring. According to Father Amato, it was meant "to make people feel the invisible presence of God." Think of those evenings around the fire when, it is said, an initiate of the Um gathered his people to hand down history, proverbs and myths. Perhaps that is how, from fire to fire, the story of the rock reached us.

And the women? They had their own society, the Koo, whose name means the snail. Listen to how a descendant of Logbikoy, very close to the rock, tells of its birth. The women watched their husbands leave every night for the sacred grove to decide everyone's affairs, and come back in the morning. One woman said: and what about us? They agreed to meet at midnight at the foot of the great baobab of the village, while the men slept. They talked about their own affairs all night, and the day caught them there. The men, finding them gathered, ceded them an institution of their own. It is a story of tradition, and I give it to you as such. But remember Ki Wom pouring water over Mban's head. In this culture, woman is not outside sacred power. She is one of its doors.

Finally there was the *saï*, which Titi Nwel compares to prayer. When someone was gravely ill, the mbombog of his lineage gathered everyone who lived around him and asked aloud: what has this son done? Let whoever knows his faults, or has reason to hold a grudge against him, say so openly. Each person then confessed the wrong the sick man had done him, or that he had done the sick man: a sheep killed, a palm tree damaged. Then the mbombog plunged his sacred insignia into water and washed the sick man. The *mbombog* Ngimbus Mbondo summed it up: "In the old days there was no church. The mbombok was the priest, and his mass was the saï." And Titi Nwel records the formula by which the mbombog grounded his authority: he showed the insignia and said, I did not steal this, I received it from this man, who received it from that one, and so on back to the founding ancestor, who received it from Hilolomb. Then he closed with these words: "It was so; it is no more." I put an end to the evil.

Do you understand now? The rock is not only the place where the ancestors were born. It is the place where, according to the myth, the chain of transmission began. Every mbombog who says "I received it from..." climbs back, link by link, to Mban before his three doors. According to Molibok's informants, the Basaa received chiefly the Mbog there, the Mpoo the Ngé, the Bati the Ngambi, the art of divination. And the Elog-Mpoo say they owe to the rock the rite that once marked the chests of their newborns, so that they would be recognised wherever they went.

### VI. What the Historians Say

Now leave the storytellers' fire for a moment and listen to those who work with archives and dates. You deserve the whole truth.

What researchers consider probable is this. The ancestors of the Basaa reached this region, on the frontier between savanna and forest, several centuries ago. The researcher Ilsemargret Luttmann writes that their arrival at Ngog Lituba "may be dated around the 15th century." The historian Molibok, relying on traditions and genealogies, says the 14th instead. No archaeological excavation has dated the site itself. And about what came before the rock, Luttmann is frank: almost nothing is known.

What follows is better known. For generations, between the 15th and the 19th centuries, families left the savanna of the rock in waves, without any overall plan. The causes? Growing numbers, quarrels, wars, attacks by groups coming from the north, the memory of a tyrant the legend calls Modè, and later the pull of the coast and the salt trade. Some went east and became the Babimbi. Others crossed the Sanaga, at Kikot, at Mbebe, then towards Edéa. Others still pushed as far as the Nkam and the banks of the Wouri, before the Duala arrived there in their turn. This movement, Luttmann says, produced a people that was dispersed, decentralised, divided into clans. It was German colonisation, at the end of the 19th century, that froze it in place.

And Egypt? You may have heard that the Basaa ancestors came from Egypt, or from the Nile valley. It is an idea that is alive and dear to many. The historian Molibok records phrases heard today: *ri nlol Egypto*, "we come from Egypt", *ri yé mbon ba Egypto*, "we are the children of Egypt". Cameroonian researchers, following the great Senegalese scholar Cheikh Anta Diop, have compared the Mbog to the Maat of the ancient Egyptians, and the mbombog's fly-whisk to the sceptres of the pharaohs. I respect this school of thought: it was born of a real wound, that of an Africa long denied any history at all. But a griot owes you not only pride; he owes you accuracy. No archaeological, linguistic or genetic evidence recognised outside this school shows a migration of the Basaa from Egypt. Basaa is a Bantu language, a cousin of its neighbours' languages. A genetic study published in 2009 links the Basaa and the Bakoko to the Western Bantu. And several historians have criticised such lineages, which opened a fierce debate. Keep Egypt for what it is here: a modern story of identity, and a question still argued over, not an established fact.

So what is the myth for, if it is not a chronicle? Luttmann saw it clearly. First it says: we were here first, we are the people of this land. Then it says: despite the scattering, we are one family, and the genealogies, even somewhat arranged, are there so that no one gets lost. Finally it offers an ideal, a just society free of conflict, which people readily take as a description of the past. Add what Mboui's story teaches each generation: that power is earned by seeking knowledge, that one stump must not command two, that a law broken by one can be rebuilt by all. That is what the rock does: it holds together a people that history has scattered.

### VII. A Cross on the Rock

And the rock itself, what became of it?

When the missionaries arrived at the beginning of the 20th century, they saw the Mbog as an enemy. To be baptised, Molibok reports, people often had to renounce the practices of the initiates. Catechists drawn from the people themselves were placed in the villages to teach contempt for what was called fetishism. The scholar Cosme Dikoumé said it: some societies of initiates that once danced in public became secret because they were hunted down. "It was colonisation that made them so."

Yet the rock stayed alive. According to Dikoumé, quoted by Molibok, the children of the rock had taken to climbing it in pilgrimage at the end of every year since 1949, and initiates swore their oaths there.

Then the Church came to plant its sign on the stone. In the mid-1950s, Bishop Thomas Mongo, appointed auxiliary bishop of Douala in 1955 and bishop of Douala in 1957, had a great cross raised on the summit, then a statue of the Virgin, to make it a Catholic place of pilgrimage. The sources hesitate between 1955 and 1959. His words, published in 1961 and quoted by Molibok, hide nothing of his intention: "We should Christianise the legend that says all the Bassa and Baso came out of this cave, 'the Mother of all'." And he added: "Your ancestors had the Ngé, a cruel initiation that forged men. I am going to confer on you, through confirmation, the strength of the Holy Spirit."

But do not forget the moment. In those years, Basaa country was at war. It was here that the great nationalist Ruben Um Nyobè was born, the *Mpodol*, "the one who carries the word of his people", founder of the UPC. His party was banned in 1955, he went into the maquis in the forest of his homeland, and he was shot dead there on 13 September 1958. The historian Achille Mbembe tells how he left behind notebooks in which he wrote down his dreams. According to an account reported in the press, Archbishop Simon-Victor Tonyé Bakot, himself Basaa, explained that the cross was meant to call torn clans to pray for reconciliation, peace and independence. That is the Church's version. For many guardians of tradition, it was a dispossession.

The wound has not healed. From 2006 onwards, the cause of the rock was carried before the United Nations, in Geneva and New York, so that the sacred character of the site for its peoples would be recognised. In January 2017, according to the French daily *La Croix*, members of the community tore down the Catholic symbols. In February, the bishop of Edéa had them put back under police escort. The *mbombog* Simon Mbog Bassong declared at the time: "Never again will it hold a pilgrimage at Ngog Lituba." A complaint for the desecration of sacred land followed.

And memory keeps rebuilding itself. In the 1990s, intellectuals and initiates revived a shared name, Basaa-Mpoo-Bati, to say once more that the three peoples are kin, and associations sprang up as far as the neighbourhoods of Yaoundé. In 2019, Rémi Sim Miyèmeg published *Sortis de Ngog Lituba* ("Out of Ngog Lituba"), drawing on his grandfather's notes. The rock is not a memory. It is a question still open.

### VIII. You Come From There

So, you who are listening to me.

Perhaps you are Basaa, Mpoo, Bati. Perhaps you were born in Douala, in Edéa, in Yaoundé, in Paris, and you have never seen the savanna of Nyanon. Perhaps you were told these stories were superstitions, or on the contrary that you must believe them word for word. I, the griot, tell you something else.

The rock does not ask you to believe that nine blind men lived in a cave, or that a spider saved your ancestors. It asks you to listen to what your ancestors wanted to tell you by telling it. That the first law was born of a question. That the first leader was chosen because he wanted to know where he came from. That it was a woman who blessed him. That you must pass through strength, night and day before you can serve others. That a people can be scattered without being lost.

Historians argue over the dates. The storytellers do not all describe the same cave. Some see Egypt at the end of the road, others see only the savanna. But all of them point to the same stone.

Do as Mban did. Do not stay sitting in the dark because you are afraid to ask. Ask the names of your fathers and your mothers. Ask where your language comes from. That is how sight is recovered.

You come from there.

## Structured Fact Sheet

### Ngog Lituba, the Pierced Rock

- **Content type**: founding myth of oral tradition, in several versions (legend, not historical fact). The settlement of the ancestors in the area of the rock and their later dispersal are historical; the stories of the cave are legendary.
- **Peoples concerned**: Basaa (Babimbi, Bikok, Likol / No Nlon), Elog-Mpoo (Bakoko, Bisoo), Bati (Kelleng, Nyambat, Ndog Bikim, Mbougué).
- **Era**: pre-colonial (settlement around the 14th-15th century according to traditions and the historians who recorded them, with no archaeological dating; dispersal from the 15th to the 19th century); colonial and contemporary memory.
- **Region**: Central Africa (Cameroon, Littoral Region, Sanaga-Maritime, Nyanon district; Basaa diaspora from Douala to Yaoundé).
- **Theme**: other (origin myth, cosmology, initiatory institutions, identity and sacred heritage).

**Timeline (history and memory, distinct from the myth)**

- Around the 14th-15th century: the ancestors reach the savanna-forest frontier zone around the rock (Luttmann: "around the 15th century"; Molibok: 14th century).
- 15th-19th century: dispersal in waves eastwards (Babimbi), to the Sanaga and Edéa, to the Nkam and the Wouri; migrations halted by German colonisation (late 19th century).
- 1949: start, according to Cosme Dikoumé, of annual traditional end-of-year pilgrimages to the rock.
- 1955-1959: Bishop Thomas Mongo has a cross, then a statue of the Virgin, raised on the summit (1955 according to Molibok citing Mongo, 1959 according to other sources); 1955-1958, the UPC war in Basaa country; Ruben Um Nyobè killed on 13 September 1958.
- 2006: the cause of the site is brought before the UN (Geneva, New York).
- 2017: Catholic symbols torn down (January), reinstalled under police escort (February), complaint for desecration filed by traditional chiefs (September).

**Debates and uncertainties**

- Date of settlement at the rock: 14th or 15th century depending on the author (10th-13th according to a contemporary mbombog); no archaeological dating of the site.
- Egyptian or Nilotic origin: an identity claim and the thesis of a school of African Egyptology (Oum Ndigi, Pegha, Molibok), not supported by linguistics or genetics (Berniell-Lee et al. 2009: Western Bantu origin); a lively debate.
- The name: "pierced rock" (common usage), "rock of refuge" (Titi Nwel's Mpoo reading), or Ngog Lipondo, "holed rock".
- The rescuing spider is absent from the version recorded by Mboui; two memories coexist.
- Precedence: the Bati call themselves the eldest and guardians of the rock; Mpouka's version (1950) makes Mpoo the eldest; Laburthe-Tolra linked the Bati to the peoples of the Mbam.
- Competing genealogies (Nnanga-Ngé-Mban; Ngog at the head according to Wognou; Elolombi-Bipoupoum according to Mpouka).
- Altitude (1,500 m, cave at 800 m) and geology (volcano, meteorite, footprints): not established; no geological study found, and relief data at the published coordinates show no such summit.
- Date of the cross: 1955 or 1959; intention: to Christianise the legend (Mongo's speech) or to call for reconciliation in the midst of the UPC war (Archbishop Tonyé Bakot's version).
- Not consulted (library only): Mboui 1967 and 1971, Nyoumea 1978, Mongo 1961, Wognou, Dikoumé, Mayi-Matip, Tonyé 1981, Ndebi Biya 1987, Sim Miyèmeg 2019, and the Lyon III thesis (1983) *Ngog-Lituba : mythe de l'origine de l'existence de l'homme Basaa du Sud-Cameroun, essai d'une anthropologie philosophique* (author given by WorldCat as Daniel Defoentem Ntem, to be checked).
