# Félix-Roland Moumié

Physician and president of the Union of the Peoples of Cameroon (UPC), a major figure in the struggle for Cameroonian independence, assassinated by thallium poisoning in Geneva in 1960.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

The Cameroon I am telling you about is not yet a free country. It is a trust territory, handed to France by the United Nations after Germany, defeated in the First World War, lost possession of it. Trusteeship, the texts say. In practice, a colonial administration like any other, with its hierarchies, its prohibitions, and that permanent obsession, among the European powers, with one day losing their colonies. It is in this country that, on the first of November 1925, at the Protestant hospital of Njissé, near Foumban, a child is born. He is named Félix-Roland Moumié. His father, Samuel Mekou Moumié, is an evangelist. His mother's name is Suzanne Mvuh. Nothing, at his birth, suggests he will become the man France will one day send a secret agent to strike down with poison, in a fashionable restaurant in Geneva.

His childhood is a string of schools: Bandjo, the Protestant school of Njissé, the public school of Bafoussam, the regional school of Dschang, where he brilliantly obtains his primary school certificate. Notice this already: in the Cameroon of that time, very few African children gain access to education, and fewer still to what comes after. In 1941, he is admitted to the École supérieure Édouard-Renard, in Brazzaville. In 1945, he leaves for Dakar, where he is trained as a physician, in the tracks the colonial administration reserves for a handful of Africans destined to become its auxiliaries: teachers, nurses, African doctors, tasked with keeping the empire running without ever commanding it. It is a clever trap, this kind of training. It is meant to produce loyal servants. Sometimes it produces, instead, men who see from the inside exactly how the machine they are asked to serve works, and who one day decide to dismantle it.

In 1947, Moumié returns home as a colonial physician and joins the hospital of Lolodorf, near Kribi. It is there, in this small southern town, that his life turns: he meets Ruben Um Nyobè. This man will become, a few years later, the most respected face of Cameroonian resistance, before being killed while underground by Franco-Cameroonian forces. But at this moment, in 1947, Um Nyobè is still only a trade unionist and activist, already nourished by the anticolonialist and communist ideas then circulating in French Africa, carried by figures such as Gabriel d'Arboussier and the historian Jean Suret-Canale. On 10 April 1948, in Douala, trade unionists found the Union of the Peoples of Cameroon, the UPC. Um Nyobè takes its helm as early as November of that same year. Moumié, a young physician of twenty-two, joins this fledgling party. He does not know it yet, but he has just chosen the side that will kill him.

His rise is swift. In April 1950, at the Dschang congress, he is elected vice-president of the UPC. That same year, he becomes one of four vice-presidents of an international conference of partisans of peace, one of those pacifist and anticolonialist networks which, in the full swing of the Cold War, link African militants to broader movements, often close to communist circles. In 1952, at the party's second congress, in Éséka, he is elected president of the UPC, in absentia, succeeding Mathias Djoumessi. Understand clearly how the roles were split at that time: Um Nyobè remains the strategist, the orator, the soul of the movement on Cameroonian ground and before the UN. Moumié, for his part, gradually becomes the voice that carries the message beyond the borders.

Then comes May 1955. On the 22nd, the UPC adopts its emblem, a black crab on a red background. A few days later, between the 25th and 30th of May, UPC demonstrations degenerate into bloody repression: colonial forces open fire on unarmed crowds, in Douala, in the New-Bell district and elsewhere. More than a thousand dead in New-Bell alone, according to some estimates, up to five thousand dead if one counts the whole territory in the weeks that follow. The exact figures will remain forever disputed, but the scale of the massacre is not. On 13 July 1955, the UPC is banned. Moumié, a wanted man, crosses the Moungo river to take refuge in neighboring British Cameroons. He leaves everything behind: his hospital, his legal standing in his own country, the possibility of living there in the open. He enters five years of exile that will only end with his death.

In this British Cameroons where he hides, he does not remain idle. He organizes twenty-one underground central committees, co-founds the "One Kamerun" movement, which calls for the reunification of the two Cameroons split apart by colonial borders. France, already, does not let go of him: on 14 August 1956, an assassination attempt organized from the French military base at Koutaba narrowly fails. Hold onto that date: five years before Geneva, they were already trying to kill him. On 30 July 1957, it is the British authorities themselves who arrest him and deport him to Sudan, where he is held for ten days. Chased by some, arrested by others, hunted by a third: this is the daily life of a man whose only crime is to demand, on his own terms rather than those he is willing to be granted, the independence of his country.

In April 1958, in Accra, at the conference of independent African states convened at Kwame Nkrumah's invitation, Moumié forms a friendship with Frantz Fanon, the Martinican psychiatrist who has become a spokesman for the Algerian National Liberation Front. Two men whom everything seems to bring together: the same conviction that colonization is not reformed, it is fought. Then, on 13 September 1958, the news breaks: Ruben Um Nyobè, the man who had drawn him into politics eleven years earlier, is killed by Franco-Cameroonian forces in the Sanaga-Maritime maquis, where he was leading the armed struggle. Moumié becomes Secretary-General of the UPC, the de facto leader of a movement that is banned, decapitated, hunted, which he must now lead from exile, between Nkrumah's Ghana and Ahmed Sékou Touré's Guinea, never able to board a plane that would fly over French territory, for fear of being intercepted.

In 1959, he leads a UPC delegation before the United Nations to demand that free elections precede the proclamation of independence, rather than an independence granted from above, under the control of France and the man it has chosen to lead the future state, Ahmadou Ahidjo. For Moumié, this independence being prepared for the first of January 1960 is an illusion, a sovereignty under surveillance. In 1960, he travels to the newly independent Congo, together with another UPC militant, Osendé Afana, to meet Patrice Lumumba. Two men, two parallel paths, both destined for a violent end within months of each other: Lumumba will be assassinated in January 1961; Moumié will never live to see that day.

Because in October 1960, Moumié is in Geneva. He is there seeking financing, weapons, contacts, making contact with Chinese diplomats, having pamphlets printed for the UPC. It is the patient and dangerous work of a man playing his hand on the chessboard of the Cold War and decolonization at once, far from the microphones, in the back rooms of chancelleries. On 15 October 1960, he dines at the restaurant Le Plat d'Argent, in Geneva's old town, in the company of three other people. One of his dinner companions presents himself as an anticolonialist journalist. His name is William Bechtel. That evening, Moumié drinks three glasses of pastis. One of them, he will say later, tastes foul. The next day, a partial paralysis of his limbs seizes him, along with an intense sensation of cold. No one, at the time, recognizes the signs of thallium poisoning, that heavy metal, colorless and odorless, which the secret services of the era favor precisely because it is almost undetectable.

Hospitalized, Moumié declines for nearly three weeks, an agony that worsens sharply in his final days. He dies on 3 November 1960, at ten minutes past seven in the evening, in a Geneva hospital. The autopsy reveals a massive level of thallium in his blood and urine. The investigation will later find traces of the same poison on William Bechtel's jacket. But justice moves slowly, and above all, it moves alone against the silence of states. Bechtel is only arrested years later, in the mid-1970s, during a routine check, before being extradited to Switzerland. In October 1980, for lack of evidence deemed sufficient, Swiss justice issues a dismissal. Bechtel is released. He will never be convicted.

Who ordered this murder? The archives, the journalistic investigations, and the half-admissions of the actors of the time converge on the same answer: the French secret service, the SDECE, ancestor of today's DGSE, acting most likely with the unofficial consent of the highest French authorities, through Jacques Foccart, the man who, under de Gaulle, directs France's entire African policy from the shadows. Foccart himself, in 1995, questioned about this affair in a series of interviews that would become the book "Foccart parle," never plainly denies French involvement, settling instead for ambiguous answers that many, since then, read as a veiled admission. Some Cameroonian sources put forward a different version, in which Bechtel is said to have acted on the orders of Ahmadou Ahidjo's government, then only just installed at the head of independent Cameroon. This lead is less solidly established than the SDECE and Foccart one in the sources consulted: I flag it for you because it exists within Cameroonian memory of the affair, but it does not, to this day, rest on evidence as thoroughly documented.

It would take until 16 December 1991, thirty-one years after his death, for Cameroon's National Assembly to proclaim Félix-Roland Moumié a national hero. Thirty-one years of official silence, during which the history of the UPC remained largely suppressed in its own country. A street in Montpellier, France, now bears his name, the very country whose services most likely organized his death. And for a long time, a shadow keeps hanging over this family: his wife, Marthe Ekemeyong Moumié, whom he had married in 1950, would be found murdered at her home in Mbalmayo, on 7 January 2009, at the age of seventy-eight. No source establishes a proven link between this later crime and her husband's political commitment fifty years earlier: I do not claim one, I report it, and I leave it to you as well to weigh the burden of a whole life marked by violence.

This is the story of a child from Foumban, son of an evangelist, trained by the colonial system to serve it, who ended up becoming one of its most feared adversaries, to the point that a powerful state judged it simpler to pour poison into his glass than to negotiate with him. Hold onto what he truly accomplished: he needed no legend to become dangerous in the eyes of those who wanted to keep Cameroon under trusteeship. It was enough for him to demand, loudly and before the whole world, that his people choose their own destiny. That alone was enough to get him killed at forty, far from home, in a neutral country that was never supposed to be a battlefield. Remember him the next time someone tells you that independence should be accepted without asking questions. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Félix-Roland Moumié**

- **Era**: colonial / independences (Cameroon under French trusteeship, assassinated the very year of independence, 1925-1960)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Cameroon; exile in British Cameroons, Ghana and Guinea; assassinated in Geneva, Switzerland)
- **Theme**: resistance / politics

**Timeline**

- 1 November 1925: born at the Protestant hospital of Njissé, Foumban region, French Cameroon under trusteeship
- 1947-1948: colonial physician in Lolodorf, meets Ruben Um Nyobè in Kribi and joins the UPC, founded on 10 April 1948 in Douala
- 1952: elected president of the UPC (in absentia) at the Éséka congress
- 25-30 May 1955: bloody repression of UPC demonstrations (Douala, New-Bell district); UPC banned on 13 July 1955; Moumié goes into exile in British Cameroons
- 13 September 1958: death of Ruben Um Nyobè; Moumié becomes Secretary-General of the UPC, leader of the movement in exile (Ghana, Guinea)
- 15 October to 3 November 1960: poisoned with thallium in Geneva by William Bechtel, a presumed SDECE agent; dies on 3 November 1960

**Notable quotes**

No quote from Félix-Roland Moumié has been authenticated by a dated and verifiable primary source in the research conducted for this account (a full speech, a signed written archive, a dated contemporary testimony). His only reported statement with any degree of precision is descriptive and clinical, not political: he is said to have told hospital staff he had drunk three glasses of pastis, one of them "tasting foul," a detail relayed by the Swiss press from the investigation's records, but it does not constitute a quote of historical or symbolic value. No political speech or statement can therefore be presented here as authenticated.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

No distinct legend or oral tradition was identified around Félix-Roland Moumié in the sources consulted, unlike older figures such as Martin Paul Samba. The main zone of uncertainty here is not one of popular myth but of the historical and judicial reconstruction of who ordered the assassination: the best-established version points to the French SDECE and Jacques Foccart, while some Cameroonian sources put forward the involvement of Ahmadou Ahidjo's government, a lead less solidly documented in the sources consulted for this account. Both versions are reported here, with this differentiated level of certainty, rather than arbitrarily settled.
