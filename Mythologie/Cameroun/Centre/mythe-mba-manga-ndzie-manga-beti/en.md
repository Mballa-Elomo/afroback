# Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga, the Two Mother Sisters

A genealogical tradition of the Ewondo, the Bënë and the Manguissa (Beti), Central Cameroon. Version 1, 2026-10-18 (research file in `recherche.md`, in French).

> This story tells a genealogical tradition, not a civil register. The griot says what each source reports, shows where they disagree, and never fills a silence with an invention. The historical facts (a war around 1880, a colonial chieftaincy, a blocked succession) are presented as such.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. The womb and the name

Listen.

Among the Beti, when people want to know who you are, they do not stop at your first name. They want to know which *mvog* you belong to: which house, which people. The old Ewondo dictionary of Abbé Théodore Tsala gives the expression *mod ya mvog*: "a man from our home". To be from our home is to be able to name an ancestor.

Mvog Atangana Mbala. Mvog Tsungi Mbala. Mvog Fouda Mbala. Mvog Belinga. Mvog Manze. Every name is a door, and behind every door stands a long line of fathers.

Fathers, yes. For Beti society is what anthropologists call patrilineal: you belong to your father's lineage, land passes from father to son, a woman marries far from home and her children belong to her husband's clan. In the genealogies people recite, it is men who found, men who cross, men who command. You already know a few of them if you have listened to the earlier stories: Nanga and his children, Nnë Bodo the ferryman of men, Essomba Nag Ba'ana the child spared on the riverbank. I will not tell them a second time. The serpent of the crossing waits for you in *Ngan Medza, the Serpent of the Crossing*; the diviner, the child and the staff wait for you in *Elig-Ekulu and the Staff of Ngat*.

Tonight I want to tell you about something else. Something rare.

Among the Ewondo and their neighbours the Bënë, there is a word passed down that does not go back to a man. It goes back to two women. Two sisters, some say. Two children of the same womb, says the oldest source. Their names were Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga.

Here is what that tradition says, in its simplest form, as a Manguissa scholar, Bernadette Ngono, set it down in writing in 2024: the elders of the Ewondo and the Bënë "call themselves descendants of two Manguissa sisters, Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga. The first is said to be the ancestress of Atangana-Mbala, Tsoungui-Mbala and Fouda Mbala among the Ewondo." As for Ndzie Manga, "she is said to be that of the Bënë." And she adds: "The quarrel between the two sisters is said to explain the endless tensions between the Ewondo and the Bënë."

That is all. A few lines. No scene, no dialogue, no quarrel told word for word. I tell you this at once, because I will not invent what the sources do not say: the quarrel of the two sisters has no developed narrative in the texts I have been able to read. We do not know what they said to each other. We do not know where they lived. We do not even know, as you will see, whether every mouth calls them sisters.

So why tell you so short a story?

Because those few lines are a key. They open three doors at once. The door of kinship: how does a people that counts through its fathers remember its mothers? The door of politics: why have two neighbouring peoples, who speak almost the same language, kept their distance from each other for more than a century? And the door of time: how did one sentence about two women of long ago travel through a war, a colonisation, a chieftaincy invented by the Germans, all the way to a succession that is still blocked today in the heart of Yaoundé?

And there is something more. While searching for this tradition, I went back to a book printed in Hamburg in 1919, written by two Ewondo men. And in that book I found the two names again. Not in a folktale. In the account of a war, and in the cry of a chief calling his warriors to battle.

Come closer. I will show you.

### II. The wife-givers

Let us begin with the Manguissa, since it is among them, say those who tell it, that the tradition places the two sisters.

The Manguissa live north-west of Yaoundé, in the hills of the Lékié, around Sa'a and Ebebda, where the Sanaga receives the Mbam. They are Beti, but they have a feature that Bernadette Ngono, who is one of them, underlines with pride: they have "two languages at [their] disposal", their own and that of their neighbours. She also reports that they once had another name, Wa-Ngidzo, and that the name Manguissa came to them from their Eton neighbours, who mispronounced their own. It is a memory of tradition, and I give it to you as such.

Now these Manguissa have a singular reputation among the Beti. Listen to what Philippe Laburthe-Tolra wrote about them, the anthropologist who spent years at Minlaaba, in his great book *Les Seigneurs de la forêt* (The Lords of the Forest), published in 1981. I quote the sentence as Bernadette Ngono reproduces it:

"There is no doubt that the Mengisa enjoy particular consideration among the southern Bëti, without anyone knowing quite why. They are the Bëti par excellence, perhaps because they have kept their atí language and the old customs, or because they were the first to pass into the forest, or because they appear as wife-givers to the Enoa, the Bënë and the Ewondo; perhaps for other, still older reasons."

Stop at those words: wife-givers.

To understand them you must step into the logic of Beti marriage. A Beti lineage is exogamous: you do not take a wife at home, among the descendants of the same ancestor. You must look for a wife elsewhere, in another lineage, and that lineage, in giving you its daughter, becomes your ally, and your creditor too. For a woman is not received for nothing: a compensation is paid, the bridewealth, and the bond does not end with the payment. The children born of that marriage will belong to their father's lineage, but they will keep forever a maternal uncle, their mother's brother, whom Tsala's dictionary calls *ndom ñań*: *ndom*, a woman's brother, and *ñań*, the mother. The mother's brother. Do not think this bond is a formality. The man who passed on the genealogy of the Ewondo of Ngomedzap to a Yaoundé student in 2014 had learned it while growing up among his maternal uncles. And you will see further on that a woman, in the twentieth century, rose to her father's seat "encouraged and motivated by maternal uncles". The mothers' side is not the silent side.

So understand what it means for a whole people to be "wife-givers". If the Manguissa gave wives to the Ewondo and the Bënë, they stand, collectively, on the side of the maternal uncles. They are the house the mothers came from.

And Bernadette Ngono makes the link herself: in her view, the expression "wife-givers" "is confirmed, moreover, by the fact that the elders of the Ewondo and the Bënë (or Bane) call themselves descendants of two Manguissa sisters". So it is not I who bring the two things together: it is a Manguissa voice, reading Laburthe-Tolra in the light of what the elders say. Laburthe-Tolra himself, in the sentence we have, does not speak of the two sisters. He only says "perhaps". He hesitates. He does not know "quite why". Remember the scholar's caution: it is worth more than a manufactured certainty.

Bernadette Ngono also links the tradition to the order of the migrations. She quotes a geographer, J.-L. Morinière, for whom the entry of the Beti into the forest, at the passage of the Sanaga, stretched from the late eighteenth to the mid-nineteenth century, with "first the Manguissa and the Eton, then the Ewondo and the Bane, and finally the Boulou and the Fang". And she writes: "This closeness in the order of the crossing between Manguissa, Ewondo and Bënë should perhaps be connected with their origins through the two Manguissa sisters, their ancestresses." Another "perhaps". Morinière's dates, by the way, are not those of every historian: Laburthe-Tolra, through his genealogical calculations, had the Bënë cross much earlier, around 1680 to 1685, led by Nnë Bodo. Beti chronologies are chronologies of memory, and memory counts in generations, not in years.

But the logic is sound. In a world where peoples bind themselves through the women they give one another, to say "our ancestresses were two sisters who came from the Manguissa" is to say three things in a single breath. We Ewondo and you Bënë are cousins through our mothers. The Manguissa are the house of our uncles. And what separates us is not a difference of blood: it is a family quarrel.

A quarrel between sisters.

### III. Three mouths, one womb

A griot does not tell a story with a single voice. He listens to all those who spoke before him, and he lets you hear them, even when they disagree. About Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga I found four voices. Listen to them in the order of time.

**The first voice dates from 1919.** It comes from a strange book printed in Hamburg, in Germany: the *Jaunde-Texte*. Between 1911 and 1913, two Ewondo men, Karl Atangana and Paul Messi, wrote down in their own language, in Hamburg, letters, dialogues, customs and historical accounts. A German linguist, Martin Heepe, published them with a German translation. Some of the dialogues were even recorded on gramophone discs. It is the oldest written source by Ewondo themselves about their past.

Text number 19 is titled, in Heepe's translation, "The War of the Jaunde and the Bene": Jaunde was what the Germans called the Ewondo. And it opens like this. I give you Heepe's German translation, rendered in English: "Omba Manga and Ndzie Manga were brothers of the same mother. But they parted. One tried his fortune among the Ewondo, the other among the Bene. Only a few years had passed when these two houses of Manga-Mbolo began a great feud, in the course of which they fought many battles."

Brothers? Wait. Do not decide too quickly.

Look at the Ewondo sentence that Atangana wrote beneath the translation. It says that the two "were of one womb": *abum da*. Tsala's dictionary gives the exact formula: *bíné abum da*, "we are of the same mother", word for word "we are of the same womb". And this formula does not state the sex. It states the mother. The word "brothers" belongs to the German translator, who had to choose a gender because his language forced him to. In Atangana's language, Omba Manga and Ndzie Manga are first of all this: two children who came out of the same womb.

Hold on to that detail, because it changes everything. The oldest source does not contradict the sisters. Nor does it confirm them. It puts at the centre what every other version will put at the centre: a shared mother.

**The second voice dates from 1967.** It comes from a journal called *Songo Beti*, number 12, published in June 1967, written by Ekobena Abé. I was not able to read that journal myself. I know it through a history thesis defended at the University of Yaoundé I in 2022, by Michèle Carmèle Bolo Mbala, which sums it up like this: "the three sisters, respective mothers of the Ewondo, the Bene and the Enoa, split apart over a quarrel, indeed a dispute, about the fertility onion of their father Nnebodo, which they were supposed to share."

Hear how the word moves. Here they are no longer two, but three: a third sister for the Enoa, the cousins who lived near the Bënë and whom Laburthe-Tolra has crossing the Sanaga among the first. Here their father has a name, and what a name: Nnebodo, that is, Nnë Bodo, the ferryman of men, the ancestor the Bënë place at the head of their crossing. And here the quarrel has an object: a fertility onion that the sisters "were supposed to share". I will tell you about that onion in a moment. It deserves our attention.

**The third voice is that of the genealogists.** The Cameroonian scholar Henri Ngoa, author of a thesis on marriage among the Ewondo and of studies on their history, drew up in the 1970s the family tree of the descendants of Essomba Nag Ba'ana. A dissertation from the École normale supérieure in Yaoundé reproduces it. In it, Essomba Nag Ba'ana has two wives. The first is called Mbala Manga, and she is the mother of three sons: Atangana Mbala, Tsungi Mbala and Fouda Mbala. The second is called Mendana Mezoa, mother of Essomba Ndana.

Did you hear that? The three sons of Mbala Manga are exactly the three forebears whom Bernadette Ngono attributes to Mba Manga. Mba, Mbala: the same woman, under two forms of her name. The dissertation's informant hesitated himself and said "Mbala Man(ga)". And Ngono reports a third form, sung among the Mvog Fouda Mballa: Omgba Manga. In Atangana, in 1919: Omba Manga. Four spellings, one ancestress. None of these sources makes that connection for me, but it leaps to the eye.

There is a catch, though: in Ngoa, Mbala Manga is not the mother of "all the Ewondo". She is the wife of an Ewondo ancestor and the mother of three of his sons, and so of three great Ewondo houses among others. The fourth son, Essomba Ndana, has another mother. And the Ewondo count many other clans. The tradition of the sisters therefore widens the circle: out of a wife, it makes the mother of a people. That is what genealogies do: they take the most powerful branch and set it down as the trunk.

And Ndzie Manga, the other one? In Ngoa, as far as I know his work, she does not appear. Another tree, reproduced in Bolo Mbala's thesis from Ngoa and from a biography of Charles Atangana, gives as the eponym of the Ewondo a certain Ewundu Ndzié, son of a Manga. A Ndzie, a Manga, once again. But I can draw nothing certain from it, and I will not: in these trees the same names come back again and again, and a returning name is not a proof.

**The fourth voice is that of Bernadette Ngono, in 2024**, whom you already know: two Manguissa sisters, Mba Manga ancestress of the three Ewondo houses, Ndzie Manga ancestress of the Bënë, and a quarrel that is said to explain the tensions.

So, two brothers, two sisters or three sisters? Daughters of Manga, daughters of Nnë Bodo, or daughters of the Manguissa? I will not decide for you, because no one can. But look at what remains when you lay the four voices one upon the other. A shared womb remains. A parting remains. Two houses remain, or three, who call themselves kin and who quarrel. And there remains, at least for Mba Manga, a woman whose name has survived everything, because men went on carrying it.

### IV. The fertility onion

Let us come back to that onion which three sisters are said to have fought over. It might make you smile. It does not make the elders smile.

In Ewondo, the onion is called *ayan*. And in Beti thought it is not first of all a vegetable. The glossary of Bolo Mbala's thesis, drawn from Tsala's dictionary and from the work of Laburthe-Tolra and Jeanne-Françoise Vincent, defines it thus: "onion (protective or propitiatory)". An object that protects and draws favour. The same thesis describes the fertility rites, the *mebian*, "the best known of which is the fertility onion": herbs whose names evoke growth and prosperity were left to steep in a pot, the yams were sprinkled with the mixture, and the pot was left in the middle of the field. And when men founded the great initiation rite of the So, an onion was planted beside the ritual pot, and some was poured at the foot of the mediating tree and at the foundation of the initiation hut: Laburthe-Tolra reports it in *Initiations et sociétés secrètes au Cameroun*.

Do you see now what the sisters were fighting over? Not a bulb. Fertility itself. The power of a house to multiply, to fill its fields, to have sons and daughters. In a society where a man's greatness was measured by the number of his children and dependants, the power of fertility was the first of treasures.

And here the traditions answer one another without knowing one another.

In the story *Elig-Ekulu and the Staff of Ngat*, I already told you, in a single sentence, a legend reported by Laburthe-Tolra: Mbala Manga, the mother of Essomba Nag Ba'ana's three eldest sons, "is said to have received from her father the talisman of fertility". That is why, say those who tell it, the Ewondo are so numerous. At Ngomedzap, an eighty-year-old elder told a student something else in 2015: it was Atangana Mbala, the eldest son, who inherited from his father "the talisman of fertility", which he calls *ayang abié*. In *ayang* you recognise *ayan*, the onion.

Now set these words side by side. A woman receives fertility from her father. Her eldest son inherits it. Three sisters fight over their father's fertility onion. Three traditions, collected at different times and in different places, which do not quote one another, and which turn around the same object: the power to give birth, handed down by a father to his daughter, then kept, or lost, or disputed. It is I, the griot, who brings them together. I am not telling you that they tell the same story. I am telling you that they think with the same images.

And those images are not innocent. Listen to the anthropologist Marie-Paule Bochet de Thé, author of a thesis on women in Beti society from 1887 to 1966: in these societies, she writes, "women's fertility, in all its forms, appears as the principal instrument" of the group's survival and growth. And she adds at once: "the roles the woman must take on are sharply defined by the male group." The two sentences belong together. The woman carries the strength of the lineage, but it is the men of the lineage who decide how it is used. A tradition that makes two women the mothers of two peoples speaks of the importance of mothers. It also says that their fertility is fought over like an inheritance.

Women, for their part, had their own rites to protect it. The most famous was called the *mevungu*. Laburthe-Tolra studied it at Minlaaba, listening to women and men in the 1960s. It was celebrated, he says, when the village became "hard": when game would no longer be caught, when the fields no longer grew, when women were ill or barren. An old initiate, Michael Mve Meyo, told him in 1967: "In my lifetime, our mothers had a ceremony: mevungu. [...] Only the old mothers knew it." The rite was led by a woman chief, and only women who were already mothers, known for their many births, entered its secret part. The elders compared the mevungu to the snail's shell, which catches water on its back and mud on its belly: whatever happens, you get something from it.

Should the mevungu be linked to the two sisters? I must be honest with you: none of the sources I have read does so. Laburthe-Tolra gives the mevungu no founding story, and he writes that it "was passed on at the chance of alliances". But keep that last phrase. At the chance of alliances: that is, along with the women whom lineages gave one another in marriage. Women's knowledge travelled with them, from one house to another, just as the sisters of the tradition travelled from the Manguissa to the Ewondo and the Bënë. I am not saying that the two sisters brought the mevungu. I am saying that the world in which their story is told is a world where women, when they married, carried with them fertility, its secrets and its quarrels.

### V. When the quarrel becomes war

Now leave the time of the ancestresses. Step into a time that can almost be dated.

Take up text 19 of the *Jaunde-Texte* again, where we left it. After the parting of the two children of the same womb, Atangana tells how "these two houses of Manga-Mbolo" made war on each other, and he comes to the last of those wars, the one he knows best. And this is where the account surprises us: the quarrel, he says, "began at first only among the Bene". Then one of the Bene families, the house of Belinga, called the Ewondo to its aid. And that, he writes, "only increased the differences that existed between the Ewondo and the Bene".

Another text in the same book, a dialogue performed and recorded on disc, gives the scene voices. The Belinga people arrive among the Ewondo to the sound of drums. Their chief, Eban-Akomo, explains the matter: during a feast some young men were drinking palm wine; the people of Owondzuli, another Bene family, came, there was a fight, one of theirs was wounded, and in return a Belinga man was shot and now lies dying. The Belinga are few. They ask the Ewondo for warriors, "because Owona-Metugu has grown too proud".

Owona-Metugu. Here is the adversary: "a great chief and hero of the house of Owondzuli", writes Atangana. He sends word that the whole might of the Bene will come to fight at Mvoleye.

Mvoleye is most likely Mvolyé, today a district of Yaoundé, where the biographers place the birth of Charles Atangana. At the time it was the settlement of one man: Atangana-Esomba. Listen to how his son presents him: "a heroic man and a mighty chief of the house of Ate-menge, a branch of the lineage of the Tsungi-Mbala". Tsungi Mbala. The second son of Mbala Manga. Do you see the thread? The chief who will defend the Ewondo is a descendant of the ancestress. And Atangana adds that "all the Jaunde had chosen him" and had told him to build his village "on the border of the peoples". A guardhouse, on the line where the two houses of the same mother touch.

When the news arrives, Atangana-Esomba claps his hands. He climbs up to the war drum and strikes a single blow, and his son records a detail I find unforgettable: the ivory rings slid down his arms to the ground. The drums fill the country. The next morning the warriors gather in the courtyard, brew the war drink and dance the war dance. Then the chief rises and speaks.

Listen to his first words. They are what I was looking for.

"You, Omba Manga of the east and of the west! When the stranger sets the foot of war here at Mvoleye, let no one fire his gun or throw his spear at once: let each man stay hidden until the enemy has entered my compound in full. As soon as I have fired first, let everyone come to the fight."

Omba Manga. The chief does not call his warriors "Ewondo". He does not call them by a man's name. He calls them by the name of the ancestress. As if, at the moment of greatest danger, the only word able to gather all the fighters, those of the east and those of the west, were the name of the shared mother. Bernadette Ngono had collected this cry in another form, "O'Mba Manga'o, come from the north or from the south", and attributed it to the testimony of Charles Atangana. Here is that harangue, in the 1919 book: the Manguissa tradition and the Ewondo text confirm each other. Only the directions change, east and west in Heepe, north and south in Ngono. Here I follow the oldest source.

The battle comes. Owona-Metugu appears "like lightning" with his troops. The Ewondo do as agreed. The chief fires first, and all the guns speak together, "like dry maize crackling in the fire". And then, Atangana tells us, something happened that I must present to you for what it is, a reported marvel: a palm-kernel mortar and its pestle suddenly came out of the house, as if a man were pushing them. The Bene, seeing this great wonder, scattered and were crushed in the valley of the Mfundi. Owona-Metugu himself escaped alone. So the best-documented war account in this whole story also contains its share of the marvellous. Atangana writes it without comment. I keep it as a battle legend.

Other details smell of lived memory. A Mvele man who fought on the side of the Bene captured Esomba-ngonti, the chief's brother. A friend of the prisoner, Kungu-Ntolo, struck the Mvele in the face with the barrel of his gun, so hard that his teeth flew out. "I myself have seen this man with the split mouth," writes Atangana, in the village of chief Ondobidzogo, near Mvoleye.

And then there is this sentence, which turns a war between houses into a family memory: "On the day of that battle, my mother fled into the bush, pregnant", towards Elumenden. It was during that flight that she gave birth, and the child was given a name meaning "the one who escaped from Owona-Metugu's army". That child was Charles Atangana. You will find his life in the story AFROBACK devotes to him, and I will not tell it here. Remember only this: he was born around 1880, his biographers say, amid the din of war between the two houses of the same mother. His father died a few years later, towards the end of the 1880s, and the Ewondo, the son relates, slaughtered five hundred animals at his funeral.

Atangana ends with a claim. "Since that battle the Ewondo and the Bene have remained bound in great friendship, and still today they are entirely of one mind." Write that sentence in your memory, with its date. It was written in Hamburg around 1912 by a man whom the Germans had made a chief in Yaoundé in 1910, and whom in 1914 they would name paramount chief of the "Jaunde", that is, of the Ewondo and the Bene together. We shall see whether the Bene agreed.

### VI. What the mother's womb does to the father's lineage

Before we go to the Bene, I must show you how a Beti house works from the inside. Otherwise you will not understand why two women can found two peoples in a society that counts through men.

Take a great man of old. He has several wives: among the Beti, polygyny was one of the signs of power, together with wealth, eloquence and the number of one's children. All his sons belong to his lineage, his *mvog*. So far, nothing could be more patrilineal. But look at how these sons are told apart. They are told apart by their mothers.

The Ngomedzap dissertation shows it on the tree of Essomba Nag Ba'ana. Mbala Manga's three sons are called Atangana Mbala, Tsungi Mbala, Fouda Mbala: they bear their mother's name. Mendana Mezoa's son is called Essomba Ndana: he bears his. The informant and the author draw their own explanation from it: the mother's name was given "to identify the half-brothers by the same father", that is, to know which of a father's sons came from the same womb. And the mother's name, turned into a house name, has lasted: still today in Yaoundé people say Mvog Atangana Mbala, Mvog Fouda Mbala, and Mbala Manga's name is spoken every day without a thought. A press article on the origins of Yaoundé's neighbourhoods, to be read with caution, likewise explains the Mvog Ada and the Mvog Betsi by the names of the wives of a descendant of Tsungi Mbala.

Now listen to a story Laburthe-Tolra collected among the Bënë themselves, which a student at the University of Yaoundé II reports with the exact pages of his book. Mbartsogo, ancestor of the Bënë of Nkol-Metet, had eight wives, who gave him fourteen sons. The first wife, Baana Wali, had four; the others, one or two each. When the children quarrelled, Baana's four sons stood together and won. So Mbartsogo charged one of his sons, Essissima, with rallying all the other brothers against them.

Do you see the mechanism? The lineage belongs to the father. But the camps inside the lineage belong to the mothers. Each wife's womb cuts the husband's house into sub-houses, which ally, envy and fight one another. Bolo Mbala, in her thesis, speaks of a "trans-generational cold war" between the descendants of polygynous families. The phrase is hers, not the elders', but it names the thing well.

So now read the tradition of the two sisters again. What the Beti know about every great polygynous house, the tradition says on the scale of two peoples. Ewondo and Bënë are like two sub-houses, born of two related wombs, who know they share the same blood and forgive each other nothing. The sisters' quarrel is the rivalry of co-wives and half-brothers, raised to the size of history. That is my reading, the griot's. No source puts it this way for Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga. But it rests on what those sources say about the Beti house.

And the women themselves, in all this? Do not think they were mere wombs whose names men inherited. Laburthe-Tolra writes that Beti girls were "married very early and scattered far from their family of origin". Their lives were played out between two houses. That is why, he adds, their rites were not organised by lineage, as the boys' were, but through "the relationship of co-wife, or of mother-in-law to daughter-in-law, ties of affinity or of neighbourhood within the villages". And Marie-Paule Bochet de Thé, at the end of her study of the rites of Beti women, finds the right words: "A means of balance between two antagonistic groups, their father's and their husband's, they play the role of mediator, carrying the contradiction within themselves: disorder is created through them, through them order is established, society is accomplished."

Disorder is created through them, through them order is established. Is that not exactly what the tradition of the two sisters says? Through them, two peoples are kin. Through their quarrel, those two peoples are at odds.

She also recalls that there were women of authority among the Beti: the *benya binga*, "the important woman"; the *ekomba*, healer, priestess and diviner; the *nyia-dzal*, "the mother of the village", "respected and consulted by the whole *nda-bot*", the great house. "Men must certainly reckon with such women," she writes.

But I will not tell you a beautiful story by hiding the rest. Bolo Mbala's thesis also quotes, from the interviews with Beti women published in 1976 by the anthropologist Jeanne-Françoise Vincent and from other collections of oral tradition, these words: "Women had no mouth; they were sold, or rather dowered, from childhood, they were subject to all sorts of prohibitions and could not eat every kind of meat!" They spoke of widows who were beaten, of wives put to the poison ordeal when a husband fell ill, of women killed at the death of a head of family. In the war dialogue of the *Jaunde-Texte*, when the Ewondo warriors want to say they are not cowards, they say, in Heepe's translation, that there are no "women" among them. That is the world in which Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga were remembered: a world that honoured the mother and took away the wife's voice, that made a woman the ancestress of a people and made the word for woman an insult.

That is precisely why this tradition is precious. In genealogies where women pass by without leaving a trace, two women's names held firm. They held because sons carried them. They held because a chief cried them out before battle.

### VII. The two houses today

Let us return to Atangana's sentence: since the battle of Mvoleye, the Ewondo and the Bene were supposedly "entirely of one mind".

The Bënë did not see it that way. Bernadette Ngono writes it plainly: they "did not accept Charles Atangana as their chief from the German period onwards, just as today they do not accept being called Ewondo". And the colonial archives have kept their voice: in 1924, Bane people brought a complaint against him before the courts. I do not tell that episode here; it is in the story devoted to Atangana. Remember only that the quarrel of the two houses did not die out with the last battle. It changed weapons.

For what war had not done, the colonial administration did: it put the two peoples under a single chief. The Germans, then the French, created above the Beti paramount chieftaincies that had not existed before them. Laburthe-Tolra, quoted by a researcher at the University of Yaoundé II, notes that the administration "was content to record the overall homogeneity of the culture" and that the formation of chieftaincies in Bënë country "took place without real rigour". And the appointed chief wanted to erase even the differences of speech. A nephew of Charles Atangana, Frédérick Essomba, told Bolo Mbala that his uncle "explicitly asked that there should never again be any question of their difference of language". Linguists, it is true, find only slight differences between Ewondo and Bënë speech: one people says Owona, the other Owono. But a people is not measured by its vowels.

Now you understand the two possible uses of one and the same genealogy. To say "we were born of the same womb" is the word of the chief who wants to gather: since we are one family, let us live under one roof. To say "our ancestresses quarrelled" is the word of those who want to remain themselves: we are kin, yes, but we are two houses. The tradition of the two sisters carries both words at once. Perhaps that is why it has survived.

And here is the finest twist in this story.

After independence, the paramount chieftaincy of the Ewondo and the Bene, the one at Efoulan in Yaoundé, was put to sleep. According to witnesses interviewed in 2018 for a dissertation at the École normale supérieure in Yaoundé, patriarchs of the great Mvog Tsoung Mballa family had never accepted the rise of Atangana, whose origins were modest. Who then came forward to bring it back to life? A woman. Marie-Thérèse Assiga Ahanda, born Atangana in 1941, the chief's daughter. A novelist, a former member of the National Assembly from 1983 to 1988, she had herself enthroned, the same dissertation says, "encouraged and motivated by her maternal uncles". The maternal uncles again: the mothers' side. According to an article in *Le Messager* published at her death and quoted in that dissertation, "the traditionalist old guard could hardly imagine a woman in this role". The government nevertheless recognised her as "queen of the Ewondo and the Bene" in the late 1990s. She died on 1 February 2014.

Since then the seat has been empty. In May 2021 the daily *Cameroon Tribune* ran the headline "Ewondo-Bene chieftaincy: the endless deadlock". The main reason, according to a patriarch quoted by the paper: "persistent disagreements" between two great families, the Mvog Atemengue and the Mvog Tsoung'Mballa.

Stop. Listen to those two names. Mvog Atemengue is the house of Ate Mengue, son of Tsungi Mbala, from which Atangana's father came. Mvog Tsoung'Mballa is the house of Tsungi Mbala himself. Tsungi Mbala, the second son of Mbala Manga. More than a century after the wars of "the two houses of Manga-Mbolo", the quarrel no longer runs only between the children of the two sisters. It runs inside the very descent of Mba Manga. At the time of writing, I have found no trace of a successor named after 2021.

So what does this story leave you?

It leaves you, first, two women's names. In the long lines of fathers that Beti genealogies recite, Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga are exceptions. Do not let them fall back into silence. If you are Ewondo, you may carry Mba Manga's name without knowing it, each time you say Mvog Atangana Mbala, Mvog Tsungi Mbala or Mvog Fouda Mbala. If you are Bënë, the tradition reported by Bernadette Ngono gives you Ndzie Manga as your ancestress, and another tradition, that of the 1967 journal, makes Nnë Bodo the father of the sisters. If you are Manguissa, you are told that yours was the house the mothers came from.

It leaves you, next, a lesson about kinship. Being of the same womb does not protect you from quarrels. The Beti knew it: the bitterest rivalries are born between those who are closest, between half-brothers, between co-wives, between houses of the same mother. But a quarrel does not destroy kinship either. At the moment of greatest danger, a chief at Mvoleye found no stronger word to gather his men than the name of the ancestress.

And it leaves you, finally, a healthy wariness. When someone recites a genealogy to you, ask yourself who is speaking, and to what end. The chief who wants to unite, the people who want to remain themselves, the family that wants the seat: all of them have told the same names, and each has pulled them to its own side. That is not a lie. It is how a living memory works.

And the next time you hear the name of a *mvog* in Yaoundé, remember that behind a man's name there is sometimes the name of a woman whom no one has quite forgotten.

O Mba Manga, of the east and of the west.

You come from there.

## Fact sheet

### Mba Manga and Ndzie Manga, the two mother sisters

- **Type of content**: genealogical tradition (origin myth of lineages, without a developed narrative in the sources). The names and the kinship belong to tradition; the war of Mvoleye (around 1880), the colonial chieftaincy and the blocked succession are historical facts.
- **Peoples concerned**: Ewondo, Bënë (Bane), Manguissa (Mengisa); Enoa in Ekobena Abé's version (1967).
- **Era**: precolonial (tradition); colonial and contemporary (uses of the genealogy)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Central Cameroon: Mfoundi, Lékié, Nyong-et-So'o)
- **Theme**: other (genealogy, kinship and alliance, the place of women, rivalry between neighbouring peoples, political uses of genealogies)

**Timeline (history, distinct from the tradition)**

- Late 17th to 19th century: successive crossings of the Sanaga and settlement of the Manguissa, Eton, Ewondo, Bënë and Enoa (around 1680-1685 for the Bënë according to Laburthe-Tolra's genealogical calculations; late 18th to mid-19th century according to J.-L. Morinière, quoted by B. Ngono).
- Around 1870: clashes between Bënë, Ewondo and Enoa at the confluence of the So and the Nyong (chronology of M. C. Bolo Mbala, 2022).
- Around 1880: war of Mvoleye between Atangana-Esomba (Mvog Atemenge, a branch of the Tsungi Mbala) and Owona-Metugu (Owondzuli, Bënë); birth of Charles Atangana during his mother's flight (*Jaunde-Texte*, text no. 19).
- 1911-1913: Karl Atangana and Paul Messi write the *Jaunde-Texte* in Hamburg (published 1919), the first written record of the names Omba Manga and Ndzie Manga.
- 1914: Charles Atangana paramount chief of the "Jaunde" (Ewondo and Bënë) under the Germans; 1924: complaint by the Bane against him.
- 1990s to 2014: Marie-Thérèse Assiga Ahanda, daughter of Charles Atangana, recognised as "queen of the Ewondo and the Bene"; died 1 February 2014; chieftaincy vacant and deadlocked since (Cameroon Tribune, 2021).

**Notable quotations (verified sources)**

- "Ombamanga und Ndziemanga waren Brüder von derselben Mutter. Aber sie trennten sich. Der eine versuchte sein Glück in Jaunde, der andere in Bene." ("Omba Manga and Ndzie Manga were brothers of the same mother. But they parted. One tried his fortune among the Jaunde, the other among the Bene.") Karl Atangana, *Jaunde-Texte*, text no. 19, trans. M. Heepe, 1919, p. 70. The Ewondo says "of the same womb" (*abum da*), without stating the sex.
- "Ihr Ombamanga vom Osten und vom Westen [...]" ("You, Omba Manga of the east and of the west!"), harangue of Atangana-Esomba before the battle of Mvoleye, *Jaunde-Texte*, text no. 19, p. 71. Bernadette Ngono reports it as "O'Mba Manga'o, venus du nord ou du sud".
- "Les anciens Ewondo et Bënë (ou Bane) se disent eux-mêmes descendants de deux soeurs manguissa, Mba Manga et Ndzie Manga." - "Les Mengisa [...] apparaissent comme donneurs de femmes vis-à-vis des Enoa, des Bënë et des Ewondo." ("The Mengisa [...] appear as wife-givers to the Enoa, the Bënë and the Ewondo.") Philippe Laburthe-Tolra, *Les Seigneurs de la forêt*, 1981 (quoted by B. Ngono).

("A means of balance between two antagonistic groups, their father's and their husband's, they play the role of mediator [...]: disorder is created through them, through them order is established.") **Debates and uncertainties**

- Sex of the two ancestors: "brothers" in the 1919 German translation; the Ewondo says only "of the same womb"; "sisters" in Ngono (2024) and Ekobena Abé (1967).
- Number and father: two Manguissa sisters (Ngono); three sisters, mothers of the Ewondo, the Bënë and the Enoa, daughters of Nnë Bodo (Ekobena Abé); two children of Manga ("houses of Manga-Mbolo", *Jaunde-Texte*).
- Mba Manga = Mbala Manga, wife of Essomba Nag Ba'ana and mother of three sons in Ngoa: a very likely identification (same three sons), made by the griot. In Ngoa she is the mother of three Ewondo houses, not of all the Ewondo.
- Ndzie Manga appears in no tree that was read; the "Ewundu Ndzié, son of Manga" of one Ewondo tree cannot be linked with certainty.
- Object of the quarrel: not described by Ngono; the fertility onion in Ekobena Abé. The link with Mbala Manga's "talisman of fertility" (Laburthe-Tolra) and Atangana Mbala's *ayang abié* is the griot's.
- The harangue: "of the east and of the west" (Heepe, 1919) or "from the north or from the south" (Ngono).
- Link with the mevungu: none in the sources; only a thematic connection (fertility, women's knowledge travelling through alliances).
- *Les Seigneurs de la forêt* could not be consulted: we do not know whether Laburthe-Tolra reports the tradition of the two sisters. To be checked in a library, together with Ngoa (*Abbia* no. 22; 1981), the 1967 *Songo Beti*, Vincent (1976) and the French edition of the *Jaunde-Texte* by Laburthe-Tolra and J. M. Essono (2006).
- Efoulan succession: no information found after 2021.
