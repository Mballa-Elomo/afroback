# Ngan Medza, the Serpent of the Crossing

Founding and migration myth of the Beti peoples (Ewondo, Eton, Manguissa, Bënë, Mvele, Bulu and their kin), Sanaga River, Central Cameroon. Version 2, rewritten in depth on 2026-10-15 (the research file is `recherche.md`).

> This story is a legend from oral tradition. The griot tells it as a legend, and at every step he tells you what historians know, what they argue about, and what no one can settle.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. The river that divides cousins

Listen.

In the heart of Cameroon there is a river that is never silent. It comes down from the highlands of the Adamawa, it crosses the savanna, it enters the forest, it roars through its rapids, it widens, it darkens, and it runs without rest all the way to the ocean. Geographers call it the Sanaga. The elders, in the stories I am about to tell you, give it another name: the Yom.

Stand on its bank one evening, near Batchenga or Ebebda, as the light falls. Watch the brown water slide past, wide as a plain on the move. And ask yourself the question that Beti children have asked for generations, sitting by the fire: how could a whole people, with its old men, its pregnant women, its children on their backs, its baskets, its tools, even its dead, have crossed to the other side of that?

For the Beti say it plainly: we were not born here. Our fathers came from beyond. They crossed the river. And some of them were left behind.

That is the mystery. On both sides of the Sanaga, families who speak almost the same language. Ewondo, Eton and Bënë around Yaoundé, Bulu further south, Fang as far as Gabon and Equatorial Guinea, and, the legend itself tells us, other kin left behind north of the river, towards the Mbam. All of them speak in similar proverbs, with different accents. How did one people end up cut in two by water?

Historians have their answers, and I will give them to you, because you deserve the whole truth. But before the historians there was another answer, older, that grandmothers whispered at nightfall and patriarchs repeated with gravity. That answer has a name. A name spoken softly, because it names a power.

That name is Ngan Medza.

The serpent that carried a people on its back.

But wait. Do not rush to the water's edge too fast. A griot who tells of a crossing without first saying who is crossing tells nothing. Before the serpent, you must know the people. Before the river, you must know the savanna they came from.

### II. The people without a king

Who were these travellers?

Today we call them the Beti. The word itself carries pride. Linguists and historians link it to *ati*, of which *bëti* would be the plural, a term that traditions associate with nobility, with those who do not submit. It is no accident that the great ethnologist Philippe Laburthe-Tolra, who spent years among them at Minlaaba, titled his major work *Les Seigneurs de la forêt*, the Lords of the Forest. Lords, yes. But lords of a very particular kind.

For here is what struck the first observers: this people had no king. No throne, no palace, no capital. Before colonisation there was no sovereign ruler above all the Beti. The chieftaincies we know today were largely created under colonial rule, German and then French. Before them, society held itself upright in another way.

It held together through blood and through speech. The basic unit was the lineage, the *mvog*, "the people of", the descendants of one ancestor. Mvog Manze, mvog Zang, mvog Amugu: every clan name is an ancestor's name, a living memory. These clans were exogamous, meaning that you did not take a wife from within your own clan, and it was through marriages, alliances and exchanges that lineages were bound to one another. At the head of the extended family stood an elder, chosen not only for his age but for his courage, his eloquence, his generosity. A man who knew how to speak, how to give, how to protect.

And prestige? It was not won by seizing a throne but by spectacular generosity. The Beti knew the *bilabi*, great contests of exchange and gift-giving between lineages, where men competed in largesse, where people danced, where a man's greatness was measured by what he was able to give away. Imagine a society in which you become great by giving finer gifts than your rival. Those were the lords of the forest.

Their sky was not empty. The ancient Beti acknowledged a creator God, whom the sources name Zamba, Zama, Ntondobe or Nkombot depending on region and period. They honoured their ancestors without making gods of them. They knew that beside the world we see there is a world we do not see, and that the two touch. Scholars who have studied their thought speak of a two-sided universe, day and night, visible and invisible, closely intertwined. Keep that detail in your heart. You will need it when we reach the water's edge.

And where did this people come from? Their own traditions are clear: from the north. From beyond the Sanaga. From a land of savanna, of tall grass, of hills. The old stories name places such as Nditam and Bokaga, and Laburthe-Tolra noted that these names still match localities in the Mbam division, in savanna country. An Ewondo tradition, reported in a doctoral thesis defended at the University of Yaoundé I, places the ancient home of the Beti north of the Sanaga, "between Ntitan and Ngok Lituba", the rock the Basaa hold sacred.

There are clues in objects, too. Laburthe-Tolra observed that the smelting furnaces of the Ewondo and the Bënë were strikingly similar to those of the Matakam, now called the Mafa, of northern Cameroon: bellows worked by men standing above the hearth, and long clay pipes two metres in length. He also noted the important social role of flutes among the Beti, instruments typical of savanna peoples. As if, in their hands and in their breath, the Beti had carried the memory of the savanna deep into the forest.

And kinship? Here the traditions speak of a common ancestor, Nanga, and his children. Depending on the version, they are Kolo Beti, ancestor of the Ewondo, Eton Beti, Mvele Beti, Mvan Beti, Meka Beti, or else Fang Beti, Bika Beti, Ntumu the youngest, and Bulu, the only daughter. The lists change from one storyteller to the next. And that is normal. Listen to what historians say, among them Alphonse Kisito Bouh Ma Sitna of the University of Yaoundé I: these genealogies are not civil registers. Bantu clans split, merge, migrate, take in strangers. The same clan can bear one name among the Bulu and another among the Fang or the Ewondo. The genealogy of Nanga is a way of saying "we are kin", not a tree you could check branch by branch.

But that kinship, they felt it. And that is what makes the wound of the river so deep.

### III. Why they left

Why does anyone leave the land of their fathers?

Ask the popular stories and the answer comes at once, with a name: Usman dan Fodio. The great Fulani reformer who, at the very beginning of the nineteenth century, launched from Hausaland one of the most powerful holy-war movements in African history. The traditions say that his horsemen, or those of his lieutenants, drove before them the peoples who refused Islam, and that the Beti, fleeing raids and forced conversion, went south. Many storytellers say so. So did writers of the colonial period, such as the ethnologist Idelette Dugast in 1949: according to her, the Ewondo had crossed the Sanaga "about five generations ago, at the time when all the populations north of the Sanaga felt the backlash of the pressure exerted by the Fulbe".

But an honest griot does not stop at the first answer. Listen to what today's historians say.

The Fulani jihad in the Adamawa begins in 1805, when Usman dan Fodio entrusts Modibbo Adama with leading the holy war in those lands. Yet Bouh Ma Sitna, a historian at the University of Yaoundé I, points out two things. First, between 1805 and 1829 the Fulani did not have the ancestors of the Beti as direct neighbours. Second, the holy war reached the southern Adamawa only around 1875. Meanwhile the Fang, cousins of the Beti, are attested with certainty in the forests of Gabon as early as 1842. And above all, Laburthe-Tolra's genealogical calculations place some crossings of the Sanaga much earlier: in his view the Bënë crossed the river around 1680 to 1685, and the Enoa before them. More than a century before dan Fodio.

So what? Did the legend lie? No. It did what memory does: it gathered. The name of dan Fodio, the most spectacular and most recent threat, may have settled on a far older memory, like a new label on an old basket. That is a hypothesis, and I give it to you as one. What is certain is that the savannas north of the Sanaga were shaken for a long time, and by many forces.

There were the Baare-Tchamba, horsemen from the upper Benue on their ponies, driven out by a great drought, who overturned the settlement of the Adamawa between roughly 1750 and 1850, according to the work of the historian Eldridge Mohammadou. There were the Vute, feared on the upper Sanaga, whose pressure is said to have forced the Mvele, the Bënë and the Ewondo southwards. There were the Gbaya, whom Bouh Ma Sitna suspects of pushing other peoples into the forest. There was, later, the call of the coast: from the 1840s, trade with the Atlantic dangled salt, guns and cloth, and the Beti advanced in waves towards the trade routes. And there was what no grand history tells but every family knows: quarrels, disputed inheritances, forbidden loves that forced a couple to flee far from the offended clan.

The geographer Christian Seignobos has warned his colleagues against the temptation to explain everything by a single cause. Remember that. The Beti migration is not one flight, one night, before one enemy. It is a long movement of peoples, in successive waves, over at least a century and a half, between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries. Some cross the river, settle, fight, move on. Others follow, long afterwards.

And within that long movement there are men. Listen to this name; it is precious. According to Laburthe-Tolra's calculations, based on the collective memory of the Bënë, the crossing of that group was led by a man called Matsege, or Owono Kode, who received a nickname: Nnë Bodo. Bouh Ma Sitna translates it as "the guide", or "the ferryman of men".

The ferryman of men. Keep him close. For while history remembers a man who brought his people across, the legend remembers a serpent.

### IV. The night of the Yom

Now come closer to the fire. What follows is no longer the historians' history. It is the legend, as the elders told it and still tell it. I will tell it by weaving together the voices of several storytellers whose words have been recorded and published, and each time I will tell you where each thread comes from.

They had been walking for a long time. Behind them, the savanna, the enemies, the abandoned villages. Ahead of them, the forest growing thicker. And then one day, between the trees, they heard the roar.

The Yom.

Wide. Powerful. No bridge. No ford. Too deep to wade, too vast and too violent for a whole people to swim across with its children and its old. Behind them, perhaps, danger was coming. Before them, the water.

What do you do when you can neither go forward nor go back?

In the version told in Ewondo by Elisabeth Belomo, a catechist and leader of Ekang women, recorded in Yaoundé in 2017 for a university research project, the people did what peoples do on the edge of the impossible: they called out. And something answered. An immense serpent, Ngan Medza, came and lay across the river, from one bank to the other. A living bridge.

But at first no one knew it was a serpent.

Hear the words of the Ewondo patriarch Pierre Mebe, recorded by Jean-Claude Atangana in a book written to pass the customs on to the young: "A miracle had happened when the Beti were crossing the Sanaga. Suddenly, from beneath the water, there rose a great tree trunk that joined the two banks to each other."

A trunk. That is what their eyes saw. An enormous trunk, risen from the water, laid from bank to bank like a gift.

And that gift had a law. In Pierre Mebe's version, it was forbidden to prick the trunk with the point of a spear: you could rest only the rounded butt of the shaft upon it. In Elisabeth Belomo's version, the elders forbade anyone to carry onto the bridge anything sharp or able to wound, and guardians had been appointed to watch over it.

And why did the people not know? Pierre Mebe gives a reason that makes you shiver: "Our custom among the Beti is that when you see a snake in the water, you do not call it a snake but a piece of wood of the water, for fear that people will be frightened." You do not speak the name of the terrible thing, so that fear does not make those who walk on it fall.

So they crossed. Family after family. Children clinging to their mothers' backs. Men holding their spears by the rounded end. Old people guided step by step. For a long time, the storyteller says, the rule was kept.

Then night came.

The light had dropped. To light the way, bamboo torches had been kindled. And here the voices part, as the people are about to part.

According to Pierre Mebe, an "ill-intentioned" man drove the point of his spear into the trunk, "not knowing on what they were crossing". According to Elisabeth Belomo, it was a chief who, carrying a lit bamboo torch, let it fall onto the bridge together with his sword. According to other widely told versions, the torchbearer walked at the rear, and sparks fell on the creature's back.

Fire, or iron. What is certain, in every version, is that the law was broken.

And the trunk revealed itself.

The burn, or the wound, woke the thing beneath their feet. The serpent shook, rolled over, or sank to the bottom of the water. Those on its back fell into the river and were swept away. In Elisabeth Belomo's version, only then did the people understand that they had been walking on a serpent.

And then, the silence of the water.

On the southern bank, those who had had time to cross. On the northern bank, those who had not yet stepped on. Between them, the black river, and no bridge any more. Pierre Mebe says it in one unadorned sentence: "the Beti were divided into two groups: some on this side of the Sanaga, the others on the other."

That, say the elders, is why you have cousins on the other side of the water.

### V. A thousand mouths, a thousand serpents

You think you know the story now? Wait. A living myth never has a single mouth. Every clan, every region, every storyteller carries it in their own way, and it is in those differences that you hear its heart beat most clearly.

Listen first to the Manguissa, one of the Beti peoples of the Sanaga country. Among them, according to what Philippe Laburthe-Tolra reports in his 1985 book *Initiations et sociétés secrètes au Cameroun*, the serpent does not come of its own accord. It is summoned by a man. Their ancestor was called Aso Abanda. Reaching the riverbank as the people fled, he struck the water with his magic staff, the *ngékémbé*. And from the river, the tradition says, he made a rainbow rise. Then the rainbow settled on the water and became a python, the *mvom*, on whose back they crossed.

Measure the beauty of that image. An arc of light coming down from the sky and lying upon the river as it takes on flesh. The researcher Séraphin Guy Balla Ndegue, also drawing on Laburthe-Tolra, confirms it: the Beti speak of the rainbow as a python. In this version, it is not a miracle fallen from above: it is the power of an ancestor that opens the way. The bridge is born of the science of the night, of what the Beti call magic, which the scholar Jean Bernard Evoung Fouda describes as the ancestor's "first recourse" to invisible forces on the banks of the Sanaga.

Now listen across the border, among the Fang of Gabon. In 1962 the historian Hubert Deschamps published a collection of Gabonese oral traditions, naming each informant. And there, a surprise. In Mitzic and in Oyem, the Fang speak mostly of another obstacle: a gigantic tree, an *adzap*, the butter tree, blocking the way at the bottom of a valley. They bored right through it, helped, in some versions, by the Pygmies who walked with them and guided them. The place of that passage is called Odzamboga, and the entry that the scholarly encyclopedia ELLAF devotes to Fang literature explains that this story used to be told by an elder to open an evening of tales, and sung by women in the fields: "When we were coming back from Odzamboga". In the Bwiti cult, it adds, the passage through the hole in the adzap becomes the image of the initiate's spiritual birth.

But the serpent exists among the Fang as well. In the traditions collected by Deschamps, as they have come down to us, the pursued Fang reach the river Mlong, that is, the Nyong. A chief named Osellé Bevouhou "performed a miracle": a great serpent, or something like one, served as a bridge. And that bridge broke as the enemies arrived.

Pause on that reversal. Here the breaking of the bridge is not a curse that separates brothers. It is a deliverance that saves the fugitives. And do you know who the pursuers were, in these Fang stories? The Mvele. That is to say, in other traditions, one of the children of Nanga, a Beti brother. The one who, for the Ewondo storyteller Elisabeth Belomo, guards the bridge becomes, for some Fang, the enemy who makes it break. That is how one myth can turn around depending on the bank from which you look at it.

An informant from Bitam, Obiang Beyem, told the story of Afiri-Moro, or Afiri-Kara, with his six sons, the savanna of the beginnings, the pursuit, and the river crossed on the serpent. The ancestor, he said, died at Odzamboga, asking that his skull be kept to protect his descendants. That detail speaks loudly: among the Fang, families once carried with them on every migration the basket holding the skulls of their ancestors, at the heart of the *byeri* cult. You never migrate alone. You migrate with your dead.

Afiri Kara, precisely. Among the Bulu, the great story of origins bears his name. It was written down in the Bulu language by Ondoua Engutu, in a small book titled *Dulu bon be Afrikara*, printed by the Presbyterian mission at Elat near Ebolowa (records hesitate between 1948 and 1954), then translated into French in 2012 by the scholar Marie-Rose Abomo-Maurin as *Les pérégrinations des descendants d'Afiri Kara*. In it, the Bulu, at war with the *Bivele Bibot*, "the Reds", abandon the Yom to enter the forest. Abomo-Maurin notes that the river described looks more like the Nyong than the Sanaga.

Should you believe it all? No, and the Fang themselves do not agree. An informant from Oyem, Mendame Ndong, rejected the figure of Afiri Kara: "There was no king," he said, and for him it was a story invented by the Bulu. The ethnologist who recorded those words was himself wary of a name that sounded like "Afrika", perhaps coming from the schoolroom. And Laburthe-Tolra wrote it clearly: none of the old Fang traditions speak of crossing the Sanaga. The Fang speak rather of the Ntem, the Dja or the Nyong. Even the river of the myth varies: the Sanaga for most Beti, the Nyong for some Fang, and the word *Yom* itself, according to other authors, might refer to the Lom, a tributary of the Sanaga.

You see? A myth is not a photograph. It is a river too. It changes its bed, it divides, it keeps its source.

### VI. What the serpent says

So what does Ngan Medza mean? Why a serpent, and not a canoe, a bridge of vines, a brilliant chief? Here we must listen to the scholars who lived among the Beti, and beware of answers that come too easily.

Let us begin with the name. The only scholarly explanation I have found comes from Laburthe-Tolra. And it is striking. For him, the expression *ngan medza* does not refer only to the serpent of long ago. It names a power that every powerful man possessed among the ancient Beti. Listen to how two Cameroonian researchers, each on his own, report his words: every Beti worthy of the name was supposed to possess "his own ngan medza", which Laburthe-Tolra translates literally as "ruin for (every) (hostile) charm", in the form of an invisible python, the *mvom*, which protected him and secured his prosperity, while stripping others.

Do you see what that changes? Ngan Medza is not just an animal in a tale. It is the name of a protection. The thing that makes an enemy's spells useless. The thing that carries its owner above the dangerous waters of envy and witchcraft. The serpent of the river and the invisible python of the powerful man bear the same name: whoever crosses on the back of Ngan Medza is a protected man.

And the python, among the Beti, is an animal heavy with meaning. The word *mvom*, Laburthe-Tolra recalls, plays on two senses: "python" and "happiness", "luck". In the great male initiation rite of the So, which Laburthe-Tolra studied at length, it was said that the python swallows everything, and if a candidate died during the ordeals, people said he had been eaten by the python. The serpent is the gateway. You go in a boy, you come out a man, or you do not come out. How can one not think of the crossing? The people enter the river as a band of fugitives and come out, on the far bank, as the people of the forest.

But beware of making Ngan Medza a god or a totem. The researcher Balla Ndegue stressed it: the legend says nothing of kinship between the people and the reptile, nothing of a totemic bond. Ngan Medza is nobody's ancestor. It is a power that carries, and then withdraws.

And why does it withdraw? Because the law was broken. That may be the deepest lesson of the myth. The power that saves is not owed to you. It demands respect. Do not pierce, do not burn, do not wound what carries you. Hold your spear by the rounded end. Do not even name the thing that might frighten you, so that no one falls. A single act of violence, or carelessness, or malice, and the whole people pays: some drowned, the others separated for centuries.

The myth does something else too. It explains a geography. Why are there Beti north and south of the river? Why cousins as far as Gabon? The story turns a slow dispersal, made of wars, flights and trade, into a single tragic night, and so gives distant peoples a common origin and a common wound. It tells the Ewondo of Yaoundé, the Bulu of Ebolowa, the Fang of Oyem: we were on the same road. What history scatters, myth gathers.

And then there is ambivalence. Among the Beti the serpent is not only a saviour. The anthropologist Balla Ndegue studied in Yaoundé what are now called "totem snakes": there the serpent is at once a source of power and a shield against evil for some, a nocturnal dread and a tool of witchcraft for others. The Christian missions, which fought hard against Beti rites and "mysteries" from the arrival of the Pallottine Fathers in 1901, often placed the serpent on the devil's side. If some today are afraid to say the name Ngan Medza, it is also because a century of preaching taught people to fear what the ancestors respected.

And the truth of the waters? Some have tried to explain the miracle: a thesis defended in Yaoundé reports, at second hand, the idea of a night crossing at very low water, attributed to hydrologists. Perhaps. What we know better is that real men guided these crossings. Remember Nnë Bodo, ferryman of men of the Bënë. Perhaps behind every legendary serpent there were ferrymen, canoes, nights of fear and courage, and a people chose to remember them in the highest form it knew: the form of the power that protects.

### VII. The Sanaga today

And now, what remains of Ngan Medza?

A name remains that children still hear. A story remains that is found not only in the mouths of the old: it was read in a school textbook, *Matins d'Afrique*, published by Hatier in 1984, which included "the epic of the crossing of the Sanaga". It has been drawn for children: in 2009 the young Cameroonian artist Olivier Timma turned it into a picture book, *Le Serpent magique*, published in Yaoundé and singled out by the National Library of France. It has been told into researchers' microphones, as Elisabeth Belomo did in 2017.

A festival remains. On the banks of the Sanaga, at Batchenga, a festival bears the serpent's name: the Ngan Medja festival. In July 2023, from the 13th to the 15th, it brought together lectures, debates, exhibitions, traditional dances and meetings between peoples, with the aim, its promoters say, of recalling a shared history and inviting everyone to preserve and value their culture. And according to the national press, the festival carried on into 2026, with an edition devoted to the ancestral knowledge of the Ekang and Beti in the Upper Sanaga.

A bridge remains. A real one, made of concrete. According to the National Library of France's review of Timma's book, the building of a bridge at Ebebda in 1982 once again allowed exchanges between the two banks. Think of that the next time you cross the Sanaga by car. Beneath your wheels, a work of engineers. In your memory, a serpent.

And a question remains, the most important one, which I leave with you.

Perhaps you are Ewondo, Eton, Manguissa, Bënë, Bulu, Mvele, Fang, Ntumu. Perhaps you were born in Yaoundé, Paris, Libreville or Montreal, and no longer speak your grandparents' language. Perhaps you were taught that these stories were superstition, or on the contrary that they had to be believed word for word. I, the griot, tell you something else.

This myth does not ask you to believe that a giant serpent truly carried your ancestors. It asks you to understand what your ancestors wanted to hand down to you by telling it. That your people crossed the uncrossable, in waves, over generations, with no king to command it, guided by ferrymen and by a shared word. That it knew fear, flight and loss, and kept going. That the power that carries a people demands respect, and that a single brutal act can break what thousands have built. That those who stayed on the other side are not strangers but cousins.

Historians argue over the dates, and they are right to. Storytellers do not all tell the same night, and they are right too. But all agree on what matters: there was a river, there was a crossing, there was a price.

So the next time you see the Sanaga shining under the evening sun, do not pass by without looking at it. It is not just water. It is the frontier your fathers crossed. It is the back of a serpent that dived long ago. It is the bond you can mend.

Hold your spear by the rounded end. Do not wound what carries you.

This is where you come from.

## Structured record

### Ngan Medza, the serpent of the Sanaga crossing

- **Content type**: founding and migration myth from oral tradition (a legend, not a historical fact). The Beti migration is historical; the serpent is legendary.
- **Peoples concerned**: Beti (Ewondo, Eton, Manguissa, Bënë, Mvele, Enoa...), Bulu; variants among the Fang (notably the Ntumu) of Gabon. Old Fang traditions do not mention the Sanaga.
- **Era**: pre-colonial (migrations in waves between the 17th and 19th centuries)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Centre and South Cameroon, Sanaga River; variants in Gabon)
- **Theme**: other (migration and founding myth, cosmology, unity of the Beti-Fang peoples)

**Timeline (history of the migrations, distinct from the myth)**

- c. 1665: separation of the Fang from the Bulu-Beti, according to a glottochronological estimate (Bouh Ma Sitna, 2023).
- c. 1680-1685: crossing of the Sanaga by the Bënë, led by Owono Kode known as Nnë Bodo, "the ferryman of men" (Laburthe-Tolra's genealogical calculations); the Enoa are said to have crossed earlier.
- c. 1750: date proposed for the crossing by the Ewondo patriarch Pierre Mebe (oral tradition); 1750-1850, upheavals caused by the Baare-Tchamba horsemen in the Adamawa (Eldridge Mohammadou).
- 1805: Usman dan Fodio entrusts Modibbo Adama with the holy war in the Adamawa; it reaches the southern Adamawa only around 1875 (Bouh Ma Sitna).
- 1840s: Bulu and Beti groups push towards the coast and its trade routes; the Fang are attested in Gabon by 1842.
- 1901: Pallottine missionaries arrive among the Ewondo, opening a century of Christianisation that changes how the serpent is seen.

**Debates and uncertainties**

- Date of the crossing: from c. 1680 (Bënë, Laburthe-Tolra) to the late 18th century (popular versions), with 1750 in between (Pierre Mebe). There were probably several crossings, in waves.
- Cause: dan Fodio's role is chronologically fragile for the early waves; the Baare-Tchamba, the Vute, the Gbaya, coastal trade and internal conflicts are also put forward. No single cause commands agreement.
- The river: the Sanaga in most Beti versions; *Yom* may refer to the Lom, a tributary of the Sanaga; the Nyong in some Fang and Bulu versions.
- The genealogies (Nanga and his children) differ between storytellers and are criticised by historians as a method of reconstruction.
- The Fang: Laburthe-Tolra states that their old traditions do not mention the Sanaga; Thierno Mouctar Bah (thesis, 1985) on the contrary has them coming from its right bank.
- Etymology: only Laburthe-Tolra's translation ("ruin for every hostile charm") is scholarly; the gloss "grace or magic of the twilight" circulating online is unsourced.
- The hypothesis of a crossing at very low water is reported at second hand and unverified.
- Not consulted (library only): Laburthe-Tolra 1981 and 1985, Alexandre 1965 in full, Henri Ngoa 1973 (who recorded the "Ngan Medzaa" myth), Théodore Tsala 1958, Pierre Mviena 1970, Jean-Pierre Ombolo 2000, Karl Atangana's *Jaunde-Texte* (1919), Abomo-Maurin 2013.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The serpent Ngan Medza lying as a bridge across the river, appearing as a tree trunk, summoned by the people, or sent by God depending on the version.
- The Manguissa ancestor Aso Abanda raising from the water a rainbow that becomes a python.
- The law of the point (spear held by the rounded end) and the wounding of the serpent by a spear, a torch or a sword.
- The Fang variant of chief Osellé Bevouhou and the serpent-bridge that breaks as the pursuing Mvele arrive, on the Nyong.
- The bored adzap tree of Odzamboga (Fang) and the story of Afiri Kara (Bulu, Ntumu), sister myths.
- Ngan Medza as the personal protective power (invisible python) of powerful men: an old belief reported by Laburthe-Tolra.
