# Ruben Um Nyobè (the Mpodol)

Founder and secretary general of the Union of the Populations of Cameroon (UPC), central figure in the struggle for Cameroon's independence and reunification, killed by the French army on 13 September 1958.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

On 10 April 1913, in a country still called Kamerun, one that had just changed colonial masters, Germany having ceded its place to France and Britain at the end of the First World War, a child is born in the village of Song Mpeck, not far from Boumnyébel, in Bassa country, a few hours' walk from Éséka. His parents, Nyobé Nsounga and Ngo Um Nonos, are peasants. Nothing at his birth foretells that one day an entire people will call him Mpodol, the one who carries the voice of his own.

He grows up in the schools of the Presbyterian mission, learns to read, write, and count in the colonizer's language. It takes him time to earn his baccalaureate, at twenty-six, in Édéa, while already working as a clerk in the courts. Picture that position: a young Bassa man sitting every day at the very heart of the colonial judicial machine, watching the files pass by, the sentences, the injustices inflicted on his brothers by a law that was never cut for them. It is there, in those courthouse corridors, far more than in any book, that Um Nyobè learns what it truly means to be colonized.

In the late 1930s, he joins the French Cameroonian Youth movement, then, after the war, a Marxist study circle meeting in Yaoundé. On 18 October 1945, he becomes deputy secretary general of the Confederated Trade Union of Cameroon, then, in 1947, its secretary general. In September 1946, he crosses the continent to Bamako to represent Cameroonian workers at the first congress of the African Democratic Rally. There he discovers what many discover in that same moment: that colonization is not some fate unique to Cameroon, but a system weighing, in the same way, on all of Africa.

I must tell you something here, a truth too often left out: Um Nyobè did not found his first party alone, nor did he succeed on the first try. On 5 April 1947, he takes part in creating the Rassemblement camerounais, a first attempt to unite Cameroonians under a shared charter, demanding an end to the trusteeship and the reunification of the country. Barely two months later, the French administration dissolves the party and forcibly transfers Um Nyobè to Ngaoundéré, hundreds of kilometers from home. This first failure does not discourage him. A year later, in a bar in Douala-Bassa called Chez Sierra, on the night of 10 April 1948, a group of Cameroonian trade unionists founds a new party: the Union of the Populations of Cameroon. Um Nyobè is not even present that night. But in November 1948, after the withdrawal of another leader, Léopold Moumé Etia, it is Um Nyobè who is brought to the head of the party as secretary general. True history is not always that of the visionary founder present on day one. Sometimes it is the history of the man people go looking for because they know he will hold the helm.

And hold it he does. Um Nyobè refuses tribal retreat, refuses religious escalation, prefers the weapons of peaceful struggle: boycott, strike, demonstration, patient and methodical grassroots organizing, in the villages as much as in the unions. He becomes a feared orator, capable of rallying a crowd of Bassa peasants as easily as a room of diplomats in New York. That is why they call him Mpodol.

Because it is indeed in New York, before the United Nations, that Um Nyobè carries the fight to its highest stage. In December 1952, then again in 1953 and 1954, he appears before the Fourth Committee of the General Assembly, responsible for trust territories. On 17 December 1952, in a sparsely filled hall, he delivers a speech that would remain a founding text of Cameroonian nationalism. In it he demands three precise things: revision of the trusteeship agreements signed without consulting the populations concerned, the immediate reunification of French Kamerun and British Kamerun, separated by the colonial powers, and a clear timeline toward independence, what he himself calls a ten-year "school program," under UN supervision, to prepare Cameroonian leaders to govern their own country. Picture this man, come from a village in the Bassa forest, standing before representatives of the entire world, saying out loud what no colonial government wants to hear: that the time of trusteeship must have an end, and that this end must be set now, not some vague day in a future endlessly pushed back.

But speaking at the UN is not enough to bend a colonial empire. In May 1955, riots break out in Douala and several other towns, a crackdown officially counted at twenty-five deaths, a toll many historians today consider badly understated. On 13 July 1955, a decree from French High Commissioner Roland Pré simply dissolves the UPC and all its sister organizations, the Democratic Youth of Cameroon and the Democratic Union of Cameroonian Women. The party's leaders, Ernest Ouandié, Félix-Roland Moumié, and others, go into exile, to Kumba in British Cameroon, then to Cairo, Conakry, Accra, all the way to Beijing.

Um Nyobè chooses a different path. He does not flee the country. He goes into the forest, into the Bassa maquis, his own homeland, the very place where he was born. For three years, underground, he holds on. He builds a parallel administration: birth certificates, marriage certificates, identity cards issued by a state that as yet exists only in the hope of its militants. On 2 December 1956, he sets up a military structure, the National Organization Committee, entrusted to Isaac Nyobè Pandjock. In June 1956 he also attempts a parallel legal path with Paul Soppo Priso, the National Union Current, never fully giving up hope of a political solution. Picture what such a life demands: sleeping in the forest, changing shelter every night, knowing French soldiers are hunting you with resources you do not have, and continuing anyway to write, to organize, to believe that a free country is still possible.

Because on the other side, France is waging what it will long call, wrongly, a simple law-and-order operation, and what historians today call a war, a real war of decolonization, with its torture, its forced displacement of entire populations, its executions without trial. On 31 December 1956, at Ekité, operations under French command claim many civilian victims. Declassified British archives will later put forward a figure on the order of seventy-six thousand deaths between 1954 and 1964 for the whole conflict, a figure other historians consider still below the reality. To this day, there is no final, universally agreed count. What is beyond doubt, however, is the scale and brutality of this repression, long disguised as mere "pacification."

On 13 September 1958, after months of tracking led by Captain Agostini and inspector Georges Conan, French troops finally locate Um Nyobè near Boumnyébel, at a place called Libog Li Ngoi. He is forty-five years old. He is shot dead on the spot. What follows goes beyond the killing itself: his body is dragged through the mud to the neighboring village of Liyong, disfigured to the point of being unrecognizable, then photographed by the French army, which circulates the images to break the morale of independence fighters and discourage anyone who might think of taking his place. On 15 September, he is buried in anonymity, without ceremony, his body sealed in a block of concrete, with no epitaph. A Cameroonian historian, Achille Mbembe, will later say that this act aimed precisely at preventing any identification and at "uprooting the very idea of independence." That day, they did not merely kill a man: they sought to kill what he represented.

It was not enough. Two years later, on 3 November 1960, Félix Moumié, who had succeeded Um Nyobè at the head of the UPC, dies in his turn in Geneva, poisoned with thallium by a French secret service agent, William Bechtel, who would never truly be convicted. The UPC's struggle would continue in the Cameroonian maquis until the early 1970s.

And yet, for more than thirty years, inside Cameroon itself, speaking the name of Ruben Um Nyobè was forbidden. France long denied any involvement: as late as 2009, a French prime minister would still state he "absolutely denied" any participation by the French army in these assassinations. It was only in 1991 that things began to change: on 27 June, the Cameroonian National Assembly proclaimed him a national hero, and on 16 December, law no. 91/022 officially lifted the ban on his memory, recognizing that he had "worked for the birth of national sentiment, independence, or the building of the country." In 2007, a six-meter statue, set on a five-meter base, twenty tons of concrete in all, was unveiled in Éséka in his honor, though Cameroonian authorities were notably discreet that day.

It would take until 2025 for France itself to officially acknowledge its responsibility. A joint Franco-Cameroonian commission of historians, set up in 2022 during a visit by Emmanuel Macron to Yaoundé and chaired by researcher Karine Ramondy, delivered a thousand-page report on the abuses committed between 1945 and 1971, handed to the French president on 21 January 2025 and to Cameroonian president Paul Biya on 28 January. On 30 July 2025, in a letter addressed to Paul Biya, Emmanuel Macron acknowledged France's responsibility in the deaths of Ruben Um Nyobè and other independence leaders killed between 1958 and 1960 during operations conducted under French command. Sixty-seven years after his death in the forest of Boumnyébel, a French state finally said, in black and white, what the Cameroonian people had always known.

That was the life of the Mpodol: a peasant's son turned court clerk, then trade unionist, then tribune before the United Nations, then underground fighter in the forest of his childhood, never a perfect man, certainly no saint without doubt or failure, but a man who chose, again and again, to carry the voice of his people to the very end, even when that voice would cost him his life. If you come from Cameroon, or from anywhere else in Africa, remember this name that they tried to silence for thirty years. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Ruben Um Nyobè (nicknamed Mpodol)**

- **Era**: colonial (Cameroon under French trusteeship, 1913-1958)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Cameroon, Bassa country, Éséka and Boumnyébel region)
- **Theme**: resistance / politics

**Timeline**

- 10 April 1913: born in Song Mpeck, near Boumnyébel and Éséka, Bassa country, son of peasants Nyobé Nsounga and Ngo Um Nonos
- 1947: secretary general of the Confederated Trade Union of Cameroon; takes part in April in founding the Rassemblement camerounais, dissolved by the French administration two months later
- November 1948: becomes secretary general of the Union of the Populations of Cameroon (UPC), founded on 10 April 1948 in Douala without him, after the withdrawal of Léopold Moumé Etia
- 17 December 1952 (then again in 1953 and 1954): speech before the Fourth Committee of the UN General Assembly, demanding revision of the trusteeship agreements, the reunification of Cameroon, and a ten-year timeline toward independence
- 13 July 1955: decree by High Commissioner Roland Pré dissolving the UPC after the riots of May 1955; the party's leaders go into exile, Um Nyobè goes underground inside Cameroon
- 13 September 1958: killed by the French army near Boumnyébel (at a place called Libog Li Ngoi), aged 45, after a manhunt led by Captain Agostini and inspector Georges Conan; his body is mutilated and photographed by the army before being buried anonymously on 15 September in a block of concrete
- 27 June, then 16 December 1991: proclaimed national hero by the Cameroonian National Assembly, then rehabilitated by law no. 91/022, lifting the ban on mentioning his name
- 30 July 2025: France, through a letter from President Emmanuel Macron to Paul Biya, officially acknowledges its responsibility in the death of Um Nyobè and other Cameroonian independence leaders

**Notable quotes**

- "We demand the immediate unification of our country and the setting of a deadline for independence," and the expression of a ten-year "school program" under UN supervision: statements made during his speech of 17 December 1952 before the UN's Fourth Committee, whose written text was published in 1984 in *Le Problème national camerounais*. Um Nyobè could not read his full speech for lack of time, but both a recording and a written version exist, which makes this quote solidly attested.
- No other quote attributed to Um Nyobè in the popular press (statements reportedly made at his arrest or during the manhunt) could be traced to a dated primary source: only the UN speech quote above is treated here as authenticated.

**Associated legends (if applicable)**

No distinct popular legend (a supernatural feat, an embellished oral tradition) was identified in the sources consulted around the life or death of Ruben Um Nyobè. His memory was built not around a marvelous tale, but around a silence imposed for more than thirty years, followed by a belated official acknowledgment, which is itself a documented historical fact, not a legend. The reader should therefore expect no mythologized feat here: the story's power lies in the facts themselves.
