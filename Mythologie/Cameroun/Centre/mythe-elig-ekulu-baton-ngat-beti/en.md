# Elig-Ekulu and the Staff of Ngat

Two sequels to the crossing of the Sanaga in Beti tradition: the spared child who would become the ancestor of the Ewondo, and the latecomers who found a chief's staff. Central Cameroon. Version 1, 2026-10-16 (research file in `recherche.md`, in French). This story follows *Ngan Medza, the Serpent of the Crossing* (`../mythe-ngan-medza-beti/en.md`) and does not retell the crossing itself.

> This story brings together two oral traditions. The griot tells them as traditions, and at every step he says where each word comes from, what scholars say about it, and what no one can settle.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. On the other bank

Listen.

You already know the night of the Yom. If you do not, go first and sit by the fire where they tell of Ngan Medza: the serpent that lay across the river, the living bridge people took for a tree trunk, the ban on spear points, the torch or the spear that wounded the beast, and the people cut in two. I will not tell that night again. It already has its griot.

Tonight, I tell you what comes after.

For a crossing myth never stops at the water's edge. Whoever has crossed the river must still learn whom to obey and whom to command. And those who arrived when the bridge had already sunk have a story too. Beside the great tale of the serpent, Beti tradition keeps smaller tales, like seeds fallen from the great tree. Two of them will keep us company tonight.

The first comes from the Ewondo and their neighbours. It speaks of a place on the banks of the Sanaga that bears a name, Elig-Ekulu. Of an ancestor who presided over the crossing, Tulasa. Of a diviner who was also a slave, Ekulu Ngonda. And of a child the diviner wanted drowned, because he had seen in him a future master of the land. The child's name was Essomba Nak. The Ewondo hold him to be their ancestor.

The second comes from a village in the Awae country, some eighty kilometres from Yaoundé, among the Mvele. It speaks of a group left behind, led by a man named Manga, of a forest that had to be walked around for seven days, and of a staff planted in the earth that no one could pull out. No one, except a young man.

Two tales, two places, two cousin peoples. And yet they ask the same question, the oldest question of human societies: who has the right to command, and why?

Among the Beti, the question burns. The Beti of old had no king: there were lineages, the *mvog*, "the people of" such and such an ancestor, bound by blood and by marriage, and elders respected for their speech and their generosity. So when a tale from such a people says of a child that he was "destined one day to command the whole land", or of a young man that he became chief because a staff gave way in his hand, we must lend an ear. These tales do not speak only of the past. They speak of power.

Where do these words come from? The first tale was collected by the ethnologist Philippe Laburthe-Tolra and published in *Les Seigneurs de la forêt* (The Lords of the Forest, 1981), a book I could only read through two works from the University of Yaoundé I that summarise it, page references included. The second was told in Ewondo in 2019 by an old man of ninety-two and published by two researchers from the same university.

Those are our two threads. Let us pull the first.

### II. The place that bears a name

Before the river, there was grass.

According to the synthesis Laburthe-Tolra drew from his many informants, as summarised by the anthropologist Jean Emile Eboé Onana Elouna in his 2022 thesis, the Beti of old lived on the right bank of the Sanaga, in a region every tradition places "not far" from the confluence of the Mbam. A country of wide grasslands, without forest, where people slept under shelters of branches, the *bibem*. Before them, the river. They called it Yom, or Ossananga. And one of the meanings of the word *Jom*, the same summary notes, is "immensity".

Immensity. That is what the Sanaga was to those people of grass and hills.

Why did they want to cross it? Laburthe-Tolra's informants give ten reasons. The people were growing numerous. Brothers quarrelled. Restless neighbours pressed on them. The "Foulbé", whom some called Sarki, wanted to enslave them. And the researcher Bernard Delpech, quoted in the same thesis, notes that the Eton and the Manguissa seem to have crossed "at different points", around 1800 in his view, driven in particular by the Vouté, who were feared as slave hunters. There was not one crossing, but many, in many places.

And the way across? Here too the voices multiply. Some pray with perseverance. Others turn to magic: among the Manguissa, the ancestor Aso Abanda strikes the water with a stem of *kékémbé* and raises the rainbow that becomes a serpent, as I told you in the tale of Ngan Medza. In almost every version, says the synthesis, "a wonder occurs", which the Beti call *asimba*: a kind of tree trunk, *nkog*, appears across the river. Only one version, an Eton one, presents the thing at once as an animal, speaking of its head, and has all the Eton cross "on the backs of two horned beasts". Two horned beasts instead of a serpent: a rarity, which I hand you the way one shows a strange stone found by the roadside.

Among the Sanaga people of the Ntui area, on the north bank, they say the serpent carried "almost all the peoples living along the Sanaga". Among the Batschenga, online notices mention a floating trunk that turned into a serpent, after a monograph I was unable to read. The serpent bridge does not belong to the Beti alone. It belongs to the river.

But here is the point that matters to us. Who led the crossing?

The traditions, still according to that synthesis, do not agree. "According to some, the discoverer of the passage is the same man, the Chief already recognised or on the way to being so, who will carry the crossing through. According to others, they are different men." For all the Ewondo and Bënë informants, the one who counts is Nnë Bodo, Owono Kode, the "ferryman of men" I spoke of in the tale of Ngan Medza, and whom the Manguissa and the Eton, for their part, do not know. And one informant, a certain Mr Onana, believed the leader was at first a young man whose exceptional qualities were revealed during the crossing. Keep that young man in a corner of your memory. We will meet him again, in another form, in the forest of Ngat.

For there is yet another way of telling the passage. A way in which there is hardly any wonder left.

In this version, the crossing is organised "under the presidency of Tulasa", whom others call Tulesa or Tolasa, the ancestor of the Etudi. It is not a serpent that carries the people. It is rafts. The summary of the synthesis notes it in brackets, like a scholar marking a detail that strikes him: "no direct question of the magic tree trunk". Rafts, built and steered by men. And the one who "carries out the operation", the one who takes the people across, is Tulasa's slave-diviner. His name is Ekulu Ngonda.

And the place of the passage has a name. It is called Elig-Ekulu, or Elig Nkulu. And it is identified, says the synthesis, with the falls that maps today call the Nachtigal Falls, on the Sanaga, north-east of Yaoundé.

Pause on that name, Elig-Ekulu. In the great Ewondo-French dictionary of Father Théodore Tsala, published in 1955, the word *elig* means the site of a village or a house that has vanished, and more broadly inheritance, what the dead leave to the living: *elig nda*, the old site of a house. So Elig-Ekulu could be understood as "the old place of Ekulu", the place that keeps the diviner's name. Careful: it is I, the griot, who bring these two words together. None of the sources I have read gives this explanation, and the variant Elig Nkulu is enough to make it uncertain. I offer it as a fireside guess, not as science.

But if it were right, measure what it would say. That the people's memory did not give the crossing place the name of the chief who presided, nor that of the ancestor who was saved, but the name of the slave who took everyone across.

### III. The diviner and the child

Now come closer. Here is the tale itself. It fits in a few words, and I will not stretch it with details no one has spoken. A short legend does not need sleeves sewn onto it.

On the bank of the Yom, at the falls, the crossing is being prepared. Tulasa, the ancestor of the Etudi, presides over it. His slave, the diviner Ekulu Ngonda, carries it out: he takes the people across on rafts.

Among those who must cross, there is a child. His name is Essomba Nak. The Ewondo, later, will say he is their ancestor.

And the diviner foretells. This child, if he lives, is destined one day to command the whole land. So Ekulu Ngonda advises his master to use the crossing to drown the child.

Tulasa refuses.

The child crosses the river. The child lives. And from him, say the Ewondo traditions, will come the lineages that still people the hills of Yaoundé and the forest as far as Ngomedzap.

That is all. That is the legend as Laburthe-Tolra reported it, and as two works from the University of Yaoundé I summarise it. The first, Onana Elouna's thesis, says that Ekulu Ngonda advises his master "(who will refuse) to drown on this occasion Essomba Nak, ancestor of the Ewondo, otherwise destined one day to command the whole land". The second, the history thesis Jean Emmanuel Dzomo Oyie devoted in 2015 to the town of Ngomedzap, refers to page 104 of *Les Seigneurs de la forêt* and puts it differently, but with the same heart: Laburthe-Tolra "presents the Etudi ancestor, named 'Tulasa' or 'Tolasa', as the one who saved the life of the Ewondo ancestor", for at the crossing his slave-diviner Ekulu Ngonda "is said to have predicted that Essomba Nag would be the Ewondo ancestor, and destined to command the whole land".

Notice the child's name: Essomba Nak in one, Essomba Nag in the other, and elsewhere Essomba Nag Ba'ana, or Essomba-Na-Bana. The pens hesitate; the person is the same.

And notice above all everything the legend does not say. It does not give the child's age, nor who his parents were that day. It does not say how the diviner saw, nor what Tulasa answered, nor why he refused. It does not say what became of Ekulu Ngonda. I respect that silence. A griot who fills a legend's silences with his own inventions betrays those who entrusted it to him.

But I can help you hear what those words carried. For that, you must know two figures of the old Beti society: the slave and the diviner.

The slave first. Ewondo society before colonisation was not a society of equals. The history doctorate Michèle Carmèle Bolo Mbala defended at the University of Yaoundé I in 2022 describes its tiers. At the top, the *nkukuma*, a word dictionaries translate both as "chief" and as "rich man": the man with wives, children, goods, and dependants around him. Among those dependants were clients, *mintobo*, who had come of their own accord to place themselves under his protection. War captives, *minkom*. And domestic slaves, the *belo*, singular *olo'o*. Their lot, the thesis says, was precarious and sometimes terrible: some were marked in their flesh, and some had to follow the dead *nkukuma* into his grave. But the same thesis also notes that a slave valued by his master could escape that precarity and "merge into the host family". I hide none of this from you: that world knew servitude, and the legend names it plainly. Ekulu Ngonda is a slave.

The diviner next. Among the Ewondo of old, the same thesis recalls, nothing important was begun without consulting the oracle. The most famous oracle bore the name of the mygale spider, *ngam*, the great hairy spider that lives at the bottom of a hole; and I have told you elsewhere, in another AFROBACK tale, how the spider became a diviner. But there were many other oracles: of the spear, of the horn, of cowries, of the mirror, of the egg, of the fly-whisk. The patriarch, the *zomeloa*, was the natural one to consult them. Diviners were men of knowledge, and memory keeps some of their names: a certain Otabetene, diviner among the Mvog Atemengue around 1900.

So look at the scene again. The lowest man in society, the slave, is also the one who sees furthest. He takes a whole people across on his rafts, and he sees a child's future. He has the knowledge, not the right to decide. He advises, and the master settles it.

And the master settles it for life.

Why did the diviner want the child drowned? The legend does not say, and I will not say it in its place. Perhaps to keep the land for his master's line. Perhaps for a reason that has been lost. What the legend does say is the essential: a wise man saw a danger in a child, a chief refused to kill that child, and the danger became a people.

### IV. What a spared child says

A people, the Beti say, is first of all a genealogy you know how to recite.

So listen to the one that springs from the spared child. The Cameroonian historian Henri Ngoa, who worked on the history of the Ewondo in the 1970s, drew up its tree, and the Ngomedzap thesis reproduces it. Essomba Nag Ba'ana had two wives, Mbala Manga and Mendana Mezoa. From the first were born three sons: Atangana Mbala, the eldest, Tsungui Mbala and Fouda Mbala. From the second, a fourth: Essomba Ndana. The first three bear their mother's name, Mbala; the last bears his own mother's, Ndana. In a household where a man has several wives, the mother's name tells you which house you belong to, and who is your brother through your father only.

From these sons came lineages that still bear their names: Mvog Atangana Mbala, Mvog Tsoungui Mbala, Mvog Fouda Mbala, Mvog Essomba Ndana. You find them at Ngomedzap, south-west of Yaoundé, and along the old trade route towards Kribi. You find them even in the names of Yaoundé's neighbourhoods, as I will show you at the end.

And the Etudi, Tulasa's line? They are there too, at Ngomedzap, beside the sons of Essomba.

There is a legend within the legend. According to Laburthe-Tolra, quoted in the same thesis, Mbala Manga, mother of the three elder sons, is said to have received from her father the talisman of fertility. That is why, say those who tell it, the Ewondo are so numerous. A woman holds in her hand the strength of a people.

And there is even a second spared child. Listen to what Constantin Ndounda Oyié, seventy-five years old, raised among his maternal uncles of the Mvog Essomba Ndana lineage, told his son in 2014 as the son was writing his thesis. According to the custom he describes, when an adult man died, one of his widows had to accompany him to the abode of the dead to go on serving him. When Essomba Nag Ba'ana died, the first wife was chosen. The second was spared, because she was young and she was pregnant. Her family took her back to her own people, the Mvele, and there she gave birth to a boy who was given his father's name: Essomba Ndana. And that boy, grown into a man, one day met his brothers, Atangana Mbala and Fouda Mbala, at a traditional wrestling contest, the *mesing*.

Do you hear it? Twice in the same line, life passes through death. Once at the river's edge, when a chief refuses to drown a child. Once at the graveside, when a pregnant woman is left to the living. This is a family tradition, collected and published as such, and I give it to you as such. But it shows one thing: among the Ewondo, the idea of a birth snatched from danger returns like a refrain.

Now let us ask the question every honest griot must ask. What does this tale do? What is it for, for those who tell it?

First, it founds a pre-eminence. "Destined to command the whole land." In a society without a king, that sentence is enormous. It says: the greatness of the Ewondo was written from the moment they crossed the river, and even the diviner of another line had seen it. That is the logic of many founding tales: future power is recognised by the threat it poses from childhood. But a prophecy like that only takes on its full meaning after the fact. The traditions Laburthe-Tolra collected were gathered in the twentieth century, at a time when the Ewondo held Yaoundé and an Ewondo was paramount chief. Did the tale take its present shape then, or is it older? No source I have read allows me to say. I leave the question open.

Next, it founds an alliance. For the hero of the scene is not only the child. It is also Tulasa, ancestor of the Etudi, who had the power to let the child drown and did not. In this legend, the Ewondo owe their life to the mercy of an Etudi. The author of the Ngomedzap thesis says it in his own way: for him, "this legend proves true", since the Etudi are as numerous as the sons of Essomba and maintain peaceful relations and marriages with them. That is not historical proof. It is a testimony of what the tale still does today: it recalls a debt, and it justifies a peace.

And that alliance hides a debate. Who are the Etudi? For the Ngomedzap thesis, and for the presentation sheet of the municipality of Soa, they are Ewondo in their own right, by language and by custom. But the historian Jean-Pierre Ombolo, in an unpublished 1986 essay quoted in a 2022 doctorate, places the "Yetudi" among "assimilated" groups, "migrants who walked in the wake of the Pahouins", like the Bamvele. If Ombolo is right, the tale of Tulasa takes on a new colour: it is the story of a group that came from alongside, perhaps different in origin, to whom tradition gives the finest place, that of president of the crossing and saviour of the Ewondo ancestor. A way of saying: you are ours, and more than ours, since without you we would not be here. That is a reading, my own. The sources do not settle it.

And here is the proof that each lineage pulls the tale towards its own bank. A history thesis from the École normale supérieure in Yaoundé devoted to Obala (Janel Landry Etoundi Bindzi, 2019) reports, after a monograph on the Mvog Mvondo, a version in which the Etoudi ancestor, whom he spells Tourasse, is no longer merely the president of the crossing. He is the one who works the miracle. "The legend says that an Etoudi forefather, named Tourasse, performed rites and incantations, then struck the river with his cane." And on the riverbed there appeared "an immense serpent called Ngan-medza", which allowed "the thousands of Etoudi sons and many other Beti, in particular the Eton, to reach the other bank". The place? Today's village of Nachtigal. The same as Elig-Ekulu. The date? "1790", says this tradition, and you know by now that such a date must be heard as memory's estimate, not as an archival date.

Measure the difference. In Laburthe-Tolra, Tulasa presides, his slave takes the people across, and the heart of the story is an Ewondo child. In the Obala version, Tourasse strikes the water with his cane, the serpent rises, and it is the sons of Etoudi whom the tradition names first. The diviner has vanished. The child has vanished. What remains is an ancestor and his staff.

Keep that staff in your hand. We are now going to walk towards those who came too late.

### V. Those who came after

Let us leave the river. Let us take the road east, towards Mefou-et-Afamba, towards the Awae country, some eighty kilometres from Yaoundé. There live Mvele, one of the Beti peoples, whom the Ewondo tradition of Elisabeth Belomo, in the tale of Ngan Medza, made the guardians who stayed near the river.

On 30 November 2019, at Nkol Essong, in the village of Ngat, a man spoke. His name was Thomas Essa Ngoukou; he was ninety-two, a village notable and a retired farmer. He spoke in Ewondo, and two researchers from the University of Yaoundé I, Eleanor A. Dasi and Larissa Aïcha Saïd, published his words in English in a university database under the title *The Mysterious Staff of Ngat*. When I quote, I quote their English rendering of words first spoken in Ewondo: between the old man and you, there is already a translation.

The old man begins at the river Yom, today the Sanaga, and with the serpent. In his version, people crossed in groups, by night: "Each night a group would cross on the snake's back, holding a lit broom to guide their way." And the rule was known, the one you already know from the tale of Ngan Medza: the serpent "was never to be touched with a sharp object, fire, or anything that could hurt it."

And this is where his version becomes his own. The culprit is not a malicious man with a spear, nor a chief who drops his torch and his sword. It is a young man holding the lit broom. He thinks he is walking on bare ground, and he rubs the broom against the serpent's back to strip off the burnt stems. An everyday gesture. A gesture without malice. The record says "accidentally". And the serpent sinks into the water, with the people on its back. Some drown. Others swim to the shore.

Notice the terrible gentleness of this version. No one meant harm, and it is enough for the bridge to vanish. In the world of the elders, ignorance does not shield you from fault.

Then comes the sequel, the part that belongs to Ngat alone.

"The next group to cross was from another tribe, led by Manga." That group did not have time to cross like the others. The serpent had dived. And, the record says, "having submerged itself, the snake left them stranded in a forest next to the river, looking for a place to settle." On which side of the water? The record does not say clearly, and I will not say it for it.

There they were, the latecomers. In a thick forest, beside a river that carried no one any more, with no bridge and no land of their own.

And here is the sentence. "As punishment for not being diligent, the gods asked them to go around the huge thick forest once a day for seven days." And a voice the record does not name announced that "they would find a staff pinned to the ground on the seventh day."

Imagine those seven days. I know no more than the recorded words, so I will describe neither the paths nor the faces. One round a day, seven times. A walk that leads nowhere, except to the end of the ordeal. A walk to learn what had been missing at the river's edge: diligence. Attention. Obedience to the word given.

And on the seventh day, the staff was there. Driven into the earth.

"Several people made attempts to remove it from the ground but to no avail."

Then a young man stepped forward. And he pulled it out without effort.

And, the record says, "under God's instructions, he became the Chief".

That is not all. The staff was not only a sign. It was a power. With it, "the newly crowned Chief could make the whole clan disappear, especially in times of external attacks from enemies." A clan that turns invisible in the hour of danger: that is what the staff gave to those who had found it.

And the old man's last sentence brings the legend all the way to us: "To this day, the Chief of the village of Ngat in Awae holds this Staff as a symbol of authority."

I have not seen that staff. No museum, no published photograph that I could find shows it. If you ever pass through Ngat, do not ask to see it as one asks to see a curiosity. Ask first to be told the story. The story is the first insignia.

Notice, finally, the two young men of this tale. The first, with his broom, sinks the bridge. The second, with his bare hands, pulls out the staff the others could not. The fault comes from a young man; so does the salvation. And remember Laburthe-Tolra's informant, that Mr Onana, for whom the leader of the crossing was a young man whose qualities were revealed along the way. Beti traditions love these young people whom nothing marked out, and whom the ordeal marks out.

### VI. The staff, the word and the chief

Why a staff?

Because among the Beti, as among many African peoples, a staff is never just a piece of wood.

Start with words. In Ewondo, a cane is *ntum*. Father Tsala's dictionary gives a mocking proverb with it: *mod wón ngâ ai ntum*, "the coward plays the swaggerer, cane raised". To raise the cane is to claim authority; the proverb reminds us that the cane alone does not make a man brave. And as early as 1913, the German ethnographer Günter Tessmann recorded among the peoples he called Pangwe (Fang, Bulu and Beti) a clan named Ntum, after *ntüm*, the walking stick. An object weighty enough with meaning for a group of people to bear its name.

Look next at the Fang cousins. The Africanist Louis Perrois, a leading authority on their sculpture, described in a 2017 catalogue note a rare Fang chief's cane, the *ntum eyema*, "the staff bearing an image", crowned with a small carved figure. Its holder was the keeper of the *byeri*, the relics of the lineage's ancestors. And this cane, he writes, could be lent for a time to the chief's messengers, to prove that the orders they carried truly came from him. The staff is a word that travels. Whoever shows it speaks with another's mouth.

And today? Among the Eton of the Lekié, the cousins north of Yaoundé, the anthropologist Onana Elouna observed in 2022 the attributes of a chief: the throne, the fly-whisk, the leopard skin, the buffalo horn, and the sceptre. His informants told him this, which I pass on to you as a living belief: if the chief decides to curse someone, he need only touch him with his sceptre, without a word, for that person's life to worsen. He photographed at Nkolngal, on 11 February 2022, a chief seated on a skin-covered chair, holding two crossed black staffs and palm fronds. The staff is not a relic of the past. It is in the hands of today's chiefs.

For here is the secret, and an Ewondo word gives it away. Bolo Mbala's doctorate recalls that the verb *dzo* means "to say, to speak", and that it carries the idea of command: from it comes *ndzoe*, the chief, the one who, by order of precedence, "has the first and the last word". Among the Beti, to command is first to speak. The staff is the chief's word made visible, a word you can hold in your hand, plant, show, lend.

Now gather the staffs of our tales. Aso Abanda's stem of *kékémbé*, which raises the rainbow. Tourasse's cane, which strikes the river and lifts the serpent. The staff of Ngat, planted in the forest earth, waiting for the chief's hand. In the first two, the staff opens a passage. In the third, it designates the one who will command. It is the same object and the same idea: the right to lead people comes from a power greater than people. The gods, or God, or the ancestors choose; the staff shows their choice; no one can contest it. The researchers who published the Ngat tale put it in their own way: gods and ancestors choose the chiefs, their decision cannot be overturned, and the staff links the living with the ancestors. They even invoke young Arthur drawing Excalibur from the stone; the comparison is theirs, not the old man's, but many peoples have dreamed of a sign that only the true chief could pull free.

And yet, remember. The Beti of old had no kings. The Ngomedzap thesis recalls it, drawing on Laburthe-Tolra: there was no Ewondo word that matched the French word "chef" exactly. There was the *nkukuma*, the rich man, judge and giver, who had wives, children and clients, and knew how to wield proverbs. There was the *mfang mot*, the true man, the good man. Their authority was earned. It was not received from above.

So where do today's village chiefs come from? Largely from colonisation. The German, then French, administrations appointed chiefs, ranked them, and removed them when they disobeyed. Onana Elouna writes it bluntly: under colonisation, yesterday's protectors became "mere instruments in the service of the colonisers", tax collectors and suppliers of labour, often for forced labour. After independence, the Cameroonian state organised these chieftaincies by a decree of 15 July 1977, in three degrees, and today's chiefs, the chief of Ngat like the others, live within that framework.

Do you now understand the force of the staff of Ngat? It says: our chief does not hold his authority from an administrator or a decree, but from a choice made on the seventh day, in the forest of origins. The myth gives an institution that history has reshaped a root deeper than history. I am not saying the myth lies. I am saying it does its work: it roots.

And this is where we meet a man whose life AFROBACK tells elsewhere: Charles Atangana, son of a modest notable of the Mvog Atemenge lineage, raised by the grace of the Germans and then the French to paramount chief of the Ewondo and the Bënë. With Paul Messi, he wrote down traditions of his people in Hamburg, published in 1919 as the *Jaunde-Texte*; I have not been able to read that book, and I will not tell you what it says about the crossing. But I know two things. First: according to the historian Frederick Quinn, in 1929 Atangana wrote a text on Beti society in which he tried to hide his unremarkable childhood and claimed descent from a line of Ewondo royalty that had never existed. Second: the Ewondo family tree reproduced in Bolo Mbala's doctorate was drawn up from Henri Ngoa's work and from a biography of Charles Atangana published in 1982. In the twentieth century, the genealogical memory of a people without a king passed in part through the family and circle of the man colonisation had made a great chief.

That does not make genealogies false. It makes them human. A genealogy is a language: it says who is the elder, who owes whom, who has the right to speak first. And the historian Bouh Ma Sitna has reminded us that Beti clans split, merge and take in strangers, so that no tree can be read as a civil register. Look, in fact: in the doctorate's tree, Essomba-Na-Bana has a grandfather named Manga and a brother named Ekulu Mbala. A Manga, like the leader of the latecomers of Ngat. An Ekulu, like the diviner of Elig-Ekulu. Is it the same man, a coincidence of names, a memory that slipped from one tale to another? Nothing allows us to say. Names travel between tales the way alliances travelled between lineages, and that is how a people is woven.

For Atangana's life, his ambitions and the complaints of those he governed, go and hear his own story. Here, keep only this: the question raised by the spared child and the staff of Ngat, who has the right to command, has never stopped being asked.

### VII. The banks today

And now, go back to the river. Go back to Elig-Ekulu.

If you take the road from Batchenga to Ntui today, north-east of Yaoundé, you cross the Sanaga at Nachtigal on a bridge four hundred metres long, opened on 18 December 2020. A little further on, the river is dammed by one of the largest works in the country's history: the Nachtigal hydroelectric plant, four hundred and twenty megawatts, whose first turbine began sending power into the grid in May 2024. Where tradition places the rafts of Ekulu Ngonda and the cane of Tourasse, water turns turbines, and lorries pass over the immensity the elders called Yom.

But memory has not left the falls. In the village of Nachtigal, in 2021, the student Albertine Alexis Mbena Mbarga attended a Sanaga healing rite, the *m'baka'â*, meant to lift a curse from a family. Participants walked past the grave of a dead man and had their bodies swept, front to back, with a broom of leaves and medicinal plants. And one of them explained to her, on 10 March 2021: "passing near the grave reminds us not only of the great crossing on the serpent's back, but it is the entry into the world of the dead or of the spirits." What set them apart from the spirits, he added, were the marks on their ears and the lines the broom had left on them.

So, at the very place of the crossing, men and women of the twenty-first century replay it as a passage between the world of the living and the world of the dead. And remember the broom of Ngat, the lit broom a young man rubbed against the serpent's back. There, the broom wounds the bridge; here, the broom protects those who pass. These two brooms do not come from the same people, and I do not claim they answer each other. I set them side by side because the river saw them both.

And look at Yaoundé. The Etoudi district, which bears the name of Tulasa's line, is today the seat of the presidential palace. And according to a press article on the origin of the city's district names, based on a local teacher's account, Mvog Atangana Mballa is the home of the descendants of Atangana Mballa, "of the ancestor Essomba-Nag-Bana", and Mvog Ada that of the descendants of Tsungui Mballa. The capital of Cameroon is written, street by street, with the names of a genealogy whose origin, according to the legend, lies in a child an Etudi chief refused to drown. That the seat of power for the whole country stands on Etudi land is a fact of geography, not a prophecy. But the griot has the right to smile as he notes it.

And in the Awae country, if we believe the old man of Ngat, a chief still keeps a staff that came from the forest of the seven days.

So, you who are listening, what do you take away from this night?

First, an answer to the question both tales ask. Who has the right to command? Not the one who wants to. Not the one who shouts, cane raised, like the coward in the proverb. In the first tale, the future belongs to a child because a powerful man chose mercy over fear: the greatness of the Ewondo begins with the mercy of an Etudi. In the second, the chief is the one the ordeal designates, after seven days of walking, when all the others have failed: authority is received at the end of patience, and it obliges you to protect the whole clan. Mercy and the ordeal. Those, according to these elders, are the two doors of legitimate power.

Next, a lesson in vigilance. Genealogies have served to found alliances and honour debts; in the twentieth century they also served to invent royal lines that had never existed. Love them, recite them, but always ask who is telling them, and why.

And finally, a new way of seeing those whom history forgets. The slave who saw the future and took everyone across, whose name the place may still keep. The young pregnant widow left to the living. The group of latecomers, lost in a forest, who were given the finest of insignia. The young man with the broom, who meant no harm. The young man with bare hands, whom no one expected. Beti traditions do not tell only of the strong. They keep a place for those who arrive late, for those who serve, for those who could have been drowned.

Perhaps you are one of them. Perhaps you feel you arrived after the bridge, on the wrong side of the water, without land and without a name. Then remember Ngat. The staff was not waiting for those who crossed first. It was waiting at the end of the seven days.

Do not wound what carries you. Do not crush the child you do not understand. Walk your forest to the seventh day.

This is where you come from.

## Fact sheet

### Elig-Ekulu and the Staff of Ngat (sequels to the crossing of the Sanaga)

- **Content type**: founding oral traditions (legends, not historical facts), sequels to the Ngan Medza myth. The lineages, places and institutions mentioned are real; the episodes (the diviner's prophecy, the spared child, the staff found on the seventh day) are legendary.
- **Peoples concerned**: Ewondo and Etudi (Elig-Ekulu episode); Mvele of Awae (staff of Ngat); Eton, Manguissa, Batschenga and Sanaga variants.
- **Era**: pre-colonial (legendary time of the crossings; 17th-19th century for the actual migrations); colonial and contemporary memory.
- **Region**: Central Africa (Central Cameroon: Nachtigal Falls on the Sanaga; Awae, Mefou-et-Afamba; Yaoundé; Ngomedzap).
- **Theme**: other (lineage founding myth, legitimacy of power, object of power)

**Timeline (history and memory, distinct from the legend)**

- 17th-19th century: crossings of the Sanaga in successive waves (see the Ngan Medza sheet); around 1800, Eton and Manguissa crossings at different points according to B. Delpech; "1790" is the date given by an Etoudi tradition recorded in Obala (a memory estimate, not a historical date).
- 1911-1919: Charles Atangana and Paul Messi write down Ewondo traditions in Hamburg (*Jaunde-Texte*, 1919).
- 1929: Atangana writes a text on Beti society and claims descent from a fictitious Ewondo royal line (F. Quinn, 1980).
- 1977: decree of 15 July organising Cameroon's traditional chieftaincies in three degrees.
- 1981: Philippe Laburthe-Tolra publishes *Les Seigneurs de la forêt*, which contains the episode of Tulasa, Ekulu Ngonda and Essomba Nak (p. 104 according to Dzomo Oyie).
- 2019-2024: the Ngat staff tale is recorded (30 November 2019); Nachtigal bridge opened (18 December 2020); a rite recalling the crossing observed at Nachtigal (2021); first turbine of the Nachtigal dam (May 2024).

**Debates and uncertainties**

- The Tulasa / Ekulu Ngonda / Essomba Nak episode is known here only through two student paraphrases of Laburthe-Tolra; the exact page (p. 104 in one, a reference to "1985" in the other) must be checked in the book.
- Elig-Ekulu = Nachtigal Falls: an identification given by Laburthe-Tolra alone; the Etoudi tradition recorded in Obala also places the crossing at Nachtigal.
- "Elig-Ekulu = the old place of Ekulu": the griot's hypothesis based on Tsala's dictionary, not attested; the variant "Elig Nkulu" weakens it.
- Status of the Etudi: Ewondo in their own right (Dzomo Oyie, municipality of Soa) or an "assimilated" group (Ombolo 1986). Unresolved.
- Ekulu Ngonda is a slave-diviner in Laburthe-Tolra; an "Ekulu Mbala" is a brother of Essomba-Na-Bana in the Ngoa / G. Atangana tree: coincidence of names or another tradition.
- The Manga of Ngat has no established link with the Manga who is Essomba's grandfather in the Ewondo tree, nor with Mba Manga (the myth of the two sisters).
- Staff of Ngat: the bank where Manga's group is stranded is not specified; the staff has not been seen and no museum collection was found; the young man is not named; the OMC record's word "totemic" is disputed by S. G. Balla Ndegue (2015).
- When the tale of the child "destined to command" took its present form is unknown; the idea of a late shaping (during Ewondo dominance over Yaoundé) is only an open question.
- Not consulted: *Les Seigneurs de la forêt*, *Jaunde-Texte* (1919), H. Ngoa 1973, J.-P. Ombolo 1986, T. E. Onana (Mvog Mvondo monograph), D. Onana (Batschenga monograph), C. Mbarga Owono, *Les Beti/Baki au cœur du Cameroun* (2019), J.-C. Atangana, *Des us et coutumes de nos ancêtres*.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The slave-diviner Ekulu Ngonda foretells that Essomba Nak will command the whole land and advises that he be drowned; Tulasa refuses.
- Tourasse (Tulasa), the Etoudi ancestor, strikes the river with his cane and raises the serpent Ngan-medza.
- Mbala Manga receives the talisman of fertility from her father.
- Essomba Ndana, a posthumous son spared along with his mother, finds his brothers at a wrestling contest (family tradition).
- Manga's group walks around the forest for seven days; a young man pulls out the staff no one could remove and becomes chief; the staff makes the clan invisible to enemies.
- Variants of the crossing: two horned beasts (Eton), a trunk turned serpent (Batschenga), a serpent carrying almost all the riverside peoples (Sanaga).
