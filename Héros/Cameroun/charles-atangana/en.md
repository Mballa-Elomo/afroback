# Charles Atangana (Ntsama, known as Karl)

Paramount chief of the Ewondo and Bane, a central and controversial figure of Cameroon under German and then French administration, from the late nineteenth century to 1943.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen, but listen carefully, because this story cannot be told with a single voice.

In Yaoundé today, there is an avenue that bears his name, a square, and on a hill overlooking the city, a statue gazing into the distance. Many Cameroonians walk past it without really knowing who this man was. Others know all too well, and still argue over his memory. Because Charles Atangana does not belong to the same family as Martin Paul Samba or Rudolf Duala Manga Bell, those chiefs who ended up shot or hanged by the Germans for preparing an uprising. Atangana lived to old age, rich, honored by two successive colonial powers. And it is precisely for that reason that he must be told without bending the story in either direction.

He was born around 1880, perhaps 1883, the sources hesitate, in Mvolyé, a village that is now part of Yaoundé. He was named Ntsama. The eleventh of twelve children, son of Essomba Atangana, a headman of the Mvog Atemenge sublineage among the Ewondo. The world did not spare him from the start either: his father died when he was about six years old, and the child grew up in the family compound, like so many other Beti boys in the region between the Sanaga and Nyong rivers.

Then the Germans arrived, searching for ivory and trade routes. Around 1896 or 1897, young Ntsama was sent to Kribi, on the coast, to the school run by the Pallottine mission fathers. There, a German missionary, Father Heinrich Vieter, took a particular interest in this bright child. Ntsama learned German, history, geography, arithmetic, catechism. He was baptized, becoming the first Ewondo convert to Catholicism, and received a new name: Charles, in German Karl.

His schooling stopped abruptly in 1899. The Bulu, a neighboring people whose son you may already know, Martin Paul Samba, rose up and sacked the school and church at Kribi. Young Atangana waited it out, taking refuge in Douala with the Fathers, until colonial troops crushed the revolt.

And this is where the path that sets Atangana apart from so many others truly begins. In August 1900, the Germans appointed him interpreter for five hundred Bulu hostages pressed into forced labor at Victoria. He also served there as a nurse. Listen carefully to this detail, because it matters: this role of interpreter is not a neutral post. It is the very hinge through which colonial violence passes, between those who impose it and those who suffer it. Atangana then became an office clerk in Buea, married a young woman, Marie Biloa, and in 1901 secured land so the Pallottine Fathers could build a mission in Yaoundé, opening the east and south of the country to Catholic evangelization.

In 1902, the German administration made him its official representative to the Ewondo people, in charge of organizing the census and tax collection. He personally chose three hundred village headmen as tax collectors, of whom two hundred and thirty-three were approved by the Germans, and negotiated a five percent commission for them. Remember this mechanism, because it is the real key to this story: Atangana did not rise to power through force of arms, but by making himself indispensable to the colonial fiscal and administrative system, a system that squeezed his own Ewondo brothers.

From 1904 onward, he became the shadow and right hand of Hans Dominik, commander of the Yaoundé post. The very same Hans Dominik, it must be said, whose so-called "pacification" campaigns crushed so many Cameroonian communities, and under whose orders Martin Paul Samba himself once served before turning against Germany. For six years, Atangana accompanied Dominik on at least fifteen administrative patrols, negotiated with rebellious Manguissa groups to avoid confrontation, and helped open colonial posts from Bafia to Maroua. A rare thing in a colony where strict social segregation between whites and Blacks was the rule: Dominik and Atangana sometimes shared the same tent, the same table. Meanwhile, Atangana's fortune grew: workshops, plantations, selling produce to the railway construction camps.

But I must also tell you what official memory prefers not to dwell on. In 1907, members of the Mvog Ada sublineage rebelled against his appointment as official interpreter and plotted to poison him. When the plot leaked, it was Atangana himself who informed the German authorities. On 11 April, six conspirators were executed, two others imprisoned. Here is a fact that cannot be erased: men of his own people died before a firing squad because Atangana chose loyalty to the colonial administration over silence toward his own.

Dominik died in 1910. That same year, Atangana inherited leadership of his father's sublineage. Then, in 1911, the German administration appointed him president of the court of first instance and, above all, paramount chief of the Ewondo and Bane, a title the Germans invented from whole cloth to unify under a single authority peoples who had traditionally had no such supreme chief. It was precisely this appointment that, the following year, alarmed Martin Paul Samba among the neighboring Bulu: he saw in it a sign that the Germans intended to govern southern Kamerun by relying on peoples other than his own, playing Cameroonians against one another. This concern was among the reasons that pushed Samba to secretly prepare the uprising that would cost him his life in 1914. Hold onto this: Atangana did nothing out of personal malice to provoke this rivalry, but his rise, willed by Berlin, served the old colonial method of divide and rule.

In late 1911, Atangana crossed the sea to Germany. He taught the Ewondo language at the Colonial Institute of the University of Hamburg, and compiled, for posterity, a collection of Beti stories, tales, and proverbs that would become the Jaunde-Texte, a valuable document on Ewondo culture before the full colonial rupture. In 1913, he was received by Kaiser Wilhelm II and by Pope Pius X. Few Africans of his era, anywhere on the continent, knew such official recognition. He himself wrote, about what it took to succeed with the Germans, a sentence that has remained famous: "To dare to approach the Germans it is necessary to abandon the traits which displease them, to become their friend and then be valued by them." Remember this sentence, because it says everything: Atangana never hid that his strategy was voluntary assimilation to the manners and tastes of the colonizer.

In 1914, history brushed past him a second time on the path of resistance, without him stepping onto it. Envoys of Rudolf Duala Manga Bell, the Duala king who was preparing a pan-African uprising against the Germans together with Samba, came seeking his support. Atangana kept silent about what he learned, did not repeat it to the Germans, but instructed the envoy to urge Manga Bell to reconsider. It is important to be precise here, because history sometimes blurs the roles: it was not Atangana who betrayed the plot. It was Sultan Njoya of the Bamum, approached separately, who refused to join it and alerted the Basel Mission in late April 1914, triggering the arrest and then the hanging of Manga Bell and his adviser Ngosso Din on 8 August, the very same day Samba fell before a German firing squad in Ebolowa. So Atangana did not, with his own hand, betray his peers in this particular affair, but neither did he join them. He remained, to the end, the man who never risks losing the trust of whoever holds power.

Then came the war, the real one, the one that swept up all of Europe and caught up with Kamerun. The Allies took Douala in September 1914, the Germans fell back on Yaoundé, held it until the first of January 1916, then had to flee before British troops. And here, perhaps, lies the most meaningful moment in Charles Atangana's life. He chose to flee with his German masters. He led with him seventy-two Ewondo and Bane chiefs, and a human column that the archives estimate at between fourteen thousand and twenty thousand people, soldiers, porters, families, through the forest, all the way to Spanish Guinea. A Beti song, kept alive in popular memory, tells of this exodus: "Atangana Ntsama, the war is over," a song that, depending on how you listen to it, either celebrates or laments this loyalty carried to the ends of the earth for the German occupier. How many of those thousands of people followed Atangana by choice, how many under duress, how many simply for lack of any alternative amid the chaos of the retreat? No source can tell you for certain, and it is important to acknowledge that.

A refugee on Fernando Po, then sent to Spain with six other chiefs to testify, if needed, on behalf of how the Germans treated their African subjects, Atangana was received in 1919 by King Alfonso XIII. For two years he waited in Madrid, while the French took charge, under League of Nations mandate, of the Ewondo homeland he had left. So he wrote to the French government, swore loyalty, demanded to return. In late November 1920, he landed in Douala. But the French, for their part, did not trust him outright: his unwavering loyalty to Germany was a concern. He was first assigned to Dschang, to supervise forced-labor road-construction gangs, a kind of probation. It was only in 1921, or early 1922, that Yaoundé found him once more as paramount chief, some saying the country, without him, had become ungovernable even for the French administration itself.

Under the French, the power of traditional chiefs was far narrower than under the Germans. One governor-general of the time, Van Vollenhoven, put it bluntly: chiefs held no authority of their own, there existed only one authority in the territory, that of France. Atangana thus became, above all, the zealous relay of what France demanded: tax collection, mobilization of labor for plantations and the railway, the forced introduction of cocoa and coffee into the peasant economy. He even equipped himself with a private police force, the fulus, and a cabinet modeled on what he had observed in Spain, though he never gave it any real power. His fortune, meanwhile, kept growing: his annual salary rose from six thousand francs in 1922 to twenty-four thousand francs in 1938, not counting commissions on taxes collected by the chiefs under his authority, which could bring the most powerful chiefs, in the 1930s, more than four hundred thousand francs a year. Witnesses of the time attributed to him immense plantations, cocoa, oil palms, food crops, hundreds of head of cattle, figures likely inflated by legend but revealing of the image of power he projected.

Not everyone accepted this power in silence. In 1924, the Bane, one of the peoples placed under his authority, filed a complaint before the courts: "We work always and it is Atangana who receives the money. For all the things that we have sent to the Europeans, such as chickens and eggs, through Atangana, we have received nothing." This sentence, preserved in colonial archives, deserves to be heard at least as much as the honors received from kings and popes: it is the voice of those who, at the bottom of the ladder, paid the price of one man's rise. In 1929, Atangana went so far as to rewrite his own history, claiming the title of "king" and inventing a fictitious royal Ewondo ancestry, erasing in the process the modest, orphaned origin that had actually been his. And while education could have been his finest legacy, he who had learned so much from the Germans, he opposed in the following years the expansion of public schooling among his own people, fearing, according to testimony gathered by historians, that a too-educated youth might eventually challenge his authority.

To be fair, something in this same life amounts to a real contribution. Atangana obtained German and later French funding for roads, schools, clinics, churches. He pushed for public health campaigns, notably against sleeping sickness. He was among those who turned Yaoundé, then a simple administrative post, into the sketch of a capital, with its first urban plans. His palace at Efoulan, now a listed historical monument, still bears witness to that builder's ambition. He traveled, received at the Paris Colonial Exposition in 1931, at the French Colonial Conference in 1935. His first wife, Marie Biloa, died in 1938; he remarried in January 1940 to a young Beti woman, Julienne Ngonoa.

Charles Atangana died on the first of September 1943, in Mvolyé, the very village where it all began, a little past sixty years old. No one succeeded him immediately: the title of paramount chief died with him, his palace fell into ruin, forgotten for decades, until traditional chieftaincies were legally reestablished in 1977, and then, in the 1990s and 2000s, his daughter Marie-Thérèse Assiga Ahanda revived the lineage, became in turn paramount chief of the Ewondo, and undertook to restore the family palace.

So who was Charles Atangana, really? Cameroonian historians themselves do not speak with a single voice, and I will not decide for you. For some, he is a collaborator in the fullest sense of the word: a man who built his fortune and power on submission to two successive colonial powers, who handed conspirators from his own people over to the German gallows, who silenced the voice of the school to preserve his throne, who rewrote his own past to invent a legitimacy he had not been born with. For others, he is a pragmatic builder, a man who, understanding that no armed resistance could then overturn colonial power, chose to negotiate from within the system whatever he could wrest for his people: roads, schools, healthcare, the written preservation of Ewondo culture, the sketch of a capital city. Both readings rest on the same facts, and that is precisely the uncomfortable truth of this story: Charles Atangana did not choose between good and evil as in a fable. He chose, relentlessly, his own closeness to power, whoever held it, and that closeness produced both roads and gallows, both schools and silenced injustices.

African history, the real kind, is not told only through those who died standing before a firing squad. It is also told through those who lived long, in the palaces the colonizer allowed them to build, and whose memory remains, even today, a battlefield. Remember this name, Charles Atangana, not to bow before him, nor to judge him wholesale, but to understand that colonization did not produce only heroes and executioners: it also produced men like him, and they must be looked at honestly, face on.

## Reference sheet

**Charles Atangana (born Ntsama, known as Karl)**

- **Era**: colonial (German Kamerun, then Cameroon under French mandate, c. 1880-1943)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Cameroon, Yaoundé region, Ewondo and Bane peoples)
- **Theme**: politics (ambivalent figure: colonial administration and collaboration, not resistance)

**Timeline**

- c. 1880-1883: born as Ntsama in Mvolyé (present-day Yaoundé), eleventh child of Essomba Atangana, a Mvog Atemenge headman among the Ewondo (sources waver between 1876 and 1885); orphaned of his father around age six
- 1896-1900: educated at the Pallottine mission in Kribi, Catholic baptism (first Ewondo baptized), schooling interrupted by the 1899 Bulu revolt; becomes interpreter for Bulu forced laborers at Victoria in 1900, then a clerk in Buea
- 1902-1910: official German representative to the Ewondo in charge of census and taxation (1902), then right hand of commander Hans Dominik on administrative patrols (1904-1910); in 1907, reports to the Germans a plot against him by the Mvog Ada sublineage, leading to the execution of six conspirators
- 1911-1913: appointed court president and paramount chief of the Ewondo and Bane by the Germans (1911), an unprecedented title that notably alarms Bulu chief Martin Paul Samba; travels to Germany to teach Ewondo at the University of Hamburg and compile the Jaunde-Texte (1911-1912); received by Kaiser Wilhelm II and Pope Pius X (1913)
- 1914-1920: approached by envoys of Rudolf Duala Manga Bell to support a pan-African uprising, keeps silent without joining or personally betraying them (it is Sultan Njoya of the Bamum who alerts the Germans); flees with the retreating German troops to Spanish Guinea (1916), exiled in Spain, received by King Alfonso XIII (1919)
- 1920-1943: returns to Cameroon under French mandate (November 1920), a probationary period at Dschang, reinstated as paramount chief (1921-1922); accumulates wealth and power under French administration, faces a Bane complaint in 1924, invents a royal ancestry in 1929, opposes the expansion of schooling; dies on 1 September 1943 in Mvolyé

**Notable quotes**

- "To dare to approach the Germans it is necessary to abandon the traits which displease them, to become their friend and then be valued by them." Attributed to Charles Atangana himself, reported by historian Frederick Quinn ("Charles Atangana of Yaounde," *The Journal of African History*, 1980) and repeated by Wikipedia (English version).
- "We work always and it is Atangana who receives the money. For all the things that we have sent to the Europeans, such as chickens and eggs, through Atangana, we have received nothing." A complaint filed by Bane chiefs in 1924, preserved in the French colonial archives and cited by Frederick Quinn.

Both quotes are documented through academic sources (colonial archives and the work of historian Frederick Quinn) rather than oral tradition: they are therefore treated here as authenticated quotes, unlike the legendary phrases attributed to resistance fighters such as Martin Paul Samba.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- Some popular and popularized accounts cite extremely precise personal fortunes (exact plantation acreages, exact cattle counts) that stem from oral testimony gathered long after the events rather than verified period inventories: these figures, described by the historiography itself as "likely exaggerated," should be treated as symbolic orders of magnitude of his wealth rather than exact accounting data.
- Atangana's own 1929 claim of a royal Ewondo ancestry tracing back to fictitious origins should be treated as a self-legitimizing narrative he constructed himself, not as an established genealogical fact: the sources agree on the modest, non-princely nature of his actual birth.
