# Zamba, Mingon and the evu: why God went away

A Beti myth (Ewondo, Eton, Bënë, Evuzok and their Bulu, Ntumu and Fang cousins), Centre region, Cameroon. Version 1 of 2026-10-16, written from the research file `recherche.md` (same folder). Entry B1 of the Centre inventory: the origin of death, of the power called evu, and of God's withdrawal.

> This is a myth from oral tradition. The griot tells it as a myth, never as fact. The beliefs surrounding the evu, the rites, the accusations and the trials, on the other hand, are real, and have been described by historians, anthropologists and witnesses: at every step, the griot tells you what belongs to the story and what belongs to history.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. When God hunted for humankind

Listen.

There was a time, the Beti elders say, when no one was buried.

Not because the dead were hidden away. Because there were none. A man might fall into a deep sleep, so deep that his breath seemed gone. No grave was dug. People waited. For the one who could wake him still lived in the village.

There was a time when hunger was unknown. No one toiled in the fields to eat, says a storyteller from Yaoundé. Every morning someone went alone into the forest, to a place only he knew, and came back at dusk with fresh meat, wild pig, antelope. He brought back enough for all.

That someone, the Ewondo call Zamba.

Zamba is the name the Beti give to the Creator. Father Théodore Tsala, an Ewondo priest and the great lexicographer of his language, wrote it in his 1956 dictionary: *Zambá anë nkom yob ai si*, "Zamba is the creator of heaven and earth." The Cameroonian author Alexandre Nana, who published a version of our story in 2010 in *Anthropologie beti et sens chrétien de l'homme*, calls him *Zambá-Nkom bot*, and translates it simply: God the Creator. Further south, among the Fang of Gabon and Equatorial Guinea, people say Nzame, or Nsambe. And the same Tsala records another expression that sums up everything you are about to hear: *Sósólō Zamba*, the hidden God.

For this is the story of a God who was not hidden, and became so.

The elders know that names change from one storyteller to the next. In a version collected in the Yaoundé country in the 1930s by a missionary, Father Guillemin, the one who lives among humans is not called Zamba but Okombodo. The German ethnologist Günter Tessmann had come across that name among the Fang in the early twentieth century, as Nkombodo, but for a different being, a primordial twin born long before God. And some scholars, such as the anthropologist Lluís Mallart-Guimerà, who lived for years among the Evuzok of Lolodorf, see Zamba as a mythical ancestor as much as a god. The anthropologist Sophie Bouly de Lesdain, summarising Philippe Laburthe-Tolra's version, writes "great God or monitoring ancestor." Both at once, perhaps: among the Beti, the one who gave the world is also the one who gave the rule.

Zamba had a wife. And a daughter, already grown.

The storyteller Elisabeth Belomo, who told this myth in Ewondo in Yaoundé on 23 August 2016 for the researchers of the Our Mythical Childhood project, gives this wife a name: Mingon. She is the only source we know of that names her so. In the other versions she has no name. She is "the woman." Remember that detail: it will matter when the time for questions comes.

One day Zamba had to go on a journey. A long journey. And before he left, he gathered his household and left them two sayings. I give them to you as Alexandre Nana reports them, and as they appear, almost word for word, in a tale published in 1982 by L. Messi in the review *Ensemble*, and in Elisabeth Belomo's version:

"If anyone falls into the *nnom oyo*, the male sleep, do not bury him. Wait for my return."

"There is in the forest a wicked beast called evu. Do not take it. Have nothing to do with it."

Two prohibitions. One about death, the other about the forest.

And the griot tells you straight away, because an honest griot does not hide the ending to keep you hooked: both sayings will be broken. And it is because they were broken that, ever since, the dead are buried, God lives far away, and every Beti, when someone dies, asks who ate them.

### II. The village, the forest and the belly

Before we go to the swamp, sit for a while in the village. For this myth cannot be understood without knowing how those who told it lived.

The Beti of central and southern Cameroon lived in the forest, in lineage villages. The village, the *dzal*, was the ordered world. Around it lay the forest. Vast, nourishing, and dangerous. Mallart-Guimerà, who worked among the Evuzok from 1961 to 1968, showed how their whole way of thinking was organised around that boundary. On one side the village: social life, marriage, sharing, the cooked, the light, the order left by the ancestor. On the other the forest: the asocial, the raw, the absence of rule. People spat towards the forest to cast out evil, he writes, and towards the village to draw in prosperity.

Work was divided along the same lines. To men, hunting. To women, the fields and the cooking; and in the Evuzok versions, it is while going fishing that the woman meets the evu. The anthropologist André Mary, a specialist of the neighbouring Fang, makes the point when he reads our myth: game comes from the forest by the hand of the man, and it is the woman who turns it, at the hearth, into shared food. Everyone in their place, and wild meat kept at a safe distance.

Then there is marriage. Among the Beti, lineages were exogamous: one took a wife from outside one's clan. The wife therefore always came from elsewhere. She bore children for a lineage that was not her own by birth. She was at the heart of the household and, at the same time, in some eyes, a stranger. Keep that in mind, you too. We will come back to it.

And finally, there is the body. For the old Beti, a human being was not made of a single piece. Mallart-Guimerà sums it up: the head is the bond with the ancestors; the heart is natural knowledge; and the belly is the seat of the evu. The belly is not only where one digests. It is a place of power. And the word itself has two meanings: in its first sense, explains the Ewondo author Jean-Claude Atangana in 2010, *evú* means the stomach, *evú tsit*, the stomach of an animal; in its second sense, it names a hidden force that allows the one who carries it to do good or evil.

So here is the setting of our myth. An ordered village, a wild forest, a boundary that men cross to hunt, women who come from other clans, and a belly that can shelter something other than oneself.

And in the middle of the forest, the story says, there was a swamp.

The versions do not all name the same place. For Philippe Laburthe-Tolra, the ethnologist who lived for years at Minlaaba among the Ewondo and the Bënë, the evu "lived in the swamps." In Father Guillemin's version it is a swamp in the middle of an immense forest, the home "of an invisible spirit, powerful and voracious, feeding on the beasts of the forest." In the version Elisabeth Belomo told in 2016, it is the edge of a swamp, beneath a great tree. In one of the six Evuzok versions Mallart-Guimerà published in 1981, it is no longer water but a great rock surrounded by ferns. And in another Evuzok version, the evu lives alone in the hollow of a tree and eats its game raw.

Still water, stone, the hollow tree. Always a place beyond the village's reach.

That is where Zamba went for meat.

For, and this was the secret the woman did not know, Zamba did not hunt alone. At the edge of that swamp, every day, he found a heap of freshly killed game. Antelopes. Wild pigs. Someone in the black water was killing for him. In Laburthe-Tolra's version and in Nana's, the evu presented itself as Zamba's hunter. According to the Cameroonian historian Alice Mathilde Ekani Zinga, who analysed these versions in her 2022 doctoral thesis at the University of Yaoundé I, there was a kind of hunting contract between God and the evu.

And another version, collected by Mallart-Guimerà among the Evuzok, reaches even further back. Listen to it, it is short and it changes everything. The ancestor Zamba gave a wife to each of his children: to Man-son-of-Zamba, to Gorilla-son-of-Zamba, to Chimpanzee-son-of-Zamba. He gave them riches. But to the evu he gave nothing. No wife. No goods. He hid it in the hollow of a forest tree, where, the anthropologist writes, "marriage, social life, cultural goods do not exist."

In that version, the evu is not a monster fallen out of nowhere. It is a son of God. The forgotten son. The one left without a wife, at the edge of the world.

Remember it. When the evu arrives in the village demanding food, you will know where its hunger comes from.

### III. The voice from the swamp

Zamba has gone. The days pass.

And in Elisabeth Belomo's version there is a detail the others do not give: hunger comes. Zamba, the provider, is no longer there. The village is hungry. So the wife does what, the storyteller says, other women did not do. She goes hunting.

In Nana's version, it is not hunger that drives her. It is curiosity; in Messi's, she is won over by the evu's talent as a hunter. She wants to know where her husband finds so much meat. She goes to her daughter and asks for the trail. And the daughter, Nana reports, answers: "But it is forbidden to follow it..."

Stop here, you who are listening. Set these versions side by side. In one, a woman who faces famine by stepping outside her role. In the other, a curious woman who disregards a prohibition. The same act, two judgements.

The woman walks a long way. She leaves the village paths behind. She reaches the edge of the swamp. And there she sees what Zamba saw every morning: an immense heap of freshly killed game.

No one around. Nothing but black water and silence.

She asks aloud who killed these beasts.

And a voice rises from the swamp.

"It was I."

In Father Guillemin's version, the exchange has come down to us as he noted it in the 1930s:

"Who are you?
I am the Evou.
Why do you not come to the village?
I am hard to feed and I hate the light."

In Nana's version, the voice adds: "It is I, Evú, who am Zambá's hunter." And it says something terrible, which casts light on all that follows: "I have no use for flesh; I feed on the principles of life," the *nhebe enyin*, which Nana explains as the principle of breath that gives life.

And in the Evuzok version Mallart-Guimerà published in 1981, the woman first came to fish. She caught many fish. She finds a dead animal on a rock and thinks the leopard killed it. Looking for a vine to tie it with, she discovers two more. Then a voice comes from the middle of the rock: "Is it you who killed these animals?" "Who are you?" "I am Evu!" And the woman discovers a thing standing upright, the text says, "like the frog we call *mvon*."

A frog. A toad. All the versions agree on this point: when the evu shows itself, it looks like an amphibian. Nana says "an enormous toad." Guillemin, "a hideous frog" emerging from the mud. Belomo, a frog again.

In Mallart's version, the evu is generous at first. "It is my game! I killed it! But if you want it, you can take it." The woman takes the meat home and tells her husband she found it, killed by the leopard. The whole family eats. Three days later she returns.

And that is when the evu asks.

"You know, I would like to go to your village. If I go, you will give me meat, won't you? Since I give you so much."

Listen carefully to what the evu proposes. It is not theft. It is an exchange. Meat for meat. The game of the forest for the food of the village. A pact.

In Nana's version, it is the woman herself who invites the evu. She praises the village to it "as to a true hunter": everything is there, she says, poultry, livestock, and living people.

Living people. She said it without knowing.

One difficulty remains: how to carry the evu? It cannot walk. "I cannot walk on my own feet," it says in Nana's version. In Mallart's, the woman offers to carry it in the big basket. No, says the evu, everyone would see me. In the small narrow-necked basket, the *nkun*? No, I would be too cramped. On her back? No: "I do not want to be carried where people scratch themselves."

There is humour in this scene, and the storytellers know it. The evu plays hard to please like a guest of rank. But beneath the humour, the threat is rising. For in the end it says: "I want to tell you how you must bring me to the village."

In Nana's version the words are graver: "I am not a being of light, I live in darkness... let me enter your belly, by the way people come out when they are born."

And the woman agrees. Mallart gives her reason with a simplicity that chills: she agrees "because it is he who gives her the meat."

She crouches at the water's edge. And the evu enters her.

Here the griot pauses for a moment. For this act is the heart of the myth, and scholars have reflected on it at length. André Mary notes that in most versions it is the woman who takes the initiative of bringing the thing to the village, but it is the evu that suggests she crouch and open herself. He calls this moment "a birth in reverse": through the passage by which children come out, something goes in. Instead of giving life, the belly receives what devours it.

Mallart-Guimerà, in his 1975 article, says the evu enters her "until the two become one and the same being."

"Let us go!" says the evu.

And the woman returns to the village, carrying in her belly the beast of the swamp, as one carries a child.

### IV. "Woman, I am hungry"

In the village, life seems to resume. Then one morning a voice speaks from inside the woman.

"Woman, I am hungry."

This sentence appears almost identically in Nana's version and in Messi's. It is simple. It is terrible. It will return, again and again, until the end of the story.

"Give me that goat crossing the yard."

The woman points to the goat. And the goat falls dead on the spot. The evu eats it. The next day: "Woman, I am hungry." Another goat. The black goat. Then the sheep. Soon there are no goats or sheep left in the village. So the evu turns to the henhouse, and the henhouse empties in its turn.

In Mallart's Evuzok version, the evu even refuses the chickens with contempt: "Did I give you birds in the forest? Didn't I kill real game for you?" It demands the promised meat: "Who can kill game for you now? I am no longer there, since I live here now."

Look at the order of the victims. Mary saw it clearly: first the wild game the evu used to give. Then the domestic animals, those of the village, what Mallart calls the animals "intended for exchanges and sacrifices." Then, as it has known from the start, human beings. Step by step, the evu climbs the ladder of everything that keeps a society alive.

At last the woman understands. In Nana's version she cries: "I am tired of you: go back to your lake."

And the evu answers with the most frightening sentence in the myth:

"Here I am, here I stay. You should not have taken me. We are bound for life, forever. Give me your daughter, or it is you I will eat."

And the woman answers. Every version gives her answer, in different words. In Nana's: "Take her, then." In Belomo's, according to the English translation of the record: "Good! Then eat her and leave!" And the 2016 storyteller adds a reason we must hear: the mother was counting on Zamba. She believed that on his return he would bring the child back to life. In the Evuzok version of 1981, darker still, it is not one daughter but all her children, one after another. "He eats them; he drinks their blood. Evu is already among us!"

Do not look away too quickly, you who are listening. This myth is not about a monster attacking an innocent family from outside. It is about a mother caught in a pact, who hands over her child to save herself, or because she believes death does not yet exist. It is about what we do when we have already given too much to turn back.

The daughter falls. Not dead, says Nana: she falls into the *nnom oyo*, the male sleep. The sleep of which Zamba had said: do not bury the one who falls into it, wait for me.

But Zamba does not come back.

One day passes. Two days. And the sleep changes its nature. The body begins to decay, to smell. Nana writes a sentence I give you word for word, because it holds the whole myth: "But this *nnom oyo* becomes death."

Do you understand? Before, there was no death. There was a sleep from which one returned. And it is the waiting that lasted too long, the absence of God, the unbearable smell, that turned sleep into death.

So the mother does what any mother would do before the body of her child coming apart. She buries her.

The second prohibition is broken.

Another version, which the historian Michèle Carmèle Bolo Mbala cites in her 2022 thesis as "the legend of God with humans," tells only this moment, with no evu at all. God goes on a journey; he says: if your child falls into the great sleep, do not put him in the ground; the child dies, the smell becomes unbearable, they bury him. And God, on his return, sees the mound. As if, at the bottom of the great myth, there lay a simpler story, that of the first burial. Which came first? No one can say.

At last Zamba returns.

He looks at his village. Not a goat left in the yard. Not a sheep. Not a hen. And these words, which Nana and Messi report in the same terms: "I no longer recognise anything in my village, once so beautiful!"

His wife weeps. She confesses. In Guillemin's version, it is he who guesses: "Would you by any chance have brought the Evou here?"

Zamba asks where his daughter is. "Show her to me, I will bring her back to life."

And the mother speaks the sentence that closes the old world: "But I have buried her..."

Twice she had disobeyed. Once by taking the evu. Once by burying the sleep. And Zamba, furious, pronounces his sentence. I give it to you in Nana's version:

"I am leaving for good! I will never come back, and I leave you with evu, eater of life, author of death, and *awú*, death itself."

He leaves. Where to? Belomo says: from the earth to the sky, promising never to return. Guillemin says he threw the ocean between himself and the human race. And one of the versions Tessmann recorded among the Fang has God say: "I have gone beyond the sea... I have gone up to heaven! Your children will not know it."

Other Beti evenings tell God's departure differently: it is the spider Ngam who, by accusing the Creator of humanity's misfortunes, drives him away. That story the griot has told you elsewhere, in the myth of Ngam. Here, it is the woman and the swamp who speak.

And the woman? She remains. With the evu in her belly. Belomo adds that she later died too, of ordinary death, for humans have no right to end their own lives. Father Guillemin's version gives an ending that weighs on generations: "The Evou multiplied and passed to the woman's descendants; soon everyone had their own."

That is what the Beti elders tell when asked why we die.

God was here. Something was taken from the forest that should not have been taken. Something was buried that should have been waited for. And God went away.

But among the Beti, the story never stops at the sentence. For what he left behind, the evu, is like nothing you imagine.

### V. Neither back nor belly

Now leave the tale. What follows is no longer legend: it is what the Beti believed, said and did around the evu, as described by witnesses, Ewondo priests, ethnologists and historians. And prepare yourself, for what you are about to hear is not simple "witchcraft."

The Evuzok have a formula for what the evu is. Mallart-Guimerà chose it as the title of his book: the evu has "neither back nor belly." He explains: for them the back is male, the belly female. To say the evu has neither is to say it is "neither male nor female, neither strong nor weak, neither good nor bad, but potentially all of these at once." The anthropologist compares it to what other scholars call a "zero symbolic value": a pure force that takes on the meaning of the one who carries it.

How was it imagined? The descriptions vary, and the differences are precious. For Mallart's Evuzok, it is a "thing," *dzomo*, lodged in the belly, with two arms, two legs and no heart, "like a small, very round ball," like a crab, a spider, a bat or a frog; or like a tree that sinks its roots into the belly and spreads its branches through the whole body. For the Mvae of the South, interviewed by Sophie Bouly de Lesdain, it has the shape of a crab with a powerful jaw. For Father Isidore Tabi, it may take a human form, with teeth, eyes, mouth, feet, ears. And for Joséphine Etoundi, an Ewondo woman in her seventies whom the ethnologist Jeanne-Françoise Vincent interviewed near Minlaaba, and whose words were published in 1976: "The évu is a small animal with eyes and legs. [...] The évu looks like a spider with legs. [...] The évu has no heart, since even you who carry it in your belly, it can kill you!"

But listen to what she says next, for this is where the evu shows another face: "If a child does brilliantly at school, that is the évu. [...] People believe white people have very powerful évu, for instance to make aeroplanes. They say the évu has a very daring character, so a bold girl has the évu."

The brilliant pupil. The engineer of aeroplanes. The bold girl. That too is the evu.

For the evu is not only the force that kills. It is the force that surpasses. Mallart-Guimerà recorded among the Evuzok, by their own count, "ninety-nine kinds of evu," nine being for them the number of abundance and ritual efficacy. There are the evu of the night, which ruin and kill. But there is also the evu of prosperity, *bisie*, the evu of wealth, *akuma*, the evu of love, *ayas*, the evu of skill, *akeng*, and the evu of speech and command, *edzoe*. Of that one, Mallart writes: when, during a trial, the one who has it rises to speak, "everyone falls silent at once."

Healers, diviners, counter-sorcerers, lucky hunters, orators, dancers, musicians, the skilled *mvet* player: all of them, according to the Evuzok, carry an evu. As early as the beginning of the twentieth century, Günter Tessmann noted among the Fang that the "good" bearers of evu were all the important people: healers, cult leaders, chiefs, the wealthy, artists and singers. Alexandre Nana put it with perfect clarity in 2010: the evu kills at a distance, of course, "yet it is also what gives material wealth, the power to command, victory in war. [...] It is with the evu that the sorcerer kills people at a distance, but also, contradictorily, thanks to the evu that life can be protected."

And those who have none? They bear a magnificent name. The Evuzok say *mmimie*. Tessmann, among the Fang, heard *memieme*, and translated it "people of light," from *mie*, moonlight. A 2012 dissertation from the École normale supérieure in Yaoundé still gives: "person of light, of honey, of moonlight." They are, says Mallart, a "portion of Zamba": the share of order that God left behind. But they are also defenceless. The informants of the historian Ekani Zinga, between 2013 and 2019, said it plainly: "If your belly is empty, better not claim you can stand up" to hostile powers.

Do you see now why the myth runs so deep? Zamba left behind two things: death, and the force that can kill. But that force is also what makes chiefs, healers, orators and artists. Among the Evuzok, the world God had ordered is now judged, in Mallart's words, "too precarious, too insufficient." Magic there is the alliance of humans with the evu to "go beyond the cultural world that Zamba left." The historian Bolo Mbala even links to this force of surpassing the feat of Nnë Bodo, the ferrier of men in the crossing of the Sanaga, whom the griot told you about in the myth of Ngan Medza.

Even the evu's name is disputed. For Father Tsala and Alexandre Nana, and according to them for Laburthe-Tolra, the word comes from the verb *vu*, "to flourish, to grow rich." For Tessmann, on the contrary, it comes from a root meaning "to kill." Others, Bolo Mbala reports, link it to *wu*, to die, or to *vud*, to bend. To flourish or to kill: the etymology itself hesitates between the two faces of the thing, and the griot will not decide in the linguists' place.

Then there is the way it is shaped. For among the Beti an evu is something one fashions. Mallart describes a rite in which the maternal uncle awakens and directs a boy's evu of prosperity, and leaves him a prohibition: "The evu that has just been awakened in you must not go to *mgbel*," the world of the night, "if you go there, you will die! [...] You have an evu that commands you to do this: give people food and love humankind!" Bolo Mbala, for her part, reports the *nkomdo mon* rite, "fashioning the child," performed by the maternal uncle nine days after birth. Jean-Claude Atangana says the same in other words: in general every person has the evu, but it must be fashioned, moulded, to give it its power. On this point the sources diverge: among the Evuzok the evu is innate and hereditary; according to Tessmann and Atangana, the seed is in everyone and must be awakened.

Give people food and love humankind. Listen closely to that sentence. It is the exact opposite of what the evu of the myth did. It took the village's food. It ate people. The rite mends the myth: it takes the same force and teaches it to feed instead of devour. Mallart showed it in his own way: sharing is what makes wealth born of the evu compatible with order. Bouly de Lesdain says the same of the Mvae: whoever possesses "eats" wealth, and it is redistribution that clears them of suspicion.

And here is the most beautiful reversal. Among Beti women at Minlaaba there was a great women's rite, the *mevungu*, described by Laburthe-Tolra and Marie-Paule Bochet de Thé. It served to fight sorcery and restore fertility, and Bochet de Thé notes that it was also, for women, "a means of protection against the tyranny of men." The woman who led the rite, the "mother of the mevungu," was chosen, Laburthe-Tolra writes, as "the one who had the greatest evú." And he concludes: "It is indeed the clearly localised evu of a woman that will allow everyone, men and women alike, to fight effectively against the surrounding sorcery."

A woman brought the evu into the world, says the myth. And it is a woman's evu, says the rite, that protects the whole village.

### VI. The trial of Mingon

So, you who are listening, here is the question we cannot avoid. Why is it a woman?

The record of the Our Mythical Childhood project, written by the researcher Divine Che Neba of the University of Yaoundé I, answers in one sentence: among the Beti, Mingon would be "a prototype of women who disobeyed patriarchy." Bouly de Lesdain, summarising Mallart, sees the woman as the mediator between the bush and the village, associated with nature and with wetness. Ekani Zinga notes similarities with the biblical Genesis, which also shows "the curiosity and the greed of the woman."

But listen to the other voices too, for the trial is not over.

André Mary observes that the woman of the myth does not remain guilty to the end. She becomes a hunter-wife, a nurturing mother turned devouring mother, then a victim in her turn, threatened with being eaten. And for him this reversal is the heart of the myth: it gives the very model of the one suspected of evu, "at once devourer and always threatened with being devoured in turn." The myth does not only say "the woman sinned." It says that whoever makes the pact becomes its prisoner.

Remember the versions, too. In Belomo's, the woman goes hunting because the village is hungry and the provider is absent. And in the Evuzok variant, the starving evu is the son Zamba himself left without a wife or goods. An absent God, an excluded son, a famine: the fault is shared.

And we must say what this myth cost real women. The historian Bolo Mbala wrote in 2022 that this female guilt helps explain the murder accusations made, "often arbitrarily," against widows, and the abuses of the widowhood rite, the *akus*. Bochet de Thé, drawing on Ewondo testimony, explains that at a man's funeral the cause of death was sought among those who came from other clans: the wives, the slaves. And in the ordeal of the *mevungu*, reported by Laburthe-Tolra, women swore: "If I gave the evu to this or that child, mevungu, take me away!" The weight of the myth was carried by mothers.

There is a second question, just as honest: was this myth written by the missionaries? A woman, a prohibition, a forbidden fruit or a forbidden beast, a God who drives out or who departs: the resemblance to Adam and Eve is striking. Mary asserts that Christian influence is evident from the earliest known versions, through added elements and a shift in meaning. In Father Guillemin's version, God creates as he leaves "the pit of fire for the wicked": the Christian hell, very probably. But Tessmann, who recorded a Fang version very close to Genesis, refused to see it as the missionaries' doing. And the core of the story, the evu entering through the belly, the pact of game for a life, the hereditary evu, has no biblical equivalent. So the griot tells you this: some ornaments are Christian; the heart is Beti; and no one can date the myth.

What is certain is what the Church made of the evu. The Pallottine missionaries reached Yaoundé in February 1901 and fought the old rites, branded "devilries." Bolo Mbala shows that catechists and believers ended up calling the evu "Satan," and that in 1949, in the newspaper *Nleb Bekristen*, the Ewondo catechist Pierre Mebe developed that identification. Among the Fang, Mary describes the same thing: the evu turned into the tempting serpent, Lucifer, the king of evil. An ambivalent force, which also made healers and orators, was reduced to its dark face alone. Mary speaks of a demonisation "without appeal" of traditional forces, one that repeats, in its own way, the European history of the witch hunts. And the Evuzok returned the irony: the first Catholic missionaries, who did not marry, were called *dzongo*, after the barren evu.

The name Mingon, finally. Only the 2016 storyteller gives it. It sounds like Nyingone, whom the Fang of the Bwiti place, according to Mary, beside Nzame, "brother and sister or husband and wife of ambiguous status." It is only an echo, which the griot points out to you without drawing any conclusion.

### VII. Since Zamba went away

Since Zamba went away, the elders say, death is never quite natural.

The Cameroonian anthropologist Séverin Cécile Abega showed in his 1987 thesis on the *esana*, the great Beti funeral rites, that death appears there as a defeat, that of a man assailed and overcome by someone else's evu, and that the rite serves to detect and contain those forces. Bochet de Thé quotes the proverb: death does not come without its cause being sought. A Fang funeral song, recorded by Tessmann, put it with a wider sadness: "death is not of one man alone." And for generations, the bodies of some of the dead were opened to search for the evu: Tessmann saw it, and Ewondo women told Jeanne-Françoise Vincent about it. The myth did not stay by the fireside: it governed mourning, suspicion, lives.

It still does. Among the neighbouring Maka of the East, the anthropologist Peter Geschiere showed that since 1980, courts of the Cameroonian state have convicted people accused of witchcraft on the testimony of healers. He sees in this not a remnant of the past, but a face of modernity.

And the objects? The German colonial administration struck at Beti rites, and since 2024 the Museum Fünf Kontinente in Munich, with the National Museum of Cameroon, has been studying the carved group *Dzom So'o*, taken around 1904 near Yaoundé on the orders of the station chief Hans Dominik, at the very moment the So rite was banned. No object linked to the evu could be found in the museums' online databases. Tessmann's plate showing a healer searching a body for the evu has remained in his 1913 book; his collection went to the museum in Lübeck. The inquiry remains to be pursued in Yaoundé, at the Museum of the Peoples of the Forest.

So you who are listening, here is what I leave you.

This myth does not tell you that the woman is guilty. It tells you that a pact made in the shadows ends up claiming what you hold dearest. It tells you that what nourishes can devour, once sharing stops. It tells you that the same force makes the sorcerer and the healer, the orator everyone listens to, the brilliant child and the bold girl.

And it leaves you the sentence the maternal uncle whispered to the child whose power he was awakening: give people food, and love humankind.

Zamba went away. But what he left in your belly, it is up to you to say what it will become.

## Structured record

### Zamba, Mingon and the evu: why God went away

- **Content type**: cosmogonic myth from oral tradition (origin of death, of the evu power and of God's withdrawal). The myth is a legend; the beliefs, rites, accusations and trials linked to the evu are historically attested.
- **Peoples concerned**: Beti (Ewondo, Bënë, Eton, Evuzok of Lolodorf and their neighbours), Mvae, Bulu; related versions among the Ntumu and the Fang of Gabon and Equatorial Guinea (Nzame, Nsambe, evus).
- **Era**: precolonial (the myth cannot be dated); written versions from 1913 to 2016
- **Region**: Central Africa (Centre and South Cameroon; Gabon, Equatorial Guinea)
- **Theme**: other (cosmology, origin of death, thinking about evil and power)

**Timeline (history of the versions and of the belief, distinct from the myth)**

- 13 February 1901: the Pallottine missionaries reach Yaoundé and fight the rites they call "devilries."
- 1904-1909: Günter Tessmann's fieldwork among the Fang and Ntumu; *Die Pangwe* (1913) describes the withdrawn Nsambe, the evu and the ritual autopsy. Around 1904-1905, the carved group *Dzom So'o* is seized near Yaoundé on Hans Dominik's orders.
- 1930s (published 1943): Father Guillemin's version, collected in the Yaoundé country (*Cameroun Catholique*, no. 22), with the name Okombodo.
- 1949: the Ewondo catechist Pierre Mebe identifies the evu with Satan in *Nleb Bekristen*.
- 1961-1968: Lluís Mallart-Guimerà's fieldwork among the Evuzok; six versions of the myth published in 1981.
- 1982: L. Messi's version, *Ensemble* no. 90; 1985: Philippe Laburthe-Tolra's version; 2010: Alexandre Nana's version; 23 August 2016: version told in Ewondo by Elisabeth Belomo, Yaoundé (OMC), the only one to name Mingon.

**Debates and uncertainties**

- Christian influence: evident from the earliest versions according to A. Mary (hell, "the pit of fire for the wicked," in Guillemin); Tessmann refused to attribute his Genesis-like version to missionaries; the core (evu in the belly, pact of game for a life, hereditary evu) has no biblical equivalent. The myth cannot be dated.
- The guilty woman: "prototype of women who disobeyed patriarchy" (OMC record); mediator between nature and culture (Mallart); hunter-wife turned victim, model of the suspect (Mary); link with accusations against widows (Bolo Mbala). In other versions, famine or the son excluded by Zamba share the responsibility.
- Etymology of evu: *vu*, "to flourish" (Tsala, Nana); a root meaning "to kill" (Tessmann); *wu*, to die, or *vud*, to bend (reported by Bolo Mbala). Unresolved.
- Evu innate and hereditary (Mallart, Laburthe-Tolra) or a universal seed to be awakened (Tessmann, Atangana).
- Zamba as creator god or mythical ancestor; the name Okombodo in Guillemin, Nkombodo as a primordial being in Tessmann.
- The name Mingon is attested only in Belomo's version (2016); the echo with Nyingone of the Bwiti Fang is merely a resemblance of sound.
- No museum object linked to the evu could be consulted online (Berlin, Quai Branly and Lübeck databases inaccessible or yielding nothing).
- To consult in a library: Mallart-Guimerà 1981 (the six versions), Laburthe-Tolra 1985, Nana 2010, Mviena 1970, Tsala 1958, Guillemin 1943, Engudu Otu Mbala 1974.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The age without death or hunger, when Zamba lived in the village and brought back game.
- The two prohibitions: do not bury one who sleeps the "male sleep" (*nnom oyo*); do not take the evu.
- The swamp evu, Zamba's hunter, appearing as a toad or frog, entering the woman's belly.
- The devouring of the goats, sheep and hens, then of the daughter (or of all the children, in the Evuzok version).
- The sleep turned into death and the first burial; Zamba's departure to the sky or beyond the sea.
- The Evuzok variant of the evu as a son of Zamba excluded from the sharing of wives and goods.
