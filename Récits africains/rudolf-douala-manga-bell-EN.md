# Rudolf Douala Manga Bell

King of the Bell clan among the Duala, resistance leader against German colonial land expropriation, executed in Douala in 1914.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

More than a century ago, on the banks of the Wouri River, where fresh water meets the sea and where the canoes of Duala traders had been landing for generations, stood a kingdom no European map had invented: that of the Bell clan, one of the great lineages of the Duala people, founded long before a single German flag flew over these shores. In 1884, the king of that clan, Ndoumbè Lobè Bell, set his mark on a treaty with the German Empire. A treaty of trade and protection, people believed at the time, not a deed of sale. The text was clear, in black and white: the lands cultivated and the villages built would remain the property of their owners and their heirs. Remember that phrase. It will haunt the rest of this story.

It is into that world, around 1873, that a child of the Bell lineage is born: Rudolf Douala Manga Bell, son of King Manga Ndoumbè Bell, grandson of that same Ndoumbè Lobè Bell who had signed the treaty. Like many sons of chiefs on the Cameroonian coast, he is sent very young to learn the language and customs of the power settling among his people. He studies in Douala, then in Germany, at the Gymnasium of Ulm, boarding with a German family in Aalen where, it is said, he converts to Christianity. He goes as far as studying law, that weapon colonial powers use to dress their conquests in legality. Back home in 1896, he marries Emily Engome Dayas, daughter of an English trader and a Duala mother, and joins the German colonial judicial administration as a civil servant. He will even go on to preside over the court of appeal for the Cameroonian littoral. Look closely at this beginning: this is not the start of a rebel. It is the start of a man the empire shaped to serve its institutions from within.

On 2 September 1908, upon his father's death, Rudolf in turn becomes king of the Bell clan, formally installed on 2 May 1910. He manages cocoa plantations, timber interests, a modest but real fortune. Nothing, in the early years of his reign, hints at the man he is about to become.

And then, in 1910, everything turns. German Governor Theodor Seitz approves an immense urban redevelopment scheme called "Gross Duala," Greater Duala. The idea: push the entire Duala population away from the banks of the Wouri, onto less fertile, often unhealthy land, so as to hand the riverfront over to European quarters alone, separated by a buffer zone a kilometer wide. An urban planning project that is, in reality, a segregation project: erase from the map the people who had lived there since time immemorial, in the name of modernizing a colonial port.

Manga Bell rises up. He brings back out the 1884 treaty, the one his grandfather had signed, the one that promised the cultivated lands and the villages would remain the property of their inhabitants. He writes to the Reichstag, the parliament in Berlin, a first time in November 1911, a second time in March 1912. Silence. On 15 January 1913, the Germans carry out the plan regardless. On 20 February 1913, Manga Bell and other chiefs dare a written warning that was nothing trivial for the time: if Germany kept violating its commitments, they warned, they might seek a treaty with another power. An enormous, almost suicidal sentence, to the ear of a colonial empire in 1913. The German answer comes down in August of that same year: Manga Bell is stripped of his chieftaincy, his pension is cut, and the Germans install his own brother, Henri Lobè Bell, judged more docile, in his place.

A less stubborn man would have stopped there. Not Manga Bell. Stripped of his title, he keeps up the fight, this time by every means he knows. He allies himself with a German journalist, Hellmut von Gerlach, who in March 1914 secures a temporary suspension of the project from the Reichstag's budget commission, immediately overturned by the Colonial Secretary, Wilhelm Solf. He secretly sends his adviser Adolf Ngoso Din to Germany to hire a lawyer. He reaches out, through messengers, to other peoples of Kamerun: to Bali, Balong, Dschang, Foumban, Ngaoundéré, Yabassi, Yaoundé. He is attempting, according to some historians, to weave a resistance that would go beyond his own Duala people alone, one of the very first attempts at union among Cameroonian peoples in the face of the colonizer. Other, more cautious historians believe he was above all seeking diplomatic leverage, not an armed uprising. The archives do not allow for certainty, and I tell you so plainly rather than choosing the more spectacular version. What is certain is that he makes contact with Martin Paul Samba, a Bulu chief from the south, and that together they consider seeking foreign backing, French for one, British for the other. What is also certain is that the Bamum sultan Ibrahim Njoya refuses to join the plan and alerts the missionaries of the Basel Mission, who in turn warn the German authorities of a possible "pan-Kamerun" plot.

That summer, Europe slides into war. On 10 May 1914, Manga Bell and Ngoso Din are arrested, charged with high treason. Then comes the waiting, the dread, until history speeds up: in early August 1914, the First World War breaks out, and with it the fear, for the German administration of Kamerun, of seeing the colony attacked by French and British troops massed at its borders. In this climate of panic, on 7 August 1914, a trial is rushed through in secrecy, with no detailed transcript surviving to this day. It was not, several historians say today, a trial worthy even of the colonial standards of the time: a parody of justice, not justice. Voices rise to ask for clemency, that of the Catholic missionary Heinrich Vieter, that of the Basel Mission, that of the Baptists. Governor Karl Ebermaier rejects them all.

On 8 August 1914, in Douala, Rudolf Douala Manga Bell and Adolf Ngoso Din are hanged. That same day, hundreds of kilometers away, in Ebolowa, a German firing squad shoots Martin Paul Samba, in a separate but kindred case: the same anger, the same method, the same colonial hand striking at the very moment its grip is starting to slip. Because you must know this, and savor it as the bitterest of ironies: barely seven weeks after this hanging, on 27 September 1914, Douala falls into Allied hands. The man who had sought, through law, through diplomacy, through alliance between peoples, to bend an empire he already judged unstable, did not live long enough to see that empire lose its footing on his own land, mere weeks after it had killed him.

It is told, in popular memory and in a Cameroonian play devoted to his life, that he hurled terrible words at his executioners, a curse against the Germans, or a call for his people's unity before he died. I tell you this because these words, whether true or reconstructed, say something true about what his people wanted to remember of him. But I must be honest: no dated and signed testimony from the time of the trial has ever authenticated a single one of these phrases. Neither the trial record, which has been lost, nor any identifiable witness confirms them. These are words of legend, not lines from a transcript.

What is beyond doubt, however, is the legacy. From the 1920s onward, Manga Bell becomes a symbol, that of a king who chose to lose his title, then his life, rather than watch his people driven from their land without a response. In 1929, a song, "Tet'Ekombo," the king of kings, is composed in his honor and remains popular in Douala for decades. His own brother, Richard Ndoumbè Manga Bell, will carry on the fight after him to recover the lost lands, this time under French mandate. His son, Alexandre Douala Manga Bell, will in turn become chief in 1951, carried by the memory of his martyred father. In 1936, for the twentieth anniversary of his death, an obelisk is erected in Douala. Much later, in 2018, a Berlin district chooses to rename a square after him and his wife Emily. In 2022, a square is dedicated to him in Ulm, the very city where he had studied as a child, in the presence of his descendants: a symbolic gesture of recognition, but Germany, to this day, has neither formally overturned his conviction for high treason nor paid reparations to his family, despite repeated requests, including one brought in 2022 by his great-granddaughter.

So remember this man. A king born into the comfort of an established dynasty, shaped by the empire itself to become one of its loyal cogs, who chose, letter after letter, refusal after refusal, to stand with his own people rather than with those who had educated him. He did not win in his lifetime. He died barely seven weeks before German Kamerun began to collapse. But what he asserted, with the weapons of law and of the word rather than the rifle, still carries his name through the streets of Douala today. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Rudolf Douala Manga Bell**

- **Era**: colonial (German Kamerun, 1884-1916)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Douala, present-day Cameroonian littoral)
- **Theme**: resistance

**Timeline**

- c. 1873: born in Douala, son of King Manga Ndoumbè Bell and grandson of Ndoumbè Lobè Bell, signatory of the 1884 Germano-Duala treaty (some sources give 1872; most sources settle on 1873)
- 2 September 1908: succeeds his father as king of the Bell clan; formally installed on 2 May 1910
- 1910-1913: opposes Governor Theodor Seitz's segregationist urban plan "Gross Duala" (expelling the Duala from the banks of the Wouri); sends repeated letters to the Reichstag (1911, 1912); the plan is carried out on 15 January 1913 despite his protests; stripped of his chieftaincy in August 1913
- 1913-1914: continues the resistance after his removal, backed by German journalist Hellmut von Gerlach and his adviser Adolf Ngoso Din, seeking support from other peoples and chieftaincies of Kamerun (including Martin Paul Samba and Bamum sultan Ibrahim Njoya)
- 10 May 1914: arrested with Adolf Ngoso Din, charged with high treason
- 7-8 August 1914: tried in a rushed trial on 7 August, hanged together with Adolf Ngoso Din on 8 August 1914 in Douala (the same day, in a separate case, Martin Paul Samba is shot in Ebolowa)

**Notable quotes**

No quote from Rudolf Douala Manga Bell has been authenticated by a dated primary source: the record of his trial on 7 August 1914 has not survived, and no identified witness contemporary with the events recorded his last words. Two phrases circulate today but must be treated as unverified: a curse against Germany reported without any primary source by an advocacy website, and a call for unity drawn from a Cameroonian play inspired by his life (see "Associated legends"). Neither is treated here as an authentic quote.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- A curse against the Germans ("You are hanging innocent blood... may the Germans never again set foot on this soil") circulates on an advocacy website with no primary source cited: it is treated here as unauthenticated, never as an established quote.
- The last words "Restez unis et solidaires" ["Remain united and in solidarity"] come from a Cameroonian play, *Ngum a Jemea ou la foi inébranlable de Rudolph Duala Manga Bell* by David Mbanga (a work included in the Cameroonian school curriculum): this is a literary dramatization of his life, not a historical testimony. It should be told as a work of popular memory, never as an established fact.
- Rudolf's conversion to Christianity during his stay with a German family in Aalen is mentioned by only one of the sources consulted; it is reported here with that caveat.
