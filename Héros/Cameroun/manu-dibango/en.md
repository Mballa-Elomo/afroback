# Manu Dibango

Cameroonian saxophonist, singer and composer, a major musical figure of the twentieth and early twenty-first century, author of "Soul Makossa."

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

Douala, 12 December 1933. Cameroon is not yet a free country, it is a territory under French mandate, and its great port sees, arriving with the ships, records from elsewhere: French songs, American be-bop, Cuban sounds, unloaded by sailors alongside their cargo. In that city a child is born who is named Emmanuel N'Djoké Dibango. His father, Michel Manfred N'Djoké Dibango, a civil servant, belongs to the Yabassi people. His mother, a seamstress and choir leader at the Protestant church, is Douala. Two peoples that local history had long set against each other meet in this marriage. Before he can even walk, this child is already, without knowing it, a meeting point between two worlds that did not always speak to each other easily.

It is through his mother's singing, in the church choirs, that young Emmanuel discovers he has an ear for music. No one, at the time, imagines that this child's voice will one day become one of the most recognized sounds on the planet.

In 1949, he is fifteen years old. His parents decide to send him to study in France, a choice some Cameroonian families who could afford it made at the time, but one that remains, for a teenager, a wrench away from everything he knows. In his bag, history remembers a detail he himself would tell for the rest of his life: three kilos of coffee, a rare and expensive commodity at the time, brought along to pay his first months of lodging with a family in Saint-Calais, in the Sarthe region. Decades later, that bag of coffee would give its name to his autobiography. Hold on to that detail, it says a great deal: this boy does not arrive in France empty-handed, he arrives with the best his country has to offer, ready to pay his way.

He studies in Chartres, in Château-Thierry, then in Reims, where he prepares a baccalaureate in philosophy. It is in the city's nightclubs, in the evenings, that he takes up the saxophone, having touched the piano a few years earlier. In 1956, he fails his exam. His furious father cuts off his allowance. What could have remained a stinging failure becomes, without his knowing it yet, the moment music stops being a pastime and becomes a necessity.

At the end of 1956, with no money and no diploma, he leaves for Belgium. He plays in the orchestras of Brussels, Antwerp, Charleroi. In 1957 he marries Marie-Josée, a Belgian painter and model everyone will call Coco, who will remain at his side until her death in 1995.

And it is in Brussels that a man crosses his path and changes everything: Joseph Kabasele, known as Grand Kallé, a founding figure of modern Congolese music. In early 1960, as the Belgian Congo prepares to become independent, the Belgo-Congolese Round Table convenes in Brussels to settle the terms. Grand Kallé brings in his orchestra, African Jazz, to record a song meant to mark the occasion. A saxophonist is missing. Dibango, then based in Paris, is called in as reinforcement. He plays on that record, and that record becomes "Indépendance Cha Cha," the song all of Africa will take up as the informal anthem of its emerging independences. Understand what this means: at barely twenty-six years old, without yet grasping it himself, this young Cameroonian takes part in one of the most symbolic recordings in the entire history of modern Africa, the one that accompanies, in music, the birth of a free nation.

He then follows Kabasele and the African Jazz orchestra to Léopoldville, where he lives for several years and even launches the twist on local dance floors with "Twist à Léo." Then, in 1963, he returns to Cameroon full of ambition: he opens his own club, the Tam Tam. The venture fails, swept away by the political turmoil shaking the young independent Cameroon. It must be said plainly: this homecoming, conceived as a triumph, ends in commercial failure. The life of an African musician of this generation is not a straight line to glory, it is a series of bets, some won, some lost.

In 1965, he returns to France, almost starting from zero. He becomes a studio musician, a man in the shadows behind stars of French popular song such as Nino Ferrer and Dick Rivers. These are years of quiet, unglamorous work, far from the spotlight, in which he learns the workings of an industry that, at the time, had little room for a Cameroonian saxophonist except as a backing musician. In 1969, his album *Saxy Party* meets real success and brings him back into the light a little.

Then comes 1972. That year, Cameroon hosts the Africa Cup of Nations football tournament for the first time, and the national team reaches the quarter-finals. To mark the occasion, Dibango is commissioned to write an official anthem, set to words by the poet Eno Belinga. He delivers the A-side as requested. And then, almost as an afterthought, on the B-side of the very same record, he slips in a more personal, more danceable piece, sung in his mother tongue, Douala: "Soul Makossa." A song he does not even imagine will travel beyond Cameroonian circles.

Here, the story completely escapes its author's control. In New York, a record dealer in Brooklyn imports a handful of copies of the disc. A disc jockey named David Mancuso finds one and plays it at his legendary Loft parties. The enthusiasm quickly turns into obsession: the few available copies are snapped up, and Frankie Crocker, the most listened-to voice on New York's Black radio station WBLS, plays it on repeat. Demand explodes so fast that at least twenty-three different groups record their own version of the song to try to ride the wave, to the point that, at one moment, nine versions of "Soul Makossa" sit simultaneously on the American Billboard Hot 100 chart, where the original climbs as high as number seven. It is cited today as one of the very first disco records in history.

Picture the vertigo of it: a man who, a few years earlier, was playing in the shadows behind French pop stars, suddenly becomes, without ever seeking it, the author of one of the most copied and sampled African songs in the entire history of popular music. The chant he had tossed off almost in passing, "mama-se, mama-sa, mama-makossa," would cross decades and continents before he himself fully grasped what he had just brought into the world.

This refrain went on to mark global popular culture, for better and for worse. In 1982, Michael Jackson takes up that very same phrase, almost unchanged, in "Wanna Be Startin' Somethin'." Asked about it, Jackson openly acknowledges the borrowing and the matter is settled privately. Decades later, in 2007, Rihanna uses the same hook in "Don't Stop the Music," never crediting its author. Dibango then takes the matter to French courts, seeking 500,000 euros in damages from the record labels. The court dismisses his claim on procedural grounds in 2009, but a financial settlement is eventually reached. This is not a minor detail: it is the fight, carried through to the end of his life, of an African artist demanding that, in a global music industry that had long taken from the continent's music without giving anything back, authorship of a work has a price and a name.

American success alone could have filled a lifetime. For Dibango, it is only one chapter among many. In 1975, Ivorian president Félix Houphouët-Boigny, wanting to draw the best musicians of Francophone Africa to his country, entrusts him with directing the orchestra of Ivorian national radio and television. For four years, with resources he had never had before, Dibango builds one of the very first pan-African orchestras, bringing together Senegalese, Ghanaian, and Malian musicians (including the young Boncana Maïga), and accompanies the early career of talents such as Salif Keïta. Here is a man who, having known the failure of his own club in Douala, finds himself a few years later building, from Abidjan, a pool of talent for an entire continent.

The following decades see him perform all over the world, blending jazz, funk, traditional Cameroonian music, and electronic sounds, never letting himself be boxed into a single category. In 1994, he brings together on a single album, *Wakafrika*, some of the greatest African and world voices of his time: Youssou N'Dour, Papa Wemba, Salif Keïta, Angélique Kidjo, King Sunny Adé, the South African group Ladysmith Black Mambazo, and even the Briton Peter Gabriel. This record is not a mere collection of covers: it is proof that a Cameroonian musician based in Paris can gather, around his own table, the musical elite of an entire continent.

In 1995, Coco, his wife of nearly forty years, dies. The album *Lamastabastani* that follows carries the mark of that grief.

The new century brings a wave of recognition. In the early 2000s, he is crowned "Cameroonian of the Century," alongside footballer Roger Milla: two men whom sport and music had made known far beyond Cameroon's borders. In 2004, UNESCO names him an Artist for Peace. In 2010, France makes him a Knight of the Legion of Honour; he also becomes a Commander of the Order of Arts and Letters. In 2015, the International Organisation of La Francophonie names him Grand Témoin de la Francophonie for the Rio Olympic Games.

Asked in 2016 what he really was, this Cameroonian turned citizen of the world answered with a clarity that sums up an entire life: "I am Cameroonian by origin, but I do not claim to be a Cameroonian musician... I am a musician of Cameroonian origin." And he added, refusing to be locked into a single definition: "I am simply a musician, let's start there and leave it at that." On jazz itself, a label he had long been saddled with without ever fully recognizing himself in it, he said: "Jazz is a combination. They took away its feet, all that's left is the head." For him, music had to be danceable as much as listenable, never one without the other.

On 18 March 2020, his COVID-19 infection is announced publicly. Six days later, on 24 March 2020, he dies at the hospital in Melun, near Paris, at the age of eighty-six. He is buried on 27 March at Père-Lachaise cemetery in Paris, the very city that had seen him arrive, a teenager, with three kilos of coffee in his bag.

So hold on to this. Manu Dibango never took up arms, never led a country, never had to choose between life and death facing a colonial power. His fight was of another kind: to have it recognized, on stages and in courtrooms around the world, that music born in Douala was worth just as much as music born in New York or London, no more, no less. To turn a track slipped almost by chance onto the B-side of a record into a bridge between Africa and the entire world, without ever denying where he came from or claiming to belong to a single box. This boy who left with three kilos of coffee in his bag became one of the most played, most sampled, most copied African musicians in history. Listen to "Soul Makossa" today, listen to that "mama-se, mama-sa, mama-makossa" that has crossed generations without most of the people humming it knowing where it comes from. Now, you know. Stand tall. You come from there too.

## Reference sheet

**Manu Dibango (born Emmanuel N'Djoké Dibango)**

- **Era**: contemporary (1933-2020)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Cameroon), with international reach from France
- **Theme**: art (music)

**Timeline**

- 12 December 1933: born in Douala, Cameroon under French mandate, to a Yabassi father and a Douala mother
- 1949: leaves for France at fifteen, studies in Saint-Calais, Chartres, then Reims
- 1960: records "Indépendance Cha Cha" with Joseph Kabasele (Grand Kallé) and the African Jazz orchestra in Brussels, during the Belgo-Congolese Round Table on Congo's independence
- 1972-1973: composes "Soul Makossa" (originally the B-side of a record commissioned for the 1972 Africa Cup of Nations), which becomes a worldwide hit and one of the very first disco records
- 1975-1979: directs the orchestra of Ivorian national radio and television (RTI) in Abidjan, at the invitation of President Félix Houphouët-Boigny
- 24 March 2020: dies of COVID-19 at the hospital in Melun, near Paris, aged 86; buried at Père-Lachaise cemetery on 27 March

**Notable quotes**

- "I am Cameroonian by origin, but I do not claim to be a Cameroonian musician... I am a musician of Cameroonian origin." — interview given to the site Le Jazzophone, published 28 April 2016
- "I am simply a musician, let's start there and leave it at that." — same interview, Le Jazzophone, 28 April 2016
- "Jazz is a combination. They took away its feet, all that's left is the head." — same interview, Le Jazzophone, 28 April 2016

Other quotes are widely attributed to him on quotation websites (for instance "We are bridge-builders between the West and the Africas" or "Music must never be put in prison"), but without a date or interview context specified by those sites: they very likely are authentic, but for lack of a datable, verifiable primary source, they are not treated here as fully authenticated.

**Associated legends (if applicable)**

No legend or oral tradition surrounds Manu Dibango's life the way one surrounds a precolonial or colonial-era hero: his life is that of a contemporary figure abundantly documented by the press, sound archives, and his own autobiography. The one point requiring a caveat is not a legend but a matter of popular memory: several quotes attributed to him on quotation websites circulate without a dated primary source (see the quotes section above) and should be taken with that reservation rather than as strictly verified statements.

**Related heroes**

- Joseph Kabasele (Grand Kallé), founder of the African Jazz orchestra and author of "Indépendance Cha Cha," who brought Dibango to Brussels in 1960
- Roger Milla, Cameroonian footballer crowned alongside him "Cameroonian of the Century"
- Salif Keïta, Malian musician whose early career Dibango supported within the RTI orchestra in Abidjan
- Queen Nzinga (Njinga a Mbande) and Martin Paul Samba, already covered in the Stories & Heroes pillar, for different themes (resistance to colonization), showing the diversity of African figures beyond political resistance alone
