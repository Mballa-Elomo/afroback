# Ndzana Nga Zogo, the man who went to fetch his woman from the dead

A sung epic of the Eton people (Lékié, Centre Cameroon), with its Bulu cousins from the South. Version 1, 2026-10-16. The research file is in `recherche.md` (in French).

> This is an epic from the oral tradition, sung to the mvet. What happens in the land of the dead is legend, and the griot tells it as legend. But behind the legend there is a people, its rites, its way of thinking about death, and perhaps a man who really lived. At every step the griot will tell you what is known, what is told, and what no one can settle.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. The song that is never the same

Listen.

North of Yaoundé lies a land of round hills that roll down, one after the other, towards the Sanaga. It is called the Lékié, after a river that crosses it: Obala, Sa'a, Monatélé, Evodoula, Okola, Lobo, Elig-Mfomo. This is the land of the Eton, who call themselves Ìtón, or Bëti b'Ìtón: Beti, kin to the Ewondo, the Bënë, the Bulu and the Fang, who came like them from beyond the great river. A tradition reported by Barnabé Bilongo says their name comes from a tree, the *etondo*, under which the ancestors rested after crossing the Sanaga. I give it to you as it was given to me: a tradition, not a birth certificate.

The Eton had no king. They lived in exogamous clans, the *ayŏn*, divided into lineages, the *mvog*, and no one ever took a wife from his own. They counted their kinship down to the seventh generation. Every man was one link in a long chain of the living and the dead.

And when this people wanted to tell its own story, it sang. It sang to the *mvet*.

Look at the instrument: a stem of bamboo about a metre and a half long, four to six thin strips lifted from its skin and stretched over a notched bridge, gourds beneath it for a sounding box. Look at the man who plays it, the *mbom mvet*, "the one who touches the mvet". In the great performances of old, says the scholar Emmanuel Matateyou, he wore animal skins and feathers on his head, his body striped white, black and red, bells on his ankles, rattles on his calves. He is singer, storyteller, dancer, mime, and memory of the clan. And he always opens by declaring that what you are about to hear conforms to tradition. Not that it is true: that it conforms. A nuance Matateyou points out with a smile. For "a mvet performance is never the same, even when the same bard takes it up again the next day". The audience sings with him, rises, dances. It is a rite as much as a show.

There are several families of mvet. The *mvet ekang*, the great epic of the immortals of Engong and their king Akoma Mba. The *mvet bibón*, the lyrical mvet of love, from the word *ibón*, "lover". And a third kind, which Matateyou calls *mvet engubi*: fables, chronicles, recent legends, ordinary men thrown into extraordinary adventures.

That is where the story I am about to tell you lives. The Eton count it among their greatest epics, alongside *Nden Bobo* and *Nomo Nga Awono*. It has been sung in villages and on records, and in 1999 a whole conference of Cameroonian scholars made it their subject.

The singer's first line says it himself: "The epic of Ndzana Ngazo'o is an epic of ancestral times."

Keep that line in a corner of your mind. At the end, I will tell you why some people doubt it.

But first, hear the story of a man who loved a woman so much that he followed her to the land where no one goes alive.

### II. The man who bore his mother's name

Even his name is a riddle.

He is called Ndzana Nga Zogo, and Nga Zogo is his mother's name. Matateyou noticed it: the hero carries a feminine form of name, a break with the usual way names are handed down. Remember this. The whole epic is made of women who save, who bind, who come to claim.

Ndzana belongs to the Benyagda clan, which others spell Menyagda. It is not a clan invented for the song: an anthropology thesis from the University of Yaoundé I lists it among the Eton clans of the Lékié. He lives in a village where palm wine is tapped, palm nuts are sold and cocoa is planted, and the singers add schools and suits: the epic, says Matateyou, is not just modernised, it is "current".

His father has several wives. The mvet master Pantaléon, in the version he recorded in 2002, speaks of twenty-seven co-wives around Ndzana's mother. A singer's exaggeration, no doubt. But it says what matters: Ndzana grows up in a courtyard full of mothers.

Now here is the drama at the start, which some versions tell and others leave out. As a young man, Ndzana knows no girl. His mother asks one of her young co-wives to check, in secret, her son's manhood. The report is crushing. "He has the vigour of a sparrow," says Pantaléon's version. "He is *éyel*. Ndzana is impotent!"

In a society where the survival of the lineage is everyone's business, this is no private misfortune. So Nga Zogo weeps. Listen closely, for here is one of the few Eton lines of the epic we possess. The Eton scholar Joseph Ndzomo-Molé remembers a tape his father once recorded, and he quotes the mother's lament:

"*Ámú dzē mē kėgë ding ílig í ngòl? Hòököö! Ámú dzē mē kėgë ding ílig í kúbi? Hòököòò!*"

"Why, oh why did I love a wretched family? ... Why did I love a family in decline?"

Do you hear the cry? *Hòököö!* It is not a word, it is pain. "Very beautiful," Ndzomo-Molé simply adds.

But the mother does more than weep. Among the Beti, recalls Jean Bernard Evoung Fouda of the University of Yaoundé I, it is very often women who hold the knowledge of barks and herbs. Nga Zogo gathers her co-wives, each brings a bark, and from them a remedy is made, which some versions call a philtre.

And Ndzana is cured, beyond all measure. The singer has a field day: the young man now wears underpants with seven pockets, and in each pocket a piece of bark to keep up his strength. The versions part ways here. According to the philosopher Hubert Mono Ndzana, quoted by Evoung Fouda, some make him "a superman of eroticism, unable to discipline his instincts", others a former impotent whom his mother's co-wife heals, then "tries out herself", before making him her lover forever.

You already see the paradox the scholars pointed out. The man who bears a woman's name is saved by women. And it is through a woman that he will be lost.

I will tell you no more about his nights: the mvet is talkative, the griot less so. Remember only this. A 1999 conference called this tale "an epic of the flesh", and some of its authors saw in it a reflection of what informants, young and old, considered normal. Others read in it the sickness of a man who cannot hold himself back. The epic does not choose. It sings, and lets the village judge.

### III. The pact of the ring

And now, here is the one who will change everything.

She has several names, depending on the mouth that sings. In the text that the Eton bard Awono Ekassi recited to two researchers, Ernest Pierre Mani and the philosopher Marcien Towa, she is Agnès Abomo, a woman married to another man. In Pantaléon's version she is Abomo, the wife of Ndzana's uncle. In other versions, noted by Hubert Mono Ndzana, she is Minko, one of the widows of Ossongo, Ndzana's own father, and so a co-wife of his mother Nga Zogo: perhaps the very one who had healed him. Others still make her the widow of a village notable somewhere around Obala.

Don't ask which one is real. Each touches a rule of old Beti society, in which a dead man's wives could pass to his heirs. Evoung Fouda notes that in these variants the son goes on loving his mother's co-wife "as custom has it", and Matateyou wonders how Ndzana would have lived "with his many wives and his father's wives" had he stayed impotent. The epic plays at the edge of the rules. It brings together, says Matateyou, "the lawful and the unlawful".

What does not change from one version to the next is what binds Ndzana and Abomo. A pact.

Listen to the verses, in the French translation of Awono Ekassi's text. Abomo takes a ring of her own, a ring with a mysterious past, and slips it onto Ndzana's finger. The water in which the ring has soaked is drunk. And Ndzana swears:

"If ever I deceive you,
I shall answer to this ring
Whose water I drink this day.
But if ever you deceive me,
You shall answer to this ring,
For you women are faithless by nature."

Did you notice the last line? He is already accusing women of infidelity at the very moment he swears. The irony is fierce: he is the one who will betray. Then comes the most terrible clause:

"If I should die, you shall die too,
And if instead it is you who die,
I shall die as well."

In life and in death. In Pantaléon's version the two lovers say it "with one voice".

Evoung Fouda links this pact to a Beti practice which, according to him, Ewondo calls *mbourna*: a blood pact that makes two lives one, so that whatever happens to one happens to the other, death included. Blood is the vehicle of the soul, the ring a spiritual bond. And this pact has its songs: those of the first to die, when they come back to demand a reckoning from the one who betrayed. The neighbouring Basaa know it too: the novelist Charly Gabriel Mbock made it the heart of *Quand saigne le palmier*.

The dictionary of Father Théodore Tsala, published in 1956, has a word for this gravity: *oyílí*, agreement, pact. And to illustrate it, he quotes a sentence that seems to come straight out of our epic: "*Man békon ñe na: wōdań abe a mvog a bekon*", "and the ghost said: among us, in the abode of the dead, an agreement is most sacred".

In the abode of the dead, a pact is still a pact. That is the warning. Ndzana did not hear it.

For Abomo dies.

In Pantaléon's version she dies barely two days after the oath, and leaves Ndzana her ring. Elsewhere, the song does not say how.

And what does the man who swore to die with her do? He does not keep his oath, says Evoung Fouda, for "the space of a breath". Before Abomo is even in the ground, he has known other women. Some singers count five. The audience laughs, then shivers: everyone knows what happens to the one who betrays a blood pact.

Then the dead woman rises.

This is where the Eton epic parts ways with the stories you think you know. In the Greek myth, it is Orpheus, the living man, who goes to fetch Eurydice. Here, Evoung Fouda insists, the crossing does not run one way. It is the dead woman who first crosses the border between the invisible and the visible.

Some singers say she finds Ndzana perched at the top of a palm tree. In Pantaléon's version he is three leagues from home, tapping palm wine, when a voice rises:

"*Toli-toli...*"

It is Abomo's voice. And the voice asks: "What have you done with the pact?"

This song is no isolated invention. Evoung Fouda observes that Beti tales in which a ghost returns to claim its due after a broken pact almost always have a song, a river, often a large animal such as the elephant, and he quotes some refrains: "*Tcholi tcholi tcholi tcho tcholiii*", "*à moé é é, koum koum koum*". Usually the ghost comes in disguise. Abomo comes as herself. She claims her ring, singing. And there is nowhere to run at the top of a palm tree.

Why does she come back? For the Beti of old, says Evoung Fouda, death was not an end but a change of condition, a passage into an invisible world that is not far away. The dead keep their bonds and their rights. A betrayed dead woman has accounts to settle, and the ring is her share.

Ndzana has two choices. Give back the ring and break the pact. Or keep his word, and go where Abomo has gone.

He chooses to go.

### IV. The road to the land of ghosts

How does a living man go to the dead?

One variant says Ndzana dies first, since the pact required him to die with her. Evoung Fouda notes that this version "is much debated". Most storytellers, and he himself, prefer the other: Ndzana sets out alive, of his own free will. It is madder. That is how I will tell it.

In Pantaléon's version, the departure tastes of everyday life. Ndzana sells palm nuts and buys himself "underpants with seven pockets, a crumpled suit, a shoe like a heel". No armour, no magic spear: a crumpled suit. The *mvet engubi* loves heroes who are not giants.

His wife weeps, for Ndzana has a lawful wife. He leaves her a brutal sentence: "here's a cocoa farm, and the peace I'm damn well leaving you as I go". The unworthy husband is sung so that he may be judged. And his mother, the one who had saved him, pronounces a sentence that sounds like a prophecy:

"Every passion is a murderer. You will die for having loved a widow. So be it."

Then he leaves. And the road changes with the mouth that sings it.

A storyteller named Onguéné, whose version Evoung Fouda quotes, tells the journey almost step by step. One morning, before sunrise, Ndzana sets off to find "the Unforgettable One". He crosses lands that seem uninhabited. He drinks from streams, up to the fifth, out of strange vessels that astonish him. He sees wonders. He passes indifferent travellers and pays them back with his own indifference. And so he reaches the village of the dead.

Another storyteller, Nke Assolo, is briefer and more unsettling. Ndzana takes seven steps on the path to the stream, the path to the water that everyone walks every day. At the seventh step, he finds himself in a world of wonders.

Seven steps. The land of the dead is not at the end of the world: it is at the end of the path to the water. Evoung Fouda speaks of two worlds "not far apart". The elders, he says, blamed ghosts for certain mysterious disappearances of objects or children, and suspected some patriarchs and healers of having a foot in each world.

In Pantaléon's version, finally, Ndzana walks to "the crossroads of a thousand villages", where Abomo stops him. A crossroads. At the start of the twentieth century, the German ethnologist Günter Tessmann, who did fieldwork from 1904 to 1909 among the Fang, Ntumu and Bulu, cousins of the Beti, noted that the souls of the dead like to linger at crossroads. I, the griot, am the one making the connection. But it is hard not to hear it.

For you must know what the world Ndzana is entering looked like to the elders. Tessmann, whose gaze remains that of a colonial man of his time and whose vocabulary I never repeat, gathered remarkable words. When a man lies dying, the souls of the recently dead come to fetch him. The dying man says: "There they are, there, and there." People look and see nothing. Souls have the lilac colour of shadows; they live in the trunks of very large trees, in the plantains behind the houses, in abandoned villages, in the water. Among themselves they are "one single friendship". And they are hungry and thirsty.

The word Tessmann writes *kun* is *kón* in Ewondo: ghost, shade, spirit of a dead person, according to Tsala, and *kon* is the singular of *bekoñ*, the dead, the ancestors, explains the historian Martial-Jacques Mendouga Tabi. The Eton say that Ndzana went *pōt á kón*, to the land of ghosts, and Ndzomo-Molé gives that land a name: Ingönkēng. Tsala also knows a river, *Endám*, "river of the underworld, of the land of the dead". The same word named the place that candidates for certain initiations had to cross on a pitch-black night, such as the *melan*, the ancient society that protected against sorcerers and which, according to other accounts, kept the skulls of the ancestors in a reliquary box. As if, to be initiated, one had to spend a night passing through the river of the dead. Tsala even names a chief of that land, *Zaméyo*, "chief of the shades", whose daughter the tortoise of a folktale went to ask for in marriage. No version of Ndzana I have read brings him in: I name him only so you know that the Beti beyond had its chief.

There is yet another version, from the South, among the Bulu of Sangmelima, which the researcher Eleanor Dasi of the University of Yaoundé I recorded on 4 January 2019 from the storyteller Anatole Ikuana. There the man is called Dzàna Ngazok, and the woman he goes to fetch is his wife, Angonomane, whom he loves and who has died. After the funeral he takes a machete, a spear and a dog. He walks for days, through hills, forests and bush, to the bank of a river: the Yom, the border between the living and the dead.

The Yom. That is the name the elders gave the Sanaga in the story of the great crossing on the back of the serpent Ngan Medza. The same name for the river you cross to become a people and the river you cross to become one of the dead. But only the Bulu version says so, and nothing I have read identifies the Yom with the Endam of the Ewondo dictionary: I will not confuse them.

On the bank of the Yom, Dzàna knows a secret. A living person may enter the land of the dead if he washes his face with water that has first washed the face of a dog. He washes his dog's face, washes himself with that water, and crosses. Why the dog? The storyteller does not explain, and I will not invent it. Keep the question. Great stories always leave a door ajar.

### V. The couscous that never ends

And here he is.

Abomo is there. In Awono Ekassi's version, she welcomes her lover the way a man is welcomed in the village: with food. A dish of cassava couscous, that thick paste of cooked cassava flour eaten with sauce. And this couscous never runs out. In Pantaléon's version, Ndzana gives her back the ring, she strikes with a stick, and "the pot filled up again with couscous!" He eats, and there is always more.

Then comes palm wine. In Pantaléon's words, "the incense caught fire, Ndzana's senses blazed". Then comes the night, which the singers describe with an excess I leave you to imagine: Mono Ndzana speaks of sheets in shreds and broken bed legs. It is the reunion of two lovers whom death has not parted. Or so they believe.

Pause on this meal. Why, among the dead, is food offered first?

Because, for the Beti of old, the dead are hungry. Evoung Fouda recalls it: food and drink are laid on the grave, for the dead are hungry and thirsty like the living, and that portion is theirs. Tessmann had noted it a century earlier: people left for the dead an old pot, a gourd, a piece of sugar cane at the first fork of the path, and the hungry dead came in dreams to ask for food. And the historian Mendouga Tabi describes Beti funeral meals: the *ndam-awu*, the feast the dead man's family offers to people from every village, a sacrifice which, in Beti thought, "conditions the official reception of the deceased into the community of the beyond"; and the goat of the *kabat osa*, eaten in the evening as a "communal meal of the living and the dead".

Food is the bridge between the two worlds. The living feed the dead so that they may be received among their fathers. In the epic, it is the dead woman who feeds the living man. The world is turned upside down.

But there is a trap in this endless dish, and it is the Bulu version from Sangmelima that reveals it.

There, Angonomane is astonished to see her husband arrive alive. He tells her: I have come to take you home. She agrees, but first she must receive him. She offers him her bed to sit on, and the bed stretches on without end. He thinks his eyes are deceiving him. Then she serves his favourite dish: cassava couscous with *kpwem*, the thick sauce of cassava leaves. Dzàna is a big eater. He dips his hand in, and the food multiplies as he eats. We will leave, she says, when you have finished your meal.

He never finishes. He never came back.

And the Beti-Fang of Sangmelima draw a law from it: marriage does not continue in the land of the dead.

Hear the difference. Among the Eton, the endless couscous is the abundance of reunion. Among the Bulu, the same couscous is a prison: a meal you cannot finish is a departure that never comes. In the land of the dead, things no longer end, and whoever enters alive cannot find the way out.

This motif exists elsewhere, and I offer it as a comparison, not a kinship. In the Greek Hymn to Demeter, Persephone eats a few pomegranate seeds among the dead, and that is enough to bind her to return there every year. In Japan, in the eighth-century *Kojiki*, Izanami can no longer follow her husband Izanagi because she has already eaten the food of the land of the dead. No one borrowed from anyone. But wherever people have mourned their dead, they have had the same intuition: to eat among the dead is to begin to belong to them.

### VI. The husband's wrath

But let us go back to the Eton, for Ndzana has forgotten one thing. Abomo had a husband. That husband is dead too. He is there, in the village of ghosts. And he has forgotten nothing.

In Awono Ekassi's version, the moment the husband steps over the threshold, Ndzana "began to tremble like a leaf tossed by a gust of wind". The husband wipes his eyes seven times, to be sure it really is his rival he sees there. Then he speaks:

"What nerve, Ndzana Ngazo'o! When I was alive, you had a wife of fairy-tale beauty, and you abandoned her to come and snatch mine after death. And as if the delights you had with her were not enough, you chose on top of that to follow her all the way to us ghosts to carry on the affair. Enough is enough!"

And Ndzana, says the text, does not know what to answer.

Ndzomo-Molé, for his part, remembers an Eton line he heard in the old version his father recorded, the stunned complaint of the cuckolded husband: "*O sō et tong ma pōt á kón i?*" "You followed me all the way to the land of ghosts?" Do you hear the humour, and the pain inside the humour? Betrayed in life, betrayed after death, and pursued by the lover all the way to the dead: is there nowhere a man can rest in peace?

Then comes the punishment. In Awono Ekassi's version, the husband gives Ndzana a thrashing that knocks him senseless. In Pantaléon's, the justice of the dead even has its officer: Abenélanga, the policeman of the spirits, who "grabbed a truncheon and beat Ndzana". A policeman in the land of ghosts: this is my reading, but the modern singer has set up in the beyond the order he knew on earth. Tessmann already noted that his informants imagined life among the dead on the earthly model, with its villages and its chief.

How does Ndzana get out of it? The versions differ.

In Awono Ekassi's version, it is Abomo herself who saves him. Seeing her lover almost at death's door, she "resolved to send him home by showing him the boulevard that had led him to the land of ghosts. With unsteady steps, the young man returned to the world of humans." The dead woman who called him sends him back. But on one condition, Evoung Fouda specifies: the husband let him go only if he came back later to pay what he owed.

In the old version Ndzomo-Molé remembers, the ending is more surprising, and more beautiful still. While the husband beats Ndzana, the ghosts look on. And these ghosts, he says, "had followed the tunes of a piece by Mbida Kouma". Their hearts grew merry. And the beaten man was pardoned.

Pause on this ending: it is dizzying. Mbida Kouma, as we shall see, is the name of the musician said to have composed the song of Ndzana. The singer puts himself into his song, and it is his music that saves the hero among the dead. Orpheus, in the Greek myth, charms the powers of the underworld with his lyre. Here it is not the lover who plays, it is the mvet player. A resemblance, not a borrowing, but it says what peoples everywhere have believed about music.

So Ndzana returns to the living. The philosopher Mono Ndzana noted it with a smile: apart from the "necessarily approximate" revelations of the village seer, only Ndzana Ngazo'o, back on "the land of conversation", has been able to tell of his stay in the beyond. The land of conversation: the world where people talk, where stories are told. That is where the story will be knotted one last time.

### VII. The essié of the Benyagda

Ndzana has come back, but broken, dying. And when a man is dying and no one knows why, the Beti summon the clan. Listen to Awono Ekassi's verses, quoted by Evoung Fouda:

"I tell you that Ndzana Ngazo'o
is about to die without ritual care [...]
Then Kogo Bikele began to beat the drum,
The calling drum which said,
in a manly voice, that Ndzana Ngazo'o was dying.
All the Benyagda came
crying out in anger."

The calling drum "said" the news, and that day it carries Ndzana's name to every hill. The whole clan rushes in, angry: if one of their own is dying, someone must have wanted it.

What begins then is the *essié*, which Evoung Fouda calls "the healing palaver": a great assembly gathered only in times of grave danger, a deadly illness, unexplained misfortunes, a lineage threatened with extinction. The patriarchs summon the village, the clan, sometimes the whole region. And the palaver has three stages.

First, suspicions are cleared: an unpaid debt, an adultery not forgiven, a jealousy. The epic stages this moment with cruel truth. The Benyagda turn on Yana, Ndzana's brother:

"It is you who want to kill Ndzana.
Though you are still a young man,
your hair is already all grey.
Is that not a sign of sorcery?"

Yana shrugs and turns the accusation on someone else:

"I am not the cause of this man's death.
Ask rather Awono, the nephew of the girl Esele,
who might inherit his cocoa farm."

A cocoa farm: the most modern suspicion there is. Cocoa has been the great wealth of the Eton since the German colonial period. The old song speaks of its own time.

If you have read AFROBACK's story of Zamba and the evu, you know what is at stake here: among the Beti a death is almost never natural, people look behind it for the hidden force, the *evu*, of a jealous living person, and in the past the great funerals of men also served to search for culprits. I do not retell that myth here; it has its own story.

Then comes confession. The sick man must say himself what is causing his illness. But the assembly already knows:

"Let the sick speak for themselves!
They called out to Ndzana Ngazo'o and said:
Is it a Mbele woman who is the cause of your illness?
Who is she?
You ate the couscous.
Ndzana denied it."

"You ate the couscous." The whole village knows where he has been. And Ndzana denies it. Evoung Fouda sees fear in this: Abomo is still after him, and he must go back to pay the husband what he owes. So he lies, and even cleverly turns the ritual words to his own advantage. The hero remains a man, to the very end.

Finally, the healing rite. Ngandeme, the master of ceremonies, takes a small pebble:

"He took the little pebble, sucked it nine times [...]
The Benyagda numbered one hundred people.
They all touched the pebble
Before passing it last of all to Ngandeme,
who said: Here is Ndzana whom I deliver to you.
I treat him for his healing."

A hundred hands on a pebble. The whole clan passes the little stone from hand to hand, until it comes back to the healer. No other remedy is given, Evoung Fouda stresses: the healing comes from the assembly. Matateyou reads a profound idea in it: in a society founded on sharing goods and deeds, even the guilty man is seen as the victim of a collective ill, which is why everyone must bear witness, and everyone must heal.

And Ndzana recovers his health.

But that is not the only ending, and an honest griot hides none from you.

In Awono Ekassi's version, he comes back ill, and the essié of the Benyagda saves him.

In Pantaléon's, he comes back, starts telling of his journey to the beyond, and then, the text simply says: "He died after telling this story." Pantaléon ends with the funeral: "Among the Beti, mourning is a festival during which the frenzied rhythm of the esani is played." The word *esani*, according to Mendouga Tabi, comes from the verb *san*, "to leap for joy", "to exult after victory": a man's great funeral celebrates his deeds to the sound of drums, and according to Pierre Mviena, author of a major book on the Beti religious world (1970), the ancestors themselves come to attend, an invisible presence that helps the dead man enter "the sacred community of his fathers". Ndzana, who had already seen the land of the dead, enters it for good, to the rhythm of the dance.

Among the Bulu of Sangmelima, he never comes back. He is still there, on an endless bed, before a dish that never ends.

Healed by his own. Dead after telling his tale. Lost forever. Which is the true ending? Each singer chooses the one that speaks to his audience. Perhaps that is the mvet's greatest lesson.

### VIII. A man, a song, a mirror

I promised to come back to the first line: "The epic of Ndzana Ngazo'o is an epic of ancestral times." Here is why some people doubt it.

In 2023, the Eton scholar Joseph Ndzomo-Molé, a senior lecturer and author of a book on Itón cultural identity, published a striking testimony. According to him, Ndzana Ngazogo was a real man. "He is not a fictional character, even if he has become mythical over the years," he writes. A Menyagda whose house still stands, near Obala. His son, says Ndzomo-Molé, was born around the same year as himself, 1957, and lives beyond Minkama. And Ndzana himself, as he remembers him, would have been born in the late 1910s or early 1920s.

So where does the song come from? Still according to this testimony, it all began with a very real love affair between Ndzana and a lady, as told to him by an elder of his father's who had settled in Obala in the 1940s. A cousin of Ndzana's in Minkama, named Mbida Kouma, was a great mvet player. From this affair he made a song, which he reworked over time, and his brother, Menono Ekassi, also a virtuoso, made other versions of it. It was they who invented the idea that Ndzana had carried his affair all the way to the land of ghosts.

And here is the most delicious part. At first Ndzana was furious with his two cousins. Then, seeing that the song had made him famous, he came to love it, and happily danced to it himself. Imagine: a man dancing to his own legend while the whole country sings that he went to the dead.

According to Ndzomo-Molé again, the song of Ndzana is "the brilliant adaptation" of a Fang tale that was very popular in his childhood: the tale of Abomo Ngele, "daughter of Ngele Minsanga", which also tells of a love affair carried all the way to the land of ghosts. He quotes its first line: "*Abómo Ngele, ngön i Ngele Minsanga, a ngá nöng ma nlem ya abum...*", "Abomo Nguele, daughter of Nguele Minsanga, has seized the heart of my belly..." The beloved's name, Abomo, is already there.

What should we make of it? What I know: Ndzomo-Molé's testimony is that of a man from the area, a scholar, speaking of what he knew as a child. It explains a great deal: the real clan, the cocoa farm, the suit, the policeman, everything the scholars at the 1999 conference had noticed when they wondered whether this tale was really an epic. The critic Gabriel Kuitche Fonkou sums up their doubts: a short text, thin on the marvellous, a hero "of no great stature", a strong modern presence. Most of them concluded that it was an epic after all. And the anthropologist Jean-Pierre Ombolo, quoted by Ndzomo-Molé, held it to be the most popular song of its genre.

What I do not know: I have found only one written testimony about the real man, and I cannot cross-check it. The dates it gives come from memory. I cannot establish a link between Menono Ekassi and the bard Awono Ekassi, whose text was published in 1999, even though the names are alike. And that the "Oncle Pathaléon" mentioned by Evoung Fouda is the master Andjeng Etaba Pantaléon, whose record *L'homme Mvet* came out in Paris in 2002, is likely, but I have not proven it.

So here is how the griot tells it. Ndzana Nga Zogo may be a man who lived in the twentieth century in the hills of Obala. His journey to the dead is a legend, made of very old materials: the blood pact, the dead woman who returns to claim her due, the path to the water, the food of the dead, the palaver that heals. Perhaps that is the genius of the mvet: to make a hero out of a neighbour, and a meditation on death out of a village affair.

For that is what it is. The tale says first that a pact binds beyond death: in the abode of the dead, says the ghost in Tsala's dictionary, an agreement remains sacred. It says next, in the Bulu version, that marriage ends at the grave: we do not follow the dead, we mourn them, feed them, celebrate them with the esani, and then let them go to join their fathers. It says, in the Eton version, that no one heals alone: it is the assembly that saves. And it says finally, laughing, that desire without measure leads all the way to the end of the path to the water. Ndzana's mother had foretold it: "Every passion is a murderer."

You have probably been thinking of Orpheus since the beginning. So did Evoung Fouda and Eleanor Dasi. The resemblance is real: a living man, a beloved dead woman, a water crossing, music that moves the beyond, a failure. But the differences are more beautiful. Among the Eton, the dead woman returns first. It is not a backward glance that undoes the hero, but a jealous husband, or a dish without end. And the music that saves is the singer's.

In Ghana, the Asante tell the story of Kwasi Benefo, retold by the writer Clyde W. Ford in *The Hero with an African Face*. Kwasi loses four wives. A dream sends him to Asamando, the land of the dead. On the bank of a deep river, Amokye, the old woman who receives the cloths and beads in which the dead are dressed, moved by his grief, lowers the waters to let him cross. His wives give him food and drink, but he can only hear them. They promise to show themselves to him when he dies, and he goes home, remarries, and lives long. The same images, another wisdom: among the Asante, the dead wives send the man back to life; among the Bulu, the dead wife keeps him. No one copied anyone. Different peoples looked at the same riddle, and each answered in its own voice.

Today Ndzana Nga Zogo still lives: on mvet records, in musical adaptations such as that of the artist Evoung Fouda calls Oncle Pathaléon, in a university book published in Yaoundé in 1999, in the memory of the Eton, and in the mouths of the Bulu storytellers of Sangmelima. Evoung Fouda sees in this revival a form of cultural resistance, a way of remembering what the missionaries had tried to erase: the conviction that the visible and invisible worlds are neighbours, and that you can pass from one to the other in seven steps.

So you who are listening, here is what I leave you.

Do not swear lightly. Do not follow the dead: honour them, then let them go. And if one day life breaks you, do not be ashamed to call your people to the sound of the drum. The hundred hands of the clan are worth more than the greatest of heroes.

And remember Ndzana Nga Zogo, the man with a woman's name, a crumpled suit and underpants with seven pockets, who went all the way to the crossroads of a thousand villages for a ring, and came back, or never came back, depending on who is singing.

## Structured profile

### Ndzana Nga Zogo, the man who went to fetch his woman from the dead

- **Content type**: epic sung to the mvet (*mvet engubi* genre according to Matateyou), from Eton oral tradition, with a Bulu version recorded in 2019. The journey to the land of the dead is legend. The rites mentioned (blood pact, offerings to the dead, essié, esani) are practices attested by ethnographers. The existence of a real man named Ndzana Ngazogo rests on a single testimony (Ndzomo-Molé, 2023).
- **Peoples concerned**: Eton (Lékié: Obala, Minkama, Benyagda/Menyagda clan); Bulu (Sangmelima); shared Beti-Fang heritage.
- **Era**: precolonial for the motifs (undatable); colonial / independence era for the song itself (man born around 1915-1925 according to the testimony; published versions from 1999 to 2019).
- **Region**: Central Africa (Centre and South Cameroon)
- **Theme**: other (the afterlife, death and mourning, pacts, oral literature)

**Timeline (history of the tale, distinct from the legend)**

- 1904-1909: Günter Tessmann describes, among the Fang, Ntumu and Bulu, the souls of the dead, their hunger, their crossroads, their village with Nsambe (*Die Pangwe*, 1913).
- Late 1910s or early 1920s: probable birth of Ndzana Ngazogo, a Menyagda from near Obala, according to J. Ndzomo-Molé's memory (not cross-checked).
- During Ndzana's lifetime (date unknown): Mbida Kouma, his cousin, a mvet player in Minkama, composes the song; his brother Menono Ekassi makes other versions (according to Ndzomo-Molé).
- Date unknown, before 1999: the Eton bard Awono Ekassi recites the epic to Ernest Pierre Mani and Marcien Towa; a 741-line text is published in French translation in E. Matateyou (ed.), PUY, 1999, with 18 studies.
- 2002: the record *L'homme Mvet* by the Eton master Andjeng Etaba Pantaléon (Arion, Paris), including "L'épopée de Ndzana Nga Zogo".
- 4 January 2019: Bulu version of Dzàna Ngazok told by Anatole Ikuana in Sangmelima, recorded by Eleanor A. Dasi (OMC).

**Notable quotations (reported words, verified sources)**

- "If I should die, you shall die too, / And if instead it is you who die, / I shall die as well." Pact of Ndzana and Agnès Abomo, lines 169-171 of the Awono Ekassi corpus, quoted in French by J. B. Evoung Fouda, *Le Courrier de la SIELEC* no. 10.
- "*Ámú dzē mē kėgë ding ílig í ngòl? Hòököö!*" ("Why, oh why did I love a wretched family?") Nga Zogo's lament, quoted from memory by J. Ndzomo-Molé, Africa-Press Cameroun, 15/03/2023.
- "*O sō et tong ma pōt á kón i?*" ("You followed me all the way to the land of ghosts?") The husband's words, same source.
- "Every passion is a murderer. You will die for having loved a widow. So be it." Ndzana's mother, Pantaléon's version (record *L'homme Mvet*, 2002, text reproduced on savoirfairekang.com).
- "He is not a fictional character, even if he has become mythical over the years." J. Ndzomo-Molé, 2023.
- "A mvet performance is never the same, even when the same bard takes it up again the next day." E. Matateyou, *Littérales* no. 29, 2002, p. 339.

**Debates and uncertainties**

- Ancestral myth or twentieth-century song? The first line says "an epic of ancestral times"; Ndzomo-Molé states that Ndzana existed and that the song was composed in his lifetime, adapted from a Fang tale, *Abomo Ngele*. A single testimony, not cross-checked. The 1999 conference debated the text's epic status (brevity, modernity) and mostly concluded that it is an epic.
- The woman's identity varies: Agnès Abomo, a married woman (Awono Ekassi); Abomo, the uncle's wife (Pantaléon); Minko, the father's widow (variants cited by Mono Ndzana); Angonomane, the lawful wife (Bulu version).
- Does Ndzana die before going? A debated variant; most versions have him set out alive.
- Several endings: healing through the essié (Awono Ekassi); death after telling the tale (Pantaléon); pardon granted by ghosts charmed by Mbida Kouma's music (old version according to Ndzomo-Molé); no return (Bulu version).
- The Yom as river of the dead appears only in the Bulu version; no source read identifies the Yom with Tsala's *Endám*.
- The term *mbourna* (blood pact) is given only by Evoung Fouda; not found in the other sources.
- "Oncle Pathaléon" (Evoung Fouda) and Andjeng Etaba Pantaléon: probably the same person, not proven. Link between Menono Ekassi and Awono Ekassi: not established.
- The original Eton text has never been published (the 1999 book gives only the French translation). To consult in a library: Matateyou (ed.), 1999; Ch. Seidou, *Cahiers de littérature orale* no. 48, 2000; J. Ndzomo-Molé, *L'identité culturelle Itón*.
