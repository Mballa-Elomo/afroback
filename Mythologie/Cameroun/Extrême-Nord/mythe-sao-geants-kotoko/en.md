# The Sao, the Giants of Lake Chad, and the Kotoko Cities That Remember Them

The legend of the Sao "giants" and the historical memory of the Kotoko, Lake Chad plain (Far North Cameroon, Chad, Nigeria). Version 2, rewritten in depth on 2026-10-15 (the previous French version is archived in `archives/fr_v1.md`, the research file is `recherche.md`).

> This story weaves together two things that must never be confused. The Sao giants are a legend of oral tradition. The fortified towns, the burial jars, the bronzes and the wars against Bornu are history, attested by archaeology and by written chronicles. At every step, the griot tells you which ground he is walking on.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. The mounds that speak

Listen.

In the far north of Cameroon, the land grows so flat you would think God had smoothed it with his palm. Not a hill, not a rock. Sand, black clay that cracks open in the dry season, grasses, thorn trees. And then the water. The Logone coming down from the south, the Chari coming in from the east, the two of them meeting near Kousseri and N'Djamena and flowing on together to lose themselves in the great lake. Lake Chad. A freshwater sea that breathes: it swells, it shrinks, it advances, it changes its face over the centuries.

When the water rises, the plain turns to marshland as far as the eye can see. People here call these flooded grasslands the *yaéré*. And in that flat immensity, there is something strange.

Mounds.

Low hills resting on the plain like the backs of giant tortoises. A few metres high, sometimes more, sometimes several hundred metres long. Some are deserted, covered in grass. Others still carry towns. But they did not rise out of the earth on their own. Scratch their sides and you find potsherds, ash, fish bones, fired clay. These hills were made by people, layer upon layer, generation after generation, living in the same place for centuries, rebuilding their earthen houses on the ruins of their fathers' houses, to stay above the water when the river overflowed.

And in the riverbanks, when the flood has gnawed the shore away, you sometimes see the rounded belly of a jar pushing out of the earth. An enormous jar. Big enough to hold a person curled up inside.

Ask the people of the plain who made these mounds, who buried these jars. For generations they have given the same answer, in a single word.

The Sao.

And if you ask who the Sao were, the storyteller straightens up, and his voice changes. Because the Sao, he says, were not people like us.

They were giants.

I am going to tell you both stories. The one the elders tell, and the one the earth finally gave back to the scholars. They do not look alike. But you will see that they answer one another, and that the greater of the two is not necessarily the one you think.

Before we begin, hold on to this, because everything else depends on it. Today, along the Logone and the Chari, live peoples known as the Kotoko. They inhabit towns that were once ringed by earthen walls: Makari, Goulfey, Afadé, Kousseri, Logone-Birni and others, in Cameroon, Chad and Nigeria. And every one of the Kotoko says the same thing: the Sao are our ancestors. The linguist Henry Tourneux, who spent years studying their speech, puts it plainly: present-day Kotoko all claim descent from the Sao.

So when a Kotoko speaks of giants, he is not speaking of vanished strangers. He is speaking of his own family. That is why this story deserves to be told with care.

### II. The word of the elders: men as tall as palm trees

Come closer to the fire. What follows is not history. It is legend, as it was collected across the whole lake basin and published by those who heard it. I will tell you where each piece comes from.

In 1943, the ethnologist Marcel Griaule gathered into a book, *Les Sao légendaires*, the stories he had heard during his missions in Chad and Cameroon. Listen to what he was told about the Sao, word for word:

"The Sao were so tall that their bow-staves were made of whole palm trees, that their drinking cups, as big as funeral jars, could hold two men sitting down. They fished without nets, damming the rivers with their hands. They caught hippopotamuses by hand and devoured them like chickens. They announced their turn to fish by shouting from one town to the next, and their voices rolled all the way to the lake like thunder, sending every bird fleeing from the trees. Their nails were so thick that they resisted rot, and some are said to be dug up in the mounds to this day (...). In their hair, birds of prey made their nests."

Eagles' nests in their hair. Hippopotamuses eaten like chickens. Rivers dammed with bare hands.

Thirty-six years later, the great Cameroonian historian Engelbert Mveng, in his history of Cameroon published in Yaoundé in 1979, passed on other images from the same family: these giants "used the trunks of palmyra palms as walking sticks and ate, in a single meal, a load of millet carried by a camel." And he added a detail children love: "When a Sao chief of Goulfey sneezed, the birds for a league around flew off like a gust of wind."

The legend does not stop at size. It also explains the landscape. According to stories reported by Griaule and taken up by the historian Manga Makrada Maïna, it was Sao women who carved out the Chari, the Logone and the Yobe: dancing at a ceremony, they pressed the earth down beneath their feet, and the water came to fill their footprints.

And where did these giants come from? Here the voices multiply. Some storytellers say from the east, from the Nile, from the Egypt of the pharaohs. Others say from Yemen, or from Mecca. Others again weave the Sao into the stories of the Bible and the Qur'an: one legend has them descend from Noah's ark, said to have come to rest near Moussoro, in Chad.

Now listen to a version collected in Cameroon, at Ngoutchoumi, in Fali country, and published by the archaeologist Jean-Gabriel Gauthier in 1979. It is almost a song:

A woman from "Kélem", perhaps Jerusalem, gives birth to twins, a girl and a boy, one white, one black. When they are grown, they are married to each other. They beget giants. It was wrong. God, in his anger, tells them: "Go!" So they walk, crossing mountains, hills, rivers, plains and marshes. They reach an island surrounded by black water. They light their way with a metal, yellow gold. Their voices are like the storm. They overturn mountains with their hands, turn rivers aside, stop the wind with their breath. And here are the last two lines; remember them well: "They built hills themselves. They buried their dead in pots."

Do you hear it? The hills. The pots. The legend speaks of the mounds and the jars. It saw them too.

But not every voice sings the glory of the giants. There is another legend, coming this time from the side of their enemies, the people of Kanem and Bornu, and Griaule collected it as well. It tells of the death of the last Sao.

The sultan of Kanem, whom the story calls Liziramman, can no longer bear this enormous neighbour, whom he accuses of being a pagan and an eater of men. He sends one of his slaves, the valiant Dalafno, to lie in wait near the giant's dwelling. The giant's wife is preparing the meal: two great measures of millet, and five watering troughs filled so that her husband can drink. The giant arrives. On his shoulder, a club as big as a tree, with four elephants hanging from it. In his hand, a fifth, which he nibbles as he walks. Dalafno, small like every hero who brings down a giant, kills him with a single thrust of his spear. They cut off the head. It takes four camels to carry it to the palace. And in the hair, where birds had built their nests, they find half a tonne of eggs.

Other tales from the same side explain the end of an entire people. The Sao were too greedy: they emptied the rivers of fish, the bush of game, the land of its fields. Their children, too big, killed the neighbours' children by accident while playing. So the neighbours joined forces. And there is this terrible trick: the Sao admired their neighbours' henna-dyed hands and wanted the same. They were told the dye only takes if your hands are tied. They let themselves be bound. Only one refused and fled. The rest were massacred.

Stop for a moment and look at what you have just heard. For some, the Sao are ancestors of light, who see by the glow of gold and whose voices make the sky tremble. For others, they are pagan ogres who had to be cut down. They are the same giants. But each side tells them its own way. Keep that in mind: we will come back to it when we look for what this myth means.

And now, step away from the fire. Let us cross to the other side. Let us go and see what the earth itself had to say.

### III. What the earth gave back

In 1928, a young French naturalist, Théodore Monod, passes near Kousseri and notices that the ground is full of remains. That same year, a researcher sent by Harvard University, F. R. Wulsin, digs a test pit into a mound at Goulfey. But the real inquiry begins in 1936. Marcel Griaule arrives with an expedition, and beside him is a man who will devote his whole life to this plain: Jean-Paul Lebeuf.

They begin digging at Midigué, in Chad. And from the very first trenches, the earth speaks. Human heads in fired clay, with elongated eyes and solemn faces. And objects of bronze, among them a small gazelle's head. His obituarist, Geneviève Calame-Griaule, would later write that this gazelle head "raised the problem of bronze in the marshes of Chad". Metal melted and cast, in a land without mines, in the middle of the swamps. Who had done this? Lebeuf would tell the story of the discovery in a book with a fairy-tale title, *Quand l'or était vivant*, "When gold was alive".

For nearly forty years, Jean-Paul Lebeuf and his wife Annie Masson-Detourbet, later Annie Lebeuf, would travel the plain, with other researchers such as Françoise Treinen-Claustre. They record the mounds one by one. In their book *Les Arts des Sao*, in 1977, they describe nearly seven hundred. Lebeuf's archaeological map and its 1981 supplement count more than eight hundred, in Chad, Cameroon and Nigeria.

Lebeuf sorts these sites into three families. The smallest mounds, low and without walls, strewn with fine sherds and with pavements made of broken pottery set on edge, he calls Sao I. The large ones, high and stretching over hundreds of metres, with remains of enclosure walls, hearths and burials, are Sao II. And most Kotoko towns today stand on mounds of this second kind.

And what lies inside these mounds?

First, the dead. The jars the rivers laid bare were indeed graves. The excavations reveal the dead placed in great jars of fired clay, sometimes covered with a second, upturned jar to seal the grave, alongside other burials in open earth. The legend said: they buried their dead in pots. The earth replies: it is true.

Then, art. Clay figurines, human heads, animals: hippopotamuses, porcupines, lizards. Pendants of copper and copper alloys, those cast objects the researchers called "Sao bronzes", shaped as heads of gazelles, ducks and crocodiles. Bracelets, anklets, necklaces, rings, lip plugs. In 1962, a Chadian researcher, Ibrahim Benoît, digging for the National Museum of Chad at Gawi, north of N'Djamena, uncovers bronze rings decorated with human heads that Lebeuf would judge exceptional.

And everyday life: net sinkers, bone harpoons, pipes, ovens, fish bones by the thousand. A people of fishers, farmers, herders, potters and metalworkers, living at the water's edge.

But how old is all this? In 1960, before radiocarbon analyses, Lebeuf believed the sites ranged from the late tenth century to the late sixteenth. Then radiocarbon overturned the calendar. The great mound of Mdaga, north of N'Djamena, excavated from 1960 to 1968, turned out to have been inhabited from the fifth century BCE until the middle of the nineteenth century: more than two thousand years of life in the same place. In 1982, new dates from Sou, near Afadé, in Cameroon, ranged from about 610 to 1450 CE, and Lebeuf even reported, for the whole set of so-called Sao sites, an isolated date going back to the thirteenth century BCE, with a considerable margin of error and all due caution.

In Cameroon, between 1980 and 1990, the archaeologist Augustin Holl excavates Houlouf, a Kotoko town in the Logone-and-Chari region, and its surroundings. He traces a very long history there, from herders' camps around 1900 BCE to an organised chiefdom. Around the year 1000, Houlouf surrounds itself with a rampart enclosing some fifteen hectares. In the sixteenth century, a cemetery receives a small elite there, buried, according to Holl, probably by a blacksmith who also served as gravedigger. And in the graves: ornaments of copper, carnelian and glass beads from far away, but also leg guards and arm guards for archers and horsemen. These people traded across the whole of the central Sudan. They had chiefs, mounted warriors, specialised craftsmen.

And the earth is still speaking today. In February 2023, a Chadian team from the National Centre for Research and Development, with researchers from the universities of Sarh and Doba, the National Museum, the University of Porto and the Russian Academy of Sciences, excavates two mounds at Toukra, in a district of N'Djamena. In one of them, beneath an upturned jar, another jar. Inside, a child, lying in a foetal position, with a few beads, perhaps of carnelian. Only the skull and the long bones were present: the archaeologists see in it a secondary burial, in which the remains were moved after death, a practice they had not found described in the publications on the Sao. The dates place this grave between the thirteenth and the early fifteenth century. And in the soil of these mounds, they identified cultivated sorghum.

One last thing, and it matters. In Nigeria, at Zilum, near the lake, German and Nigerian archaeologists have excavated a twelve-hectare village surrounded by a ditch, dated to between the seventh and fifth centuries BCE, long before anyone wrote down the word "Sao". The lake plain thus knew fortified villages for more than two thousand years.

So weigh what this means. The sites called "Sao" span century upon century, peoples who followed one another, mixed, fought. They are not the traces of a single people. They are the traces of a civilisation of earth and water, long-lived and inventive, which built, cast metal, buried its dead with care, and needed no giant to be great.

And the giants? In none of the scholarly publications I consulted is there the skeleton of a giant. Men and women of human size, and children who were mourned.

### IV. A name given by others

One mystery remains, and it lies in the name itself.

No one, anywhere, has ever found a text in which these people call themselves "Sao". The dictionary of northern Cameroon by Christian Seignobos and Henry Tourneux says it outright: the Sao, who in all likelihood never called themselves by that name, formed a galaxy of rival groups that eventually united around a few more powerful towns, of which the Kotoko would be among the most direct descendants.

"Sao", then, is a name given by others. But what did it mean?

The first answer is Lebeuf's, in 1960: a term said to mean "the people", in the sense of "the people of old". The ancients, those who came before.

The second answer, more precise, comes from the language of the Kotoko themselves. In the speech of Goulfey, Annie Lebeuf noted, *saw* is the word for the town wall. Henry Tourneux dug deeper. Among the Kotoko of Makari and Goulfey, the earthen wall is called *sàwè*. In Kousseri, *sáw* means the enclosure of dry thorn branches built around the huts. And an old word for "thorns" can be reconstructed roughly as *sahwa*. So Tourneux proposes a magnificent idea: before the earthen walls, the first villages protected themselves from wild beasts and enemies with thorn hedges. The neighbours would have named these people after their fence: the people of the thorn enclosure. Then, when earth replaced thorns, the word stayed attached to the wall, and to the people who lived behind it. The Masa, further south, did exactly the same with the former inhabitants of the land between the Chari and the Logone, whom they call *Ngulmung*, "the people of the wall".

And here is a detail that sends a shiver down the spine. Tourneux observes that the word for "wall" is very similar from one Kotoko dialect to the next, a sign that it is fairly recent. It would have appeared, he writes, at a time when living behind a hedge of thorns had disappeared and when the "Sao" had become mythical.

The Bornu chronicler himself seems to confirm the picture. Recounting, in the sixteenth century, his sultan's wars against the Sao, he calls their fortified towns *shawkiyya*. And this Arabic word, Tourneux points out, comes from *shawk*, "thorns".

His conclusion changes everything: the name "Sao", he writes, would not designate any particular ethnic group. It would rather be the name of a way of living: fortified towns, first protected by thorn hedges.

Other historians go further still. As early as 1962, the American anthropologist Ronald Cohen wondered whether the "So" were not a grouping invented by the chroniclers. And the historian Dierk Lange, a specialist in the chronicles of Kanem-Bornu, wrote in 1989 a sentence that sums up the state of knowledge: despite the traditions collected, the excavations and the study of Kotoko institutions, "the existence of the Sao remains a mystery to this day".

So remember this. When you say "the Sao", you are not naming a single people with one language, one king and one capital. You are naming the people of before, the people of the walls, the people whom others, later, looked at from a distance. And among those who looked at them from a distance were their fiercest enemies.

### V. The age of the Bornu horsemen

Raise your eyes to the north-west. Beyond the lake lies another world.

Over the centuries, on the northern and western shores of Lake Chad, one of the oldest and most powerful states in Africa took shape: Kanem, then Bornu. A kingdom of horsemen, linked by the Saharan trails to North Africa and Egypt, whose court embraced Islam very early. Its rulers bear the title *mai*. Its chroniclers write in Arabic. And to them, the people of the walled towns to the south, those who do not pray as they do, are the Sao.

For a long time the two worlds live side by side, trade, and fight. And Bornu does not always have the upper hand. Listen to what the royal list of Kanem-Bornu, the *Diwan*, reports, as read by the German traveller Heinrich Barth in the middle of the nineteenth century: in the fourteenth century, the Sao defeated and killed four kings of Bornu, one after another, within the space of a few years. Barth draws his conclusion: this "very numerous" people had not been absorbed by the empire; it pushed it back, and for a long time it "threatened its very existence".

Four kings. Remember that number the next time someone tells you that peoples without writing have no history.

Then comes the sixteenth century. And with it, a man: Mai Idris Alauma.

He is one of the greatest rulers in the history of Bornu. He reorganises his army, he has firearms, which are rare in the region at the time, and he has something rarer still: a chronicler. The imam Ahmad ibn Furtu, grand imam of Bornu, writes in 1576, in the capital Birni Gazargamo, the account of the wars of the first twelve years of his reign. The text still exists. It was translated into English by Richmond Palmer in 1926, then edited with commentary by Dierk Lange in 1987. And two of its chapters tell of the war against the Sao.

Ibn Furtu distinguishes several groups. The Sao-Gafata, settled along the Komadugu Yobe river, west of the lake. The Sao-Tatala, closer to the lake. The account, as Barth summarised it, is terribly harsh. To take the great fortified town of the Gafata, Idris sets up an entrenched camp some distance away, then harasses the besieged day after day. He has their crops and their trees cut down. He starves them. Then he storms the town. It falls; its inhabitants are killed or carried off into captivity, and the neighbouring villages suffer the same fate. Some of the survivors flee towards Kanem. Further on, another stronghold, Amsaka, is taken, Barth says, "chiefly by means of his muskets". As for the Tatala, almost wiped out, they withdraw into the marshes and islands of the lake.

These facts must be faced without softening them. This is not legend. It is a war of conquest, with hunger as a weapon, towns razed and captives led away. The end of the Sao as an independent power was no poetic fade-out. It came at a human cost.

But an honest griot also tells you the caution of the scholars. Dierk Lange reminds us that in Ibn Furtu's chronicle, the names of peoples reflect the view of the court more than the reality on the empire's frontiers. In other words, "Sao", under the chronicler's pen, first means those who resist and are not Muslims, as seen from the palace. Historians still debate the exact location of some towns and even the dates of Idris Alauma's reign: depending on the author, it begins in 1564, in 1571 or later. What is certain is the second half of the sixteenth century, and the account of a witness from the court.

The Cameroonian historian Saïbou Issa described these towns from the ground: the Sao, he writes, lived in strongholds on the edge of Bornu, with a thick forest acting as a screen between the lands under central power and the places where the Sao lived.

Three centuries later, in March 1852, Heinrich Barth crosses the region on his way to Bagirmi. People show him ruins. Near Ngala, the town of Ndiffu, he is told, was one of the last strongholds of the Sao, and remarkable ornaments are often dug up there. Further on lie the ruins of a large town called Sú, whose name seems to him a remnant of that ancient people "which formerly ruled over the whole of this region". Further still, a village called Krénik, which its inhabitants claim was "the capital, or one of the capitals" of the Sao.

And those who survived? Some took refuge on the islands. Henry Tourneux puts forward the hypothesis that the Suwa, whom several peoples of the Mandara mountains count among their clans, descend from Sao driven out in the sixteenth century. And many others stayed, behind their walls, on the banks of the Logone and the Chari. They negotiated, paid tribute, converted little by little. They changed. But they did not leave.

They are the ones, say the Kotoko, that we are.

### VI. The towns that remember

When Barth enters Afadé in 1852, he describes it as the largest town in Kotoko country. A vast wall, crumbling. The tall ruins of the governor's palace. Clay houses with upper storeys, round huts crowned with earthen terrace roofs. And he writes: "There seems to have been a considerable degree of civilization in former times in this little kingdom of Kótokó, or rather in this group of distinct principalities, the independent character of which is clearly shown by the great diversity of its dialects, which vary with every large town."

One town, one language. That is Kotoko country.

The Kotoko speak languages of the Chadic family, the family of Hausa, within the great Afro-Asiatic group. Linguists distinguish northern dialects, such as those of Afadé, Makari or Goulfey, central ones, such as *Lagwan* in Logone-Birni and *Mser* in Kousseri, and southern ones. The Kotoko themselves often name themselves after their towns: Mandagué in the north, around Goulfey, Makari and Afadé; Mser in the centre, around Kousseri; Lagouané in the south, around Logone-Birni and Houlouf.

And the very name Kotoko? The elders of Goulfey and Logone-Birni, interviewed in 2006 by the historian Pascal Greng of the University of Ngaoundéré, offer a charming explanation, to be taken as tradition: it would be the sound "ko-to-ko, ko-to-ko", the rhythm of a night-time dance and the noise fishermen make to drive fish towards their great triangular nets. Strangers would have named this people after its music.

Every town has its founding story, and each one begins with the Sao. Annie Lebeuf gathered these stories over fifteen years and published them in 1969 in *Les principautés kotoko*; Pascal Greng found them still alive among his informants. Listen to them as legends, which is what they are.

At Goulfey, on the Chari, a small group of Sao from the north first settles on the opposite bank. One day, three men and a woman cross the river in search of wild grain. On a rise, they find a stone, weapons, a spear, a helmet, a harpoon, and emblems of command: a staff and a copper necklace. The spear blocks the path. The woman, Oulmé Machilinga, seizes it, plants it in the ground and says: "I am tired. I have found water, I will go no further." That is where Goulfey will be. Later a second group arrives, and again it is a woman, Maguira Dama, who pulls out the spear and plants it in the same place. The founders of Goulfey are women who plant the spear and decide where people will live.

At Logone-Birni, on the Logone, tradition tells of a fisherman, Roudouma, who thought he was alone in the world. One day he catches a dog eating his dried fish. He chases the animal, and the dog leads him to its master, Saldoma, a hunter, who also thought he was alone. The fisherman and the hunter discover each other, decide to live together, and trace a path between their homes. The land around is covered with a vegetable they call *lugum*, and from this word, tradition says, comes the name Logone.

At Ngala, in today's Nigeria, Jean-Paul Lebeuf heard the story of a Sao named Otoman. On his arrival he finds two lizards, one white, one striped. He kills the striped one and adopts the white one, which becomes the town's protective animal, the *abuda*, "a Sao word", his informants told him. Otoman has a mound raised, surrounds it with a wall, and builds his house in the middle, at the spot marked by stones. At this place, called *bambaram*, Lebeuf saw half-buried jars full of small polished stones representing the ancestors of the Sao, and an oval stone as big as an ostrich egg. The people of Ngala told him its name: "the father and the mother of all the Sao". If you take it away, they added, it comes back by itself. And the chief of Ngala, May Tchiroma, still entered his home through the gate of the old palace, imitating, Lebeuf writes, the ways of the Sao, his ancestors.

At Makari, it is a serpent that reigns at the beginning. Annie Lebeuf records the tradition of Mey Kassimo, the serpent king, "black and of enormous size, with a white head bristling with black hairs", who lived at the centre of the town in a lair surrounded by shady trees, and to whom the inhabitants paid a heavy tribute of live sheep.

And throughout Kotoko country, there is the monitor lizard. It is called, Greng reports, "Made ho", "the mother of the town".

Why the monitor lizard? The founding stories give a dark answer. At Goulfey as at Logone-Birni, the wall collapsed every time it was raised, as long as the powers of the earth had not received their due. So, the traditions say, children were walled up inside it. At Goulfey, three sisters, Gara, Garé and Massassario; other versions say they were placed in huge jars beneath the foundations. At Logone-Birni, a boy and a girl, on either side of the western gate. And out of these graves came monitor lizards, and at Logone-Birni a monitor lizard and a python, which became the town's protectors. Since then, the wall has stood.

These are stories, passed down by named elders and published by historians. No excavation, to my knowledge, has come to confirm or refute them. But they say one thing forcefully: the town was born of a pact with the earth, sealed at the highest price, and the creatures that crawl at the foot of the walls are the children of that pact.

Then Islam came, in stages. At Goulfey, according to a tradition reported by Lebeuf, as early as the fifteenth century, through the marriage of Mey Douna to a woman from Bulala country. Elsewhere with the Bornu conquest, then with the armies of Bagirmi; at Kousseri and Logone-Birni, perhaps only in the eighteenth century. Sao names gave way to Arabic names, the imam entered the court. But the stones remained. At Afadé, Lebeuf saw a large flat stone of which the inhabitants said: "It is the bearer of power [...] it speaks in spite of not having a mouth: it is the true chief." The sultan sacrificed a red rooster to it. And his informants explained that these sacrifices came from "the ancient inhabitants of the country", the verses of the Qur'an having been added only much later.

Twice sacred is the Kotoko town: by God, and by the Sao.

### VII. What the giants are saying

So why giants?

First, because the traces were there, before everyone's eyes. Hills made by human hands, jars an adult can curl up in, earthen walls several metres thick at the base, bronzes from who knows where. When a people finds in its own soil the works of those who came before, and no longer knows how they were made, it readily credits them to someone bigger than itself. Read Griaule again: the Sao's drinking cups were "as big as funeral jars". The burial jar became a giant's drinking cup. And the Ngoutchoumi story says it plainly: they built hills, they buried their dead in pots.

Next, because time makes ancestors grow. Remember the possible meaning of the word: "the people of old". The further back you go, the taller the fathers become. The giants measure the distance that separates us from them.

But the myth also does work, and that work is not the same depending on whose mouth tells it.

In the mouths of the Kotoko, the giants are founders. Dierk Lange wrote that today's Kotoko regard their Sao ancestors as culture heroes, whose material culture was more refined than their own. To be heir to the Sao is to hold rights over the land, the mounds, the towns. This is where myth becomes power. Listen to Annie Lebeuf: Goulfey, she writes, is beyond doubt the town where the enthronement ceremonies of the Mey have kept the greatest scope, and where, better than anywhere else, the cult of the monitor lizard, protector of the town and "supreme enthroner" of the reigning prince, has stayed alive. The chosen prince spends a period of seclusion in a secret room of the palace, then is presented to the protective reptiles, which are supposed to accept him or demand someone else. The traditions of Goulfey even say that in former times the new Mey had to sacrifice his own mother, and that an ancient chief, Machi, who refused, saw his whole town die; Greng specifies that this practice, if it ever existed, belongs to the past. The message is clear: no one rules a Kotoko town by birth alone. You rule because the powers left behind by the Sao, the stones, the monitor lizard, the serpent, have accepted you.

In the mouths of the victors of Bornu and Kanem, the same giants become ogres. Gluttons, pagans, man-eaters, too big to live in peace with their neighbours. The historian Manga Makrada Maïna saw it clearly: these stories present the Sao as an accursed people, born of incest, who had to be driven out. The giant killed by little Dalafno is conquest told as the victory of the righteous over the monster. Here the myth serves to justify war.

And in the stories that bring the Sao from the Nile, from Egypt, Yemen or Jerusalem, or link them to Noah and to Adam, you hear the echo of religions that came later, Islam and Christianity, which sought to write the ancestors of this land into the great history of the Book. None of these origins has been demonstrated. They are ways of saying: our fathers were ancient, and they mattered.

Even the giants' colour changes with the teller. The historian Henri Moniot noted as early as 1964 that the legends made them sometimes black, sometimes white. A living myth takes on the colour of whoever carries it.

### VIII. The plain today

In January 2025, for the first time, the Festival of Kotoko Arts and Traditions, the Festat, leaves the Logone-and-Chari and settles for four days into the National Museum in Yaoundé. Kotoko from Chad and Nigeria make the journey. One of them, Mahamat Ahmed Ali, from Chad, tells the journalist: "We are a unique people, and this festival is an opportunity for us to show our know-how." And he adds that a descendant of the Sao "stands out first of all by his height and his build". The journalist notes that he himself is over two metres tall. You see: the legend of the giants is not dead. It has become a pride that walks down the street.

But while memory is being celebrated, the earth is suffering.

In Chad, an inventory carried out in 2021 by Chadian researchers recorded 142 Sao sites around N'Djamena and in the neighbouring provinces, and found a high degree of destruction. People dig into the mounds to extract clay for bricks. The city spreads over them. They are looted. At Toukra, in 2023, the archaeologists had to work under armed guard, and they had to explain to the residents, and above all to the schoolchildren who came every evening after class, that they were not looking for treasure. Sao terracottas appear on the Red List of the International Council of Museums, among African objects threatened by illicit trafficking. And in Nigeria, the archaeologist Graham Connah photographed, from 1963 to 1993, great Sao jars that had remained in villages of Borno, reused by the inhabitants as indigo dye vats or for brewing beer. Today, because of the passing of time and the armed violence striking the region, no one knows what has become of those jars.

So listen to me well, you who read this in Cameroon, in Chad, in Nigeria, or far from here.

The giants belong to legend. But what they were hiding is very real. People lived on the shores of this lake for more than two thousand years. They built towns on the water, cast metal, shaped clay, buried their children with beads, stood up to an empire of horsemen to the point of killing four of its kings, and when they were defeated, they did not leave: they became the Kotoko, and they kept their stones, their walls and their names of thorns.

The next time you see a low hill in the middle of the plain, do not walk past without stopping. It is not a hill. It is a book, written in earth, by the people of old.

And that book is yours to protect.

## Structured fact sheet

### The Sao, giants of Lake Chad, and the Kotoko towns

- **Content type**: a legend of oral tradition (the Sao "giants") grounded in an attested archaeological and historical reality (fortified towns on mounds, burial jars, bronzes, wars against Bornu, continuity with the Kotoko principalities). The two levels are kept separate in the tale.
- **Peoples concerned**: the Kotoko (Mandagué, Mser, Lagouané) of Cameroon, Chad and Nigeria, who all claim descent from the Sao; the same descent is also claimed by other groups (Buduma, Massa, Bilala, depending on the source); hostile versions from the Kanembu and Bornu side.
- **Era**: precolonial (so-called Sao sites from the 5th century BCE to the 19th century according to radiocarbon; Bornu wars in the 16th century; Kotoko principalities to the present day)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Lake Chad plain: Far North Cameroon, Chad, north-eastern Nigeria)
- **Theme**: other (myth of origin and legitimacy, archaeology, resistance to conquest, sacred kingship)

**Timeline (history, distinct from legend)**

- 7th-5th centuries BCE: fortified village of Zilum (Nigeria), Gajiganna culture; ditch-enclosed settlements already exist on the plain (Magnavita et al., 2006).
- 5th century BCE - mid-19th century: occupation of the great mound of Mdaga, Chad (excavations by Lebeuf, Treinen-Claustre and Courtin, 1960-1968, published 1980).
- Around 1000 CE: Houlouf (Cameroon) surrounds itself with a rampart of about 16 hectares (Holl).
- 14th century: according to the *Diwan* of Kanem-Bornu (as read by Barth), the Sao kill four kings of Bornu within a few years.
- Second half of the 16th century: campaigns of Mai Idris Alauma against the Sao-Gafata (Komadugu Yobe) and the Sao-Tatala, recounted by the imam Ahmad ibn Furtu (chronicle written in 1576 at Birni Gazargamo).
- 15th-18th centuries: gradual Islamisation of the Kotoko towns (Goulfey from the 15th century according to tradition; Kousseri and Logone-Birni perhaps in the 18th).
- 1852: Heinrich Barth describes Afadé and the ruins attributed to the Sao (Sú, Krénik, Ndiffu).
- 1936: Griaule and Lebeuf begin excavating at Midigué; 1943: Griaule's *Les Sao légendaires*.
- 2023: Chadian-Portuguese-Russian excavations at Toukra (N'Djamena); child burial in a jar dated from the 13th to the early 15th century.
- January 2025: 6th Festat (Festival of Kotoko Arts and Traditions) at the National Museum in Yaoundé.

**Debates and uncertainties**

- **The name**: "the people of old" (Lebeuf), town wall or thorn enclosure (A. Lebeuf, Tourneux), or a chroniclers' category for non-Muslims (Lange, Taïmou Adji). Cohen (1962) even doubted there was a real group.
- **One people or a mosaic**: consensus on a mosaic of groups; the so-called Sao sites span more than two millennia.
- **Chronology**: from the 10th-16th centuries (Lebeuf 1960, before radiocarbon) to the 5th century BCE - 19th century; an isolated 13th-century-BCE date (Lebeuf 1982) remains fragile; 29 published radiocarbon dates in total. The "6th century BCE" in the earlier fact sheet is only one marker among others.
- **Idris Alauma's dates**: accession in 1564 (Lange), 1571 (Palmer), even 1580 (Greng). The identification of some towns (Amsaka, Damasak) is debated.
- **Kotoko Islamisation**: staggered from the 15th to the 18th century depending on the town; dates remain approximate.
- **Sao-Kotoko descent**: claimed by all Kotoko and supported by continuity of sites, but also claimed by other groups; a cultural and political descent more than a demonstrated genealogy.
- **Bronze technique**: often described as lost-wax casting in general literature, not verified in the sources consulted; Holl notes that no metalworking features had been recorded in the first decades of research.
- **First written mentions** (Kawar before the 9th century via Palmer, al-Maqrizi, Leo Africanus, Ibn Furtu): sources and dates diverge; mentions attributed to Ibn Hawqal and Ibn Battuta by some authors have not been verified.
- **Foundation and enthronement sacrifices**: reported by oral tradition (A. Lebeuf, Greng's informants), with no archaeological attestation found.
- **Age of the Toukra child**: 5-9 years (2023 article) or 9-11 years (2025 report).

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The giant Sao: bows made of whole palm trees, cups as big as funeral jars, hippopotamuses caught by hand, thunderous voices, raptors' nests in their hair (Griaule); walking sticks of palm trunks, a camel-load meal, a Goulfey chief's sneeze scattering the birds (Mveng).
- Sao women whose dance carved out the Chari, the Logone and the Yobe.
- Eastern or biblical origins (Nile, Egypt, Yemen, Jerusalem, Noah's ark, incestuous twins): traditions, not demonstrated.
- The death of the last giant, killed by Dalafno for Sultan Liziramman, and the henna trick (Kanembu and Bornu stories).
- Founding stories: Oulmé Machilinga and Maguira Dama at Goulfey; Roudouma, Saldoma and the dog at Logone-Birni; Otoman and the white lizard at Ngala; Mey Kassimo, the serpent king of Makari; walled-up children turned into monitor lizards and a python; sacred stones, "the father and the mother of all the Sao".
- Folk etymology of "Kotoko" (the onomatopoeia "ko-to-ko").
