# Nchare Yen, the Stone and the Race

Founding legend of the Bamum (Bamoun) kingdom, Foumban, West Cameroon. Version 2, rewritten in depth on 2026-10-15 (the earlier French version is archived in `archives/fr_v1.md`, the research file is `recherche.md`).

> This story weaves together a legend from oral tradition and a dynastic history that is real but poorly dated. The griot tells the legend as a legend, and at every step he says what the palace texts claim, what historians dispute, and what no one can settle.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. A Foot on the Stone

Listen.

We are in Foumban, in the palace courtyard. A king has just died, and another is about to be born. Not born of his mother: born of his people. The new sovereign is coming back from the sacred bath, from the Nshi river, where only the king may wash at the chosen spot. He is still dripping. The dignitaries close around him. The crowd holds its breath.

And suddenly a man rises up in front of him.

He carries a raffia cleaver in a raffia sheath. Above all, he carries a name: Njikumjue. What this man is about to do was described by King Njoya himself, in the early twentieth century, in the great book of history and customs he had written down in the script he invented. Hear his words, as the missionary Henri Martin rendered them in French (translated here into English):

"Njikumjue disputes the stone with the king; he even pretends to set his foot upon that stone. Then he calls out: Pamom! But the Pamom do not answer. Then the new king calls out: Pamom! And all the Pamom answer."

Pamom is the name the Bamum people give themselves. Njikumjue calls the people, and the people are silent. The king calls the people, and the people answer with one voice. Then Njikumjue, the text says, "lets himself fall", his brothers strike him, and he cries out: "oh! oh! it is because I have no one." He gets up, he runs, and the crowd chases him, pelting him with palm pith. They catch him and bring him back. "Nshare, you are right," he cries. And he lies down before the king, who sets his foot upon him and says: "you are selfish."

Nshare, you are right.

Did you hear it? The name shouted that day is not the new king's name. It belongs to a man dead for centuries, as if, at every coronation, the founder himself came back. The ethnographer Duala-M'bedy, who did fieldwork in Foumban in 1959, reports, drawing on the palace texts, that the people greeted their freshly enthroned sovereign with a cry: Nshare has returned, a warrior, the land is happy again. And a scholar from Foumban, Idrissou Njoya, still writes today that the reigning king is known by the name Nchare Yèn. Power is one single power. Each king embodies it, dies, and hands it on.

So ask yourself the question every Bamum child has the right to ask. Who was this Nshare, also called Nchare, Nshare Yèn, Nsa're? Why does a man come to dispute a stone with him? Why does he pretend to set his foot on it? And why, all across the Bamum country, do people say that a whole kingdom was born from a race the strongest did not win?

To understand the stone, we must travel back in time and along the road. We must go three days' walk from Foumban, to the edge of a lake and a river, where, the palace texts say, everything began. "Autrefois les Pamom étaient à Rifom": "Long ago the Pamom were at Rifom." That is how the history of the Bamum kings, dictated under Njoya, opens in Martin's translation. Let us go to Rifum.

### II. Rifum, the Lake of Kings

Rifum. Today people say Bankim, or Mbankim, in the Adamawa region, on the edge of the Tikar plain, where the Mbam and the Mapé wind between savanna and gallery forest. The Bamum country lies further south-west, on a high plateau of western Cameroon, in a triangle between the Mbam, the Noun and the Mapé. King Njoya's book measures the distance as it was measured then, not in kilometres but in weariness: "Rifum is three days' walk from Fumban."

Bamum traditions, like those of their neighbours in Nso, in Bafia and in many chiefdoms of the Grassfields, say their dynasties came from the Tikar. The Tikar are a real people who still live on the plain that bears their name, around Bankim and Ngambe, and whose kingdoms are thought to have formed under the influence of groups coming from the north, from the Adamawa. The Foumban archives, quoted by Duala-M'bedy, speak of a chief named Rifoum, who broke away from the Mbum of the Ngaoundéré plateau and settled on the Mbam where Bankim now stands.

But listen closely to what the researchers who have worked hardest on this question say. The anthropologist David Price, who lived among the Tikar in the 1970s, wrote that there is no linguistic, ethnic or truly historical evidence for calling any Grassfields people "Tikar". Ian Fowler and David Zeitlyn, in a collective volume published in 1996, *African Crossroads*, confirm it: many Grassfields dynasties claim Tikar origins, yet their languages and cultures have almost nothing in common with those of the Tikar of the plain. Bamum, the Shüpamom language, belongs to the Grassfields languages, not to the Tikar language. For these scholars, Tikar origin is first of all a claim made by royal families, a way of saying: our kingship is as noble as that of the sacred kings of the plain, whose power was almost absolute.

Does that mean it was all invented? No. Some links were not manufactured for a colonial officer. Fowler and Zeitlyn note it themselves: the Bamum, like the Nso, recognise the ritual ascendancy of the chief of Bankim, and new rulers had to be blessed at Rifum, beside a sacred lake, where only the chief of Bankim remained seated on a stool. The art historian Christraud Geary recorded Bamum traditions according to which, until one of the early reigns, the iron double gongs and the sacred objects of kingship came from the east, from Tikar country. And Duala-M'bedy describes, at each enthronement, a striking gesture: the king of Rifum sent two counsellors to Foumban carrying water from the place where the ancient kings of Rifum bathed, and the new Bamum king washed in it at once. Rifum also sent three women to teach the young king the old traditions. Their titles say everything: one was called Mother of N'share, the second Mother of Fumban, the third Mother of N'so.

Mother of Nshare, mother of Foumban, mother of Nso. Three kingdoms, one source. That is what the ritual declared, far more forcefully than any family tree.

And that source has its story. The Bamum tell it. The Nso tell it. It speaks of a king who died at Rifum, of a disputed succession, of brothers and a sister who could not all reign. It speaks of a departure. That is where the legend begins.

### III. The Race

Come closer. What follows is the legend, as it is told today in the Bamum country, and as a Bamum author, Maître Aboubakar Mounpain, set it down on the kingdom's information portal. I give it to you with its names and gestures, but keep in mind that this is a word of tradition, not a court record.

At Rifum there was a king, Fonrifum. He died. And as always when a king dies, the land held its breath: who would succeed him? The elders chose Dikum, one of his sons. And another son, an ambitious one, took it as a deep wound. His name was Nchare.

Nchare had a mother, a queen, and her name was Yen. The founder still carries her name: Nchare Yen, Nchare son of Yen. The legend gives her five children: Fomban, Koumjouom, Nchare, Mfom and Nguonsoh. Passed over for the throne, Yen and her son felt dishonoured. They gathered their followers and took the road towards Mount Léké, far from the court that had refused them.

But a band in exile needs a leader. And in this family, several could become one. Koumjouom first, whom many storytellers make the favourite. Nchare next. And their sister Mfom, a claimant too.

So, the legend says, it was decided to let a trial speak. Not a war between brothers, not poison, not a plot in the night: a race. The three claimants would sit on the same tree trunk. A little less than a hundred metres away, a stone had been set. And the rule fitted in one sentence, which Mounpain writes like this: "The first to set his foot on the stone would become king."

A nephew of the dead king, Njimongha, was given the task of starting the race.

Picture the scene. Three children of a queen sitting side by side on the wood. The silence of the followers. The stone, over there, at the end of the grass. Koumjouom, already straining towards it. And Nchare.

Do you want to know what Nchare looked like? King Njoya's book kept a portrait of him, and it is one of the strangest any people has ever left of its founder. I give it to you from Martin's translation as Duala-M'bedy quotes it: Nsa're was truly small, he had a big belly, his eyes shone like a panther's, he was very cunning in war, he had short legs, he was very brave, but he could not run. He loved dancing, he drank a great deal of palm wine, and he was charitable.

He could not run.

Hold on to that sentence, because it comes from the palace itself, not from a market storyteller. The founder described by the royal book could not have won a fair race. And a race is exactly what the legend makes him win.

How? Listen. When they sat down on the trunk, Mfom settled very close to her brother Koumjouom. So close that she sat on a fold of his wrapper. He did not notice. Njimongha gave the signal. Koumjouom leapt, and the cloth held him, pulled him back, made him stumble in his rush. One second. Two, perhaps. Enough for the short-legged, round-bellied little man to charge head down and be the first to set his foot on the stone.

Nchare was king.

Was it cheating? Yes, and the legend does not hide it. It does not say Nchare was the strongest. It says he was chosen by those around him, that his sister took his side, that the cunning of his people counted for more than his brother's legs. This is not a story for well-behaved children: it is a story about power as it is won, through alliances, loyalties and clever moves. And the story fits strangely well with the rest of Bamum memory. The royal portrait calls Nchare "very cunning in war". Duala-M'bedy, who worked on the Foumban archives, pointed out that one of the meanings proposed for the very name Pamom was "the hidden ones", or "those who hide", and that it suited a people who, he said, had won their land by cunning more than by numbers. And in a Bamum folktale recorded in 2020 by researchers from the University of Yaoundé I, it is once again the smallest of the suitors, Meusep Nforpeyam, "the little animal wiser than all the others", who wins the king's daughter by hiding a stone in his bundle of grass.

A stone, again.

But careful. An honest griot must tell you where each thread comes from. I did not find the race in the royal portrait, nor in what the researchers Jacques Binet, Duala-M'bedy and Idrissou Njoya quote from the chronicle written under Njoya. Those texts tell the parting differently. The race, as I have just told it, is the tradition handed down and published by Mounpain, and it is the one you hear in Foumban today. The two memories live side by side. Now listen to the other one.

### IV. The Broken Boat

In this other memory there is no tree trunk and no stone at the end of the grass. There is water.

The art historian Idrissou Njoya, who teaches at the Foumban Institute of Fine Arts of the University of Dschang, opens one of his articles with this sentence: "Bamum history truly begins with a scene of violence and cunning led by Nshare Yèn, during the crossing of the Mapé." And he tells it. It is night. The children of Mforifum, the king of Rifum, have decided to set out and conquer new lands. They reach the bank of the Mapé, a river running towards the Mbam. They must cross. Nshare says to his brothers: "Let me cross first." They agree, suspecting nothing. Nshare crosses with his followers. And once on the far bank, he breaks the boat. Morumta and Nguonso are left on the other side, furious and powerless.

There, on the two banks of a river, the three princes part ways. Morumta goes downstream and settles at Nditam, in the Mbam, where he becomes king of the Mbam. Nguonso goes upstream and settles among the Nso, who make her their queen. Nshare marches south-west, towards Njimom, and becomes king of the Mom, the Bamum.

In 1952 a French researcher from ORSTOM, Jacques Binet, had read a similar version in the chronicles written under Njoya, one even harsher on the founder. There, Morumta is chief at Rifum, and he leaves the country because, during the seclusion that precedes his enthronement, his ministers put his mother to death according to the custom of the time. Nshare is his nephew. He follows him into exile, then, Binet writes, "breaks brutally away from the family group by crossing a river in a dugout canoe that he has smashed once his men are across". Nshare, Binet concludes, could claim no legitimacy at all. And the chronicle does not flatter him. It says that when he reached a neighbouring chief, Nshare saw that this Mfoshen was majestic, and that "envy gnawed at Nshare and his men".

Now go to the Nso, to the north-west, around Kumbo. They tell the parting from the point of view of the one left on the bank. According to Nso historians such as William Banboye and Joseph Lafon, whose accounts an Nso cultural association in the United States has republished, Princess Ngonnso was the elder sister of Nchare Yen and of another brother, Mbe, whom others call Mfombam, founder of the Nditam dynasty among the Bafia. When the king died and a half-brother, Mveing, took power, the two brothers left with their followers. Ngonnso wanted to go with them. They refused, because she was married. And after crossing the Mapé, they cut the bridge behind them. Ngonnso stayed on the bank. So she followed the river westwards, led her people from one halting place to the next, made a pact with the first inhabitants of the land, the Mtaar, who kept ownership of the soil, and founded the kingdom that still bears her name: Nso, from Ngon-Nso.

Lay the versions side by side, like calabashes on a mat. A rigged race. A broken boat. A bridge cut down. A canoe destroyed, says Mounpain, on the orders of Queen Yen herself, after the crossing of the Mbam, leaving two of her children behind: Fomban, who founded the Bafia at Ditam, and Nguonsoh, who founded Nso at Kumbo.

The names change. Morumta, Fomban, Mbe, Mfombam: always the brother who goes towards the Mbam and Nditam. Nguonso, Nguonsoh, Ngonnso: always the sister who founds Nso. Koumjouom, Kumnjuo, Njikumjue: always the rival brother who stays beside Nchare. Even the kinship shifts. Depending on the text, Nchare is the son of the king of Rifum, or the son of Yen, the king's daughter, or Morumta's nephew, or the successor's half-brother.

But look at what never moves. A royal family of Rifum. A throne that cannot go to everyone. A parting brought about by a trick. And at the end of the road, three cousin kingdoms: Bamum, Nso, Nditam. Here the myth does precise work. It explains why peoples who do not speak the same language call themselves kin. It explains why Nso and Bamum were both relatives and rivals, and how far that rivalry could go: in the nineteenth century the Bamum king Nsangu died fighting Nso, and his head long remained in enemy hands. It also explains why none of the three kingdoms could claim to rule the other two: the break has happened, and the water seals it.

And it says something more unsettling. Every version, the race and the boat alike, agrees on one point: the founder of the Bamum was not the designated heir. He took. He tricked. He left his own people on the bank. A people that tells this about its first king is not trying to carve a statue. It tells the truth about power as it knew it: something you seize, and must then make just.

For the story does not end at the river. On the other side of the Mapé there were peoples, chiefs, villages. And there were seven stones.

### V. The Seven Stones of Njimom

Those who had followed Nchare across the water were not many. Two to three hundred people, women and children included, according to the Foumban traditionalist Esaïe Fifen, quoted in a history thesis defended at the University of Yaoundé I in 2022. The land they entered was not empty. The Foumban archives, quoted by Duala-M'bedy, list more than twenty groups who occupied these lands before them. And the chronicles do not hide how Nchare made room for himself: through war, through cunning, and through blood.

Listen to the names, for they belong to the defeated, and the defeated have a right to memory. According to those archives, Nchare settled first at Sas, then at Njimom, after slaughtering the local chief Mfondono Tambou, then at Miepue, after killing the chief Mfochom. According to Mounpain there was also Monkoup, a seer protected by a wasps' nest, and Nfonchom, who refused to fight and was beaten when his dam on the Moloupet river was destroyed. According to the chronicle read by Binet, Nchare, a war leader without a title, looked for a sign that would raise him to the rank of kings. Among these peoples the panther was the animal of chiefs, and its skin belonged to them. Until then, as a good vassal, Nchare had sent to a chief the panthers his people killed. But thanks to the skill of one of his followers, Nchare received as tribute the skin of a panther killed by a neighbouring people. He accepted it. It was a sacrilege: he was behaving like a king. And so that his most influential companions could never betray him, he shared pieces of the animal with them. Together they had eaten the forbidden thing. They were bound.

Then came Njimom.

That is where the State is born. The thesis of Mohamed Chem-Soudine Nkomme, built on interviews with dignitaries from Foumban and Malantouen, places the scene beneath a tree called Nshinsep, at whose foot lie seven sacred stones, at the place called Samba Nguo. The prince sat down. Around him, his seven companions each sat on a stone. And there the pact was proclaimed that Bamum tradition has repeated ever since: "The Bamum State is born and Nshare is its king. He shall freely choose his heir among his sons."

These seven companions received a title: Kom, or Kom Ngu, the companions of the land. Tradition has kept their names, and here they are, as the thesis gives them: Njimonshare, Njikumjue, Njiamanka, Njianga, Njiamfâ, Njiansem, Njimonanka. They were the co-signatories of the pact, the counsellors who enthrone the king, the guardians of the founding law. Their office passed to their descendants. They enjoyed an autonomy the king himself had to respect, and if they were sentenced to death for treason, they had the privilege of taking their own lives. Duala-M'bedy adds that at every harvest festival the Kom came back to the capital to renew the oath their ancestors had sworn to Nchare.

And there were others. Seven more companions, tradition says, who were not there on the day of the founding meeting. They were called Nji Nguetngu. They received land and honours, but a second rank, because they had missed the hour when the land was born. Being present at the founding counts as much as bravery.

Now look at one name on the list: Njikumjue. It is the name of the man who, at every enthronement, comes to dispute the stone with the new king and pretends to set his foot on it. Idrissou Njoya speaks of a Nji Kumnjuo, "half-brother on the father's side of Nshare Yèn". And the legend of the race calls the rival brother Koumjouom. The sources I have read do not say this is the same man, and I do not state it to you as fact. But Nkomme writes plainly that the enthronement rite described by Njoya recalls a quarrel between Nshare Yèn and his brother Njikumjue, at Njimom, during the ceremony that founded the land. So listen to what the griot hears. In the legend, two brothers compete for the right to set a foot on a stone, and the smaller one wins thanks to his people. In the rite, the man who bears the brother's name comes to dispute the stone, and the people do not answer him. The race of the legend and the quarrel of the ritual may be telling the same thing: the king is not the one who runs fastest. The king is the one the people answer.

The rule that follows is a hard one, and Njoya records it: no brother may succeed the king. Power passes from father to son, never from brother to brother. The royal book even attributes to Nchare a saying that whoever killed a king's brother who had made himself king, to keep the land strong, could not be put to death for it. That was the price, among the Bamum, of the memory of a quarrel between brothers.

Where are those stones today? Here too the memories diverge: Nkomme's thesis places them at the foot of the tree in Njimom, while Jacques Binet wrote in 1952 that they had been taken to the palace, "where they can still be seen", and that at each enthronement the new ruler sat on them so that the people could be sure he would respect the customs. In outlying villages, he added, when a man was raised to a dignity, a stone was planted behind his house, and pulled out if he was removed. Centuries later, in the nineteenth century, when King Nsangu wanted to restore order after years of turmoil, he asked a question recorded in Njoya's book: what had Nchare said when he sat on the seven stones of Njimom, what had he said to the Kom? And he ordered that the laws proclaimed on the seven stones be restored. Among the Bamum, a constitution was not a parchment. It was seven stones and the memory of those who sat on them.

From Njimom, Nchare went back to war. He attacked the Pambèn, also written Mben or Pa-Mben, a people whose chief lived on the site of today's capital. He defeated them and built his town on their ruins. The name Foumban would keep the scar: according to Martin's translation and several Cameroonian scholars, Fem-pa-mbèn means "the ruin of the Mbèn", which became Fembèn, then Foumban in the mouths of the colonisers. Duala-M'bedy preferred to read it as "the residence of the king of the Mben". Ruin or residence, the defeated are in the name. And in the town itself, a quarter still bears their name: Mamben, Ma-mbèn in Martin.

For here is the last paradox of the founding, perhaps the most beautiful. This small group from Rifum did not make the defeated speak its language. It took theirs. Duala-M'bedy noted it as early as 1962: the newcomers adopted the language of the peoples they subdued. The Bamum language, Shüpamom, is the language of the conquered land, not of the conquerors. Even time bears the mark of the journey: in the eight-day Bamum week, Idrissou Njoya reports that the seventh day, Futmom, is of Tikar origin, that it was on a Futmom that the emigrants left Rifum, and that it was on that day that Nshare Yèn proclaimed the Bamum State.

Nchare's kingdom was still only a small circle. About thirty kilometres across between Njimom and Koundoum, according to the Bamum historian Mounchili. Some four to five hundred square kilometres, according to Claude Tardits. Eighteen chiefdoms subdued, according to Njoya's book; nineteen, by Oumarou Njoya's count. A core. But a core with a law, with guardians, with stones, and with a name.

### VI. What the Palace Wrote

How did Nchare die? Here again the memories part. The chronicle read by Jacques Binet says he was killed at Rifum, that one of his followers cut off his head, pulled up a young banana shoot at the same time, and brought both back to the palace, where the skull was buried beneath the roots of the banana plant, whose offshoots were still venerated. Duala-M'bedy speaks of an assassination. Mounpain believes Nchare died before Foumban was even taken, and places his grave near the Njimom market. The royal book adds that his first successors "did nothing and lived off what Nsa're had achieved", at least in the quotation Duala-M'bedy gives. And the chronicle does not hide the tragedies that followed: Nchare's heir, it says according to Binet, was killed in a trap set where he bathed, and the conspirators told the people that "those of the water" had not accepted him as king.

But how do we know all this? Who kept these names and portraits alive for centuries?

First, the spoken word. The kingdom had its own living archives. The Bamum historian Aboubakar Njiassé Njoya, quoted by Idrissou Njoya, describes a remarkable memory technique: every morning, notables and twins entered the Gbètnja, the cemetery of the kings, to count the gravestones, calling out the name of each king and the length of his reign. And there was the Nguon, the great festival of the kingdom: at each celebration, the Nguon chiefs placed a small stick in the Pangu, the "bag of the land" belonging to the reigning king. When a ruler died, the sticks were counted. One stick per festival, and in the old days one festival per harvest. That is how they knew how long each king had reigned.

Then, writing. At the end of the nineteenth century, the seventeenth king of the dynasty, Ibrahim Njoya, whom you may already have met in AFROBACK's stories, invented a script for his language. One of the first tasks he gave it was the history of his people. "In the past, tradition passed from mouth to ear. Now it must be written down": that is the gist of what he is said to have told the Basel missionary Anna Rein-Wuhrmann, in the quotation given by Duala-M'bedy. Palace scribes wrote down the succession of kings, the laws, the rites, the wars. This book, translated into French by the missionary Henri Martin and published in 1952 as *Histoire et coutumes des Bamum*, is one of the most precious sources in Central Africa: a history written from the inside, by an African kingdom, in its own alphabet. The Foumban palace archives, which hold thousands of documents in Njoya's script, have even been the subject of a British Library preservation programme.

But an honest griot must also tell you what kind of book this is. It is history told by a king, under the eyes of a king. Jacques Binet wrote it with disarming frankness: these chronicles "are not monuments of historical criticism, nor even of objectivity, since their writing was overseen by a monarch it was unwise to contradict. But if they do not retrace history as it was, they retrace it as a chief would have wished it to unfold." Yet Binet noticed something surprising: the book grants Nchare no magical power, no supernatural intervention. A small man, cunning, envious, brave, who could not run. Njoya, a man of reason who knew Islam and Christianity, set down a tradition without miracles.

And it was Njoya who gave the kingdom its most famous date: 1394. How? Idrissou Njoya reconstructs it. In 1902, on 6 July, the first Europeans arrived in Foumban. Njoya took that year as his reference point. He had celebrated thirteen Nguon since his accession: from that he deduced 1889 for the death of his father Nsangu. Then he went back, reign by reign, counting each king's Nguon, all the way to the first. And he arrived at 1394. Nchare Yen, king from 1394 to 1418.

This is where the great debate begins, and I owe it to you in full.

The translator himself, Henri Martin, wrote in his introduction that this chronology, which made Nchare a contemporary of King Charles VI of France, was "pure fantasy". He found the reigns too long: eighty-five years for Kuotu, fifty-seven for Mbuembue. Idrissou Njoya replies that Martin reasoned from prejudice, that he found it inconceivable for a Black African kingdom to be so well organised so early, and that Njoya could make mistakes, "but not by a margin of three centuries". And yet the kingdom's great historian, Claude Tardits, author in 1980 of a work of more than a thousand pages, *Le Royaume bamoum*, writes that the kingdom was founded "in the seventeenth century or a little earlier" by a handful of emigrants from the upper Mbam. Some researchers, applying an average of twenty years per reign, place Nchare around 1561-1577. The Encyclopaedia Britannica, in the extracts I was able to read, even places Nchare's arrival in the early eighteenth century. And the Cameroonian historian Eldridge Mohammadou has suggested that the Tikar kingdoms themselves, Bankim included, only took shape in the eighteenth century, which would upset the whole chronology of origins. Other sources again give 1384, or 1390.

So, 1394 or the seventeenth century? I will not decide for you, because no one can with certainty. What is certain is the length of the line: seventeen kings before colonial conquest, a succession the palace counted stone by stone and festival by festival. What is certain too is what came after Nchare. Between the late eighteenth and the early nineteenth century (1757-1814 in the palace chronology), the eleventh king, Mbuembue, the giant of the dynasty, turned the small core into a true kingdom. On his accession he is said to have declared, according to Njoya's text as quoted by Tardits: "I will set the limits of the kingdom with blood and black iron; war is my business. When the borders of the land are drawn with the mouth, they are always wiped away." At his death forty-eight chiefdoms had been subdued, and the kingdom covered about seven thousand seven hundred square kilometres, the size of today's Noun department. He had a great ditch dug around Foumban against the Fulani horsemen and made the two-headed serpent the emblem of the kingdom. Then came the Germans in 1902, the French after 1916, and the dismantling of the kingdom by the French administration in 1924 and 1925.

But even stripped of its power, the kingdom never stopped retelling its origin. For every king, as he steps onto the stone, becomes Nchare again.

### VII. Nchare Has Returned

Go to Foumban in December, in an even-numbered year. You will see the Nguon.

The Nguon is the great assembly of the kingdom. A thesis defended in Yaoundé defines it as an institution dating from Nchare, devoted to commemorating the dead kings and celebrating the harvest. You already know that it was used to count reigns, stick by stick. But it does far more. Every two years, in early December, the ritual chiefs travel the land and gather, in secret, what the people think of their king. On an agreed Friday they enter the palace by night to speak with him. And the next day, before the crowd, the king is judged. The complaints are read out in public. The ruler can be fined. Tradition even says he can be removed. If he is confirmed, he gives an acceptance speech, receives the renewed allegiance of his people, and the kingdom celebrates until the Sunday parade. That is how UNESCO describes it, having inscribed it in December 2023, at its session in Kasane, Botswana, as "Nguon, rituals of governance and associated expressions in the Bamoun community" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. It was Cameroon's first inscription on that list.

In December 2024 the Agence France-Presse was there. The young king Mouhammad Nabil Mfourifoum Mbombo Njoya, thirty-one years old, twentieth ruler of the dynasty, who came to the throne in 2021 on his father's death, faced his first public trial. Around him, his subjects planted their spears in the ground. A member of parliament stood up and reproached him, in front of everyone, for the dilapidated state of the kingdom's property. The king listened. Then his mandate was renewed, and rifles were fired into the air.

Think about that. A kingdom born of a trick, of a race won without running, of a broken boat, created, in order to last, an institution in which the king must answer to those he governs. The king of the enthronement is the one the people answer. The king of the Nguon is the one the people judge. It is the same lesson, told twice. The legend lets the little man win. The rite reminds him that it is not his victory that makes him king, but the voice of the Pamom.

Other traces remain. In Foumban, next to the palace, the Museum of the Bamum Kings, opened in 2024, unfolds its architecture in the shape of a two-headed serpent topped by a mygale spider, the spider of work and wisdom, with a giant double gong at the entrance. More than twelve thousand objects tell the story of the rulers from Nchare to our own day. But some are missing: Njoya's throne, the Mandu Yenu, is still in Berlin, at the Humboldt Forum, and the Bamum are asking for its return. Among their Nso cousins, another mother of origins has known the same exile: the statue of Ngonnso, carried off by a German officer in the early twentieth century, was the subject in 2022 of a decision to return it by the Prussian Cultural Heritage Foundation. The sister left on the bank of the Mapé has not finished travelling.

Words remain too, and not all of them are kind. The historian Ludovic Boris Pountougnigni Njuh, an associate researcher at the University of Dschang, recalls that the two-headed serpent, a symbol of power for the Bamum, has been turned against them as an insult, to accuse them of double-dealing. That too is part of memory.

And you, child of Foumban, of Njimom, of Koutaba, of Malantouen or of Magba, or child of somewhere else discovering this story, what should you keep of Nchare Yen?

Not a date. Scholars argue over the date, and they are right to keep searching. Not a miracle: your king's book tells of none. Keep this instead. A man whom his own people described as small, pot-bellied and unable to run founded one of the most enduring kingdoms of Central Africa. He was not the strongest, nor first in line. Around him he had a sister who chose his side, a nephew who gave the signal, companions who sat on stones and swore with him. He took, he tricked, he shed the blood of those who were there before him, and his descendants had to learn to make that power just. And then this small band from elsewhere had the wisdom to speak the language of the land where it settled.

Historians argue over centuries. Storytellers do not all tell the same parting. But all agree on what matters: there was a family divided on a riverbank, there were seven stones at Njimom, there was a law, and that law still holds.

So the next time you think you must be the tallest to count, remember the stone. It is not the fastest who sets a foot on it for good. It is the one the people answer.

Nshare, you are right.

This is where you come from.

## Structured profile

### Nchare Yen (Nshare Yèn, Nsa're), founder of the Bamum kingdom

- **Content type**: founding legend from oral tradition (the race, the broken boat, the cut bridge) resting on a real dynastic history whose dating is disputed. The kingdom, the dynasty and the institutions (Kom, Nguon, the stone of the land) are historical; the episodes of the parting are legendary.
- **People concerned**: Bamum (Bamoun, Pamom, Shüpamom), with the related traditions of Nso (Banso) and Nditam (Bafia), and the ritual reference to Rifum (Bankim, Tikar plain).
- **Era**: precolonial (1394 according to the palace chronology established under Njoya; seventeenth century according to Claude Tardits; dating disputed)
- **Region**: Central Africa (West Cameroon, Noun department, Foumban; Adamawa for Rifum/Bankim)
- **Theme**: politics (founding of a state, royal legend, institutions of governance)

**Timeline (palace tradition dates unless stated otherwise)**

- 1394-1418: reign of Nchare Yen according to the chronology established by King Njoya (seventeenth century according to Tardits; 1561-1577 by calculations at twenty years per reign; 1384 or 1390 in other sources). Parting of the children of Rifum, crossing of the Mapé, proclamation of the State at Njimom (seven stones, seven Kom), conquest of the Pambèn and settlement at Foumban.
- 1757-1814 (palace): reign of Mbuembue, eleventh king; forty-eight chiefdoms subdued, kingdom extended to about 7,700 km², ditch around Foumban, two-headed serpent emblem.
- Around 1896 to 1910: King Ibrahim Njoya (seventeenth king) invents the Bamum script and has the history and customs of the kingdom written down; 6 July 1902, first Europeans in Foumban, reference year of his chronology.
- 1924-1925: dismantling of the kingdom by the French colonial administration (Tardits).
- 1952: IFAN publishes *Histoire et coutumes des Bamum*, translated by Henri Martin.
- 6 December 2023: the Nguon is inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity; December 2024: first Nguon of the twentieth king, Mouhammad Nabil Mfourifoum Mbombo Njoya.

**Notable quotations (attested, with source; English renderings of French originals)**

**Debates and uncertainties**

- Founding date: 1394 (Njoya's chronology based on counting Nguon festivals) versus the seventeenth century (Tardits); 1384 and 1390 also circulate; Martin called 1394 "pure fantasy"; Idrissou Njoya defends Njoya. No consensus.
- Tikar origin: a dynastic claim according to Price, Fowler and Zeitlyn, without linguistic evidence; but real ritual ties with Rifum/Bankim. Mohammadou places the formation of the Tikar kingdoms in the eighteenth century.
- Nchare's kinship: son of the king of Rifum, son of Yen the king's daughter, nephew of Morumta, half-brother of the successor, depending on the version.
- How the parting happened: rigged race, broken boat or canoe, cut bridge, canoe destroyed by Yen.
- Place of death and burial: Rifum (Binet) or Njimom (Mounpain); conquest of Foumban by Nchare himself or by a successor.
- Etymology of Foumban ("ruin of the Mbèn" or "residence of the king of the Mben") and of Pamom ("the living", "the hidden ones", link with the day Futmom).
- The link between Koumjouom (legend), Nji Kumnjuo and Njikumjue (enthronement rite) is the griot's reading, supported by Nkomme (who sees the rite as recalling a quarrel between Nshare and his brother Njikumjue), not an explicit statement in all sources.
- Colonial ban on the Nguon in 1924: reported by secondary sources, not verified in a scholarly source; not used in the story.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- The race of the three claimants (Koumjouom, Nchare, Mfom), the wrapper held down by Mfom, Njimongha's signal, the stone to be reached.
- The boat or canoe broken after crossing the Mapé; the cut bridge that leaves Ngonnso on the bank (Nso version).
- Queen Yen, mother of five children, wounded by a buffalo; the seer Monkoup and his wasps' nest.
- The panther skin received as a sacrilege; Nchare's skull buried under a banana plant.
- Distant origins (Syria, Egypt, Bornu) mentioned in the chronicles, and the thesis of an Egyptian origin: origin narratives, not accepted as fact.
