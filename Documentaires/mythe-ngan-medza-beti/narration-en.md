# Ngan Medza, the Serpent of the Crossing: spoken tale (EN)
Target length: 15 min · 2,353 words · voice: AFROBACK griot

## S01 · The river that divides cousins
Listen.

In the heart of Cameroon runs a river that is never silent. It comes down from the highlands of the Adamawa. It crosses the savanna. It enters the forest, it roars through its rapids, it widens, it darkens. And it runs, without rest, all the way to the ocean.

Geographers call it the Sanaga. The elders give it another name. The Yom.

[pause]

Stand on its bank one evening, near Batchenga or Ebebda, as the light falls. Watch the brown water slide past, wide as a plain on the move. And ask yourself the question Beti children have asked for generations, sitting by the fire.

How could a whole people, with its old men, its children on their backs, its baskets, even its dead, have crossed to the other side of that?

For the Beti say it plainly: we were not born here. Our fathers came from beyond. They crossed the river. And some of them were left behind.

That is the mystery. Ewondo, Eton and Bënë around Yaoundé. Bulu further south. Fang as far as Gabon. And, the legend says, other kin left behind, north of the river.

How did one people end up cut in two by water?

Historians have their answers, and I will give them to you. But there is an older answer. The one grandmothers whispered at nightfall. An answer that has a name, a name spoken softly, because it names a power.

[long silence]

Ngan Medza.

The serpent that carried a people on its back.

## S02 · The people without a king
But before the serpent, you must know the people.

We call them the Beti. Their traditions link the word to nobility, to those who do not submit. The ethnologist Philippe Laburthe-Tolra, who spent years among them, called them the Lords of the Forest.

Lords, yes. But lords without a king.

No throne. No palace. No capital. Before colonisation, no sovereign ruler stood above all the Beti.

Their society held together through blood, and through speech. At its heart, the lineage: the mvog, "the people of", the descendants of one ancestor. Every clan name is an ancestor's name. At its head stood an elder, chosen for his courage, his eloquence, his generosity. For among the Beti, you became great by giving. At the bilabi, great contests of gift-giving between lineages, people danced, and men competed in largesse.

Their sky was not empty: a creator God, Zamba, and ancestors held in honour. They knew that beside the world we see, there is a world we do not see. Day and night. Visible and invisible. Closely intertwined.

Keep that detail. You will need it at the water's edge.

[pause]

Where did they come from? Their traditions are clear: from the north. From beyond the Sanaga. From a land of savanna, of tall grass, of hills, towards the Mbam. And their hands remembered: their smelting furnaces looked like those of the Mafa of northern Cameroon, and their flutes were instruments of the savanna. As if they had carried the savanna deep into the forest.

The traditions speak of a common ancestor, Nanga, and his children. Kolo Beti, ancestor of the Ewondo. Eton Beti. Mvele Beti. A way of saying "we are kin".

And it is that kinship that makes the wound of the river so deep.

## S03 · Why they left
Why does anyone leave the land of their fathers?

The popular stories answer with a name. Usman dan Fodio. The great Fulani reformer who launched a holy war at the beginning of the nineteenth century. It is said that his horsemen drove before them the peoples who refused Islam, and that the Beti, fleeing the raids, went south.

But an honest griot does not stop at the first answer.

Today's historians look at the dates. The Fulani holy war reached the southern Adamawa only around 1875. Yet, by Laburthe-Tolra's calculations, the Bënë crossed the Sanaga around 1680. More than a century before dan Fodio.

Did the legend lie? No. It did what memory does: it gathered. The name of the most recent threat may have settled on a far older memory. Like a new label on an old basket. That is a hypothesis, and I give it to you as one.

What is certain is that the northern savannas were shaken for a long time. The Baare-Tchamba horsemen on their ponies, driven out by a great drought. Later, the call of the coast, of salt, guns and cloth. And family quarrels, forbidden loves that forced a couple to flee.

The Beti migration was not a single flight, a single night. It was a long movement, in waves, between the seventeenth and the nineteenth centuries.

[pause]

And within that movement there are men. Listen to this name; it is precious. In the memory of the Bënë, their crossing was led by a man called Owono Kode. He was given a nickname: Nnë Bodo. The ferryman of men.

Keep him close. For while history remembers a man who brought his people across, the legend remembers a serpent.

## S04 · The living bridge
Come closer to the fire. What follows is no longer history. It is the legend, as the elders still tell it.

They had been walking for a long time. Behind them, the savanna, the abandoned villages. Ahead of them, the forest. And one day, between the trees, they heard the roar.

[pause]

The Yom.

Wide. Powerful. No bridge. No ford. Too deep, too violent for a whole people. Behind them, perhaps, danger. Before them, the water.

What do you do when you can neither go forward nor go back?

In the version told in Ewondo by Elisabeth Belomo, recorded in Yaoundé in 2017, the people called out. And something answered. An immense serpent, Ngan Medza, came and lay across the river, from one bank to the other.

A living bridge.

But at first, no one knew it was a serpent.

Hear the words of the Ewondo patriarch Pierre Mebe. "Suddenly, from beneath the water, there rose a great tree trunk that joined the two banks to each other."

A trunk. That is what their eyes saw. Laid from bank to bank like a gift.

And that gift had a law. In Pierre Mebe's telling, it was forbidden to prick the trunk with the point of a spear. You could rest only the rounded butt upon it. In Elisabeth Belomo's telling, nothing sharp was allowed onto the bridge, and guardians watched over it.

Why did the people not know? Pierre Mebe explains: among the Beti, when you see a snake in the water, you do not call it a snake, but "a piece of wood of the water", for fear that people will be frightened.

You do not speak the name of the terrible thing. So that fear does not make those who walk on it fall.

So they crossed. Family after family. Children clinging to their mothers' backs. Men holding their spears by the rounded end. Old people guided step by step. And for a long time, the rule was kept.

## S05 · The night the bridge broke
Then night came.

To light the way, bamboo torches were kindled. And here the voices part, as the people are about to part.

According to Pierre Mebe, an ill-intentioned man drove the point of his spear into the trunk. According to Elisabeth Belomo, it was a chief who let his lit torch fall onto the bridge, together with his sword. According to other stories, the torchbearer walked at the rear, and sparks fell on the creature's back.

Fire, or iron. In every version, the law was broken.

[pause]

And the trunk revealed itself.

The burn, or the wound, woke the thing beneath their feet. The serpent shook. It rolled over, or it sank to the bottom of the water. Those on its back fell into the river and were swept away. And only then, the storyteller says, did the people understand that they had been walking on a serpent.

[long silence]

Then, the silence of the water.

On the southern bank, those who had had time to cross. On the northern bank, those who had not yet stepped on. Between them, the black river. And no bridge any more.

Pierre Mebe says it in one sentence: "the Beti were divided into two groups: some on this side of the Sanaga, the others on the other."

That, say the elders, is why you have cousins on the other side of the water.

## S06 · A thousand mouths, a thousand serpents
You think you know the story? Wait. A living myth never has a single mouth.

Among the Manguissa, a Beti people of the Sanaga, the serpent does not come of its own accord. It is summoned by a man, the ancestor Aso Abanda. On the riverbank, he struck the water with his magic staff. And from the river, the tradition says, he made a rainbow rise. The rainbow settled on the water, and became a python. On its back, they crossed.

An arc of light coming down from the sky and lying upon the river as it takes on flesh. Here, it is the power of an ancestor that opens the way.

[pause]

Now cross the border, to the Fang of Gabon. There, the storytellers speak mostly of a gigantic tree, an adzap, blocking the way at the bottom of a valley. They bored right through it. The place is called Odzamboga, and women sang of it in the fields: "When we were coming back from Odzamboga".

But the serpent lives among the Fang as well. Pursued, they reach the river Nyong. A chief named Osellé Bevouhou performs a miracle: a great serpent serves as a bridge. And that bridge breaks as the enemies arrive.

Here, the breaking is not a curse that separates brothers. It is a deliverance that saves the fugitives. And the pursuers, in these stories, are the Mvele. Elsewhere, one of the children of Nanga. A Beti brother. One myth turns around, depending on the bank from which you look at it.

A Fang storyteller from Bitam told how the ancestor Afiri Kara, before he died, asked that his skull be kept to protect his descendants. For among the Fang, long ago, every migration carried the basket holding the skulls of the ancestors. You never migrate alone. You migrate with your dead.

Even the river changes. The Sanaga for most Beti. The Nyong for some Fang. And the word Yom may refer to the Lom, a tributary of the Sanaga.

You see? A myth is not a photograph. It is a river too. It changes its bed. It divides. It keeps its source.

## S07 · What the serpent says
So what does Ngan Medza mean?

For Laburthe-Tolra, the name does not refer only to the serpent of long ago. Among the ancient Beti, every powerful man was believed to have "his own ngan medza". An invisible python, which protected him and secured his prosperity. Laburthe-Tolra translates the word as "ruin for every hostile charm".

So Ngan Medza is the name of a protection. Whoever crosses on its back is a protected man.

And the python, among the Beti, carries great weight. The word mvom means "python", but also "happiness", "luck". In the great initiation rite of boys, the So, it was said that the python swallows everything. You go into the serpent a boy. You come out a man. Just as the people, entering the river as fugitives, come out on the far bank as the people of the forest.

But do not make Ngan Medza a god, or a totem. It is nobody's ancestor. It is a power that carries. And then withdraws.

[pause]

Why does it withdraw? Because the law was broken. That may be the deepest lesson of all. The power that saves is not owed to you. It demands respect. Do not pierce. Do not burn. Do not wound what carries you. A single act of violence, and the whole people pays.

Out of a slow dispersal, made of wars, flights and trade, the myth makes one tragic night. It tells the Ewondo of Yaoundé, the Bulu of Ebolowa, the Fang of Oyem: we were on the same road. What history scatters, myth gathers.

Much later, with the Christian missions, the serpent was often placed on the devil's side. If some are afraid today to say its name, it is also because a century of preaching taught people to fear what the ancestors respected.

Perhaps behind every legendary serpent there were ferrymen, like Nnë Bodo. Canoes. Nights of fear and courage. And a people chose to remember them in the highest form it knew.

## S08 · The Sanaga today
And now, what remains of Ngan Medza?

A story remains, read in a school textbook, drawn in a picture book for children.

A festival remains. On the banks of the Sanaga, at Batchenga, a festival bears the serpent's name. Dances, exhibitions, meetings between peoples, to recall a shared history.

A bridge remains. A real one, made of concrete, built at Ebebda in 1982. When you cross it, beneath your wheels, a work of engineers. In your memory, a serpent.

[pause]

And a question remains. The one I leave with you.

Perhaps you are Ewondo, Eton, Bënë, Bulu, Fang. Perhaps you were born far from here, and no longer speak your grandparents' language.

This myth does not ask you to believe that a giant serpent truly carried your ancestors. It asks you to understand what they wanted to hand down to you. That your people crossed the uncrossable, in waves, with no king to command it, guided by ferrymen and by a shared word. That it knew fear, flight and loss, and kept going. That those who stayed on the other side are not strangers. They are cousins.

Historians argue over the dates. Storytellers do not all tell the same night. But all agree on what matters. There was a river. There was a crossing. There was a price.

So the next time you see the Sanaga shining under the evening sun, do not pass by without looking at it. It is not just water. It is the frontier your fathers crossed. It is the back of a serpent that dived long ago. It is the bond you can mend.

[long silence]

Hold your spear by the rounded end. Do not wound what carries you.

This is where you come from.
