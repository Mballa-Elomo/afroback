# Queen Nzinga (Njinga a Mbande)

Queen of Ndongo and Matamba (present-day Angola), 17th century.

---

## The griot's tale

Listen.

Long ago, on the highlands bordering the Kwanza River, in what is now Angola, there was a kingdom called Ndongo. Its people, the Mbundu, called their sovereign the *ngola*, and it is from that word, twisted by Portuguese tongues, that the whole country later took its name. We are at the turn of the sixteenth into the seventeenth century. Portuguese ships have been sailing up this coast for generations already, and with them comes a wound that never heals: the trade in human beings, torn from their land to be sold across the ocean.

It is into this world that, around 1583, a daughter is born in the household of King Kiluanji kia Samba. It is said her birth was difficult, the umbilical cord wound around her neck, and that this is why she was named Njinga, from a Kimbundu word evoking the act of twisting, of winding. The elders would later say that this child was born with the twist already in her, a sign of what she would become: someone who would never let herself be bent straight by another's will.

Her father sees in her something few fathers of his rank saw in their daughters at that time. He keeps her close, brings her to council, has her trained in combat, in the war axe. While other children of the palace learn to stay silent, Njinga learns to speak, to observe, to understand power down to its finest workings.

But power, in royal courts, never fully forgives closeness. When her father dies, it is her half-brother Mbandi who takes the throne. And Mbandi fears her. Fears her intelligence, her popularity, what she represents. Sources agree that he has Njinga's young son killed and forces the sterilization of the women who might give him rivals to the throne, her and her sisters. Imagine the pain of that. Imagine what it is to survive a brother who has decided your line will not continue.

Njinga flees, takes refuge in Matamba, waits. And then one day, because the kingdom is cornered, crushed under Portuguese pressure, Mbandi has no choice left: he needs her. He sends her to negotiate.

In 1621, Njinga travels to Luanda, the Portuguese stronghold, to meet Governor João Correia de Sousa. And it is here that legend and history begin to weave together, as often happens when a destiny grows too large for ordinary memory. It is told, and this story has crossed the centuries down to us, that in the room where she was to negotiate, there was only a mat laid on the floor for her, while the governor and his officers sat enthroned on chairs. Njinga, it is said, signaled one of her servants to get down on all fours, and sat upon her back, upright, her gaze level with the governor's, to negotiate as an equal.

I must be honest with you here: this scene, powerful as it is, does not come to us from a witness who saw it with their own eyes that day. It comes from accounts gathered decades later, notably by the military chronicler Cadornega and Capuchin missionaries, who collected the court's oral memory, and perhaps the testimony of Njinga herself in old age, in the 1650s. It is a story that speaks a deep truth about who she was, a woman who refused to be treated as inferior. But it is a tradition, passed down, shaped by time, not a transcript of the negotiation.

What is, however, well attested is that she secured a peace agreement for Ndongo, that she was baptized in the Catholic faith under the name Ana de Sousa, in honor of the governor's wife who became her godmother, and that she returned home in 1622 in triumph, having made those who believed they could bend everyone by force bend instead through words and diplomatic cunning.

Three years later, in 1624, Mbandi dies under circumstances the sources do not clearly settle, suicide according to some, poisoning according to others. Njinga becomes queen.

And here begins the fight of a lifetime.

Becoming queen, for her, is not enough to be queen in practice. Rival nobles contest her, some because she was born of an enslaved mother rather than the first wife, others simply because she is a woman. It must be said plainly: in this struggle to secure her power, Njinga eliminates rivals, including, several sources say, a child nephew who might one day have claimed the throne. This is not a gilded legend I am telling you, it is the story of a woman in a world where power was won and kept in blood.

Facing her, the Portuguese choose a rival, refuse to recognize her, and in March 1626 declare open war on her. Njinga loses. She loses her army, her officers, her two sisters are captured. By 1628, driven from her own kingdom, she has only about two hundred fighters left.

This is the moment when another woman might have stopped. Njinga instead turns to a people feared by all, the Imbangala, nomadic warriors organized into military bands, whose initiation rites demanded she drink from the cup of the blood oath and abandon the codes of Mbundu royalty for the harsher codes of war. She accepts. She marries their chief, Kasanje, learns their ways, becomes one of them. It was the price of survival, and of return.

She returns. Between 1631 and 1635, leading these warriors, she conquers the neighboring kingdom of Matamba, deposes its queen, and takes this new throne, one that already had a tradition of female sovereigns.

Here, a truth must be looked at straight on, a truth an honest telling cannot hide. Njinga, to finance her wars and weaken the Portuguese monopoly, actively took part in the trade of human beings. Historians estimate that over the course of her reign, tens of thousands of captives, perhaps close to two hundred thousand by some estimates, were sold under her authority or that of her armies, notably to Dutch traders who became her allies from 1641 onward. She was not only the resistance figure we like to celebrate. She was also, at certain moments of her reign, a cog in the very system she otherwise fought weapon in hand against the Portuguese colonizer. More recent historians nuance this picture by showing she also sought to limit exports to war captives only and to reposition her kingdom as a trade intermediary. But nuance does not erase. Both truths coexist, and that is the real story.

With Dutch support, she retakes much of Ndongo between 1641 and 1644, winning victories at Ngoleme and Kombi. But in 1648, the Portuguese fleet retakes Luanda, the Dutch evacuate Angola without even warning their ally, and Njinga finds herself alone once more.

She is by then nearly sixty-five years old. She does not lay down her weapons, but she shifts the battlefield. She leans increasingly on Capuchin missionaries, spreads Christianity in her kingdom, at times harshly toward those who refuse to abandon the old cults. After nearly twenty-five years of continuous war, exhausted as much as her adversary, she signs a peace treaty with Portugal in 1656: she cedes coastal lands, gains in exchange a form of trade monopoly for her capital, and above all, keeps her sovereignty over Matamba. The kingdom is not colonized. That is the ultimate victory of a lifetime of resistance: to have reached the end, old, standing, still queen.

Her final years she devotes to rebuilding. She resettles former freed slaves, reforms the laws of her kingdom, once again allows the women of her camp to bear children, something the Imbangala warrior customs had forbidden. She marries, in a Christian ceremony, the man who had long been her companion.

She dies on 17 December 1663, in her sleep, at eighty years old. The Portuguese of Luanda and the Mbundu of Matamba both hold ceremonies in her honor, each in their own way. Her sister Kambu succeeds her on the throne.

That was Njinga. Not a smooth statue. A woman born into a world that twisted women to keep them from ruling, and who bent that world to her will for more than forty years, through weapons, through diplomacy, through faith, through cunning, and at times through choices history cannot fully absolve. She resisted one of the most voracious colonial empires of her time without ever losing her crown.

If you descend from this land that watched her fight into being, remember this: dignity was never asked for, it was taken. Njinga never let herself be defined by those who wanted to bend her. Stand tall. You come from there.

## Reference sheet

**Nzinga Mbandi (Njinga a Mbande), Queen of Ndongo and Matamba**

- **Era**: precolonial, reign from 1624 to 1663
- **Region**: Central Africa (kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba, present-day Angola)
- **Theme**: resistance / politics (diplomacy, war, royal sovereignty)

**Timeline**

- c. 1583: born in Kabasa, daughter of King Kiluanji kia Samba and Kangela
- 1621-1622: embassy to Luanda to negotiate peace on behalf of her brother Ngola Mbandi; Catholic baptism under the name Ana de Sousa (1622)
- 1624: death of Mbandi, Njinga's accession to the throne of Ndongo
- 15 March 1626: Portugal declares war on Njinga; successive defeats, expulsion from her kingdom around 1628-1629
- 1631-1635: alliance with and integration into the Imbangala; conquest of the kingdom of Matamba, of which she becomes queen
- 1641-1648: military alliance with the United Provinces (Dutch) against Portugal; partial reconquest of Ndongo; victories at Ngoleme (1644) and Kombi (1647); Dutch withdrawal after the Portuguese retake Luanda (1648)
- 1656: signing of a peace treaty with Portugal after nearly 25 years of war; she retains sovereignty over Matamba
- 17 December 1663: death in Matamba; succeeded by her sister Kambu (Barbara)

**Notable quotes**

No direct quote from Njinga has been authenticated against a reliable, dated primary source. The only reported statement with a relatively precise attribution comes from the Capuchin missionary Cavazzi (c. 1662), who reports that, recalling her youth, she claimed to be able to "face twenty-five armed men" during a military review: an indirect account relayed by a European third party, to be treated with caution, not a word-for-word verified quote.

**Associated legends (explicitly flagged as such)**

- **The "human throne" anecdote**: during the 1621-1622 negotiations in Luanda, Njinga is said to have had a servant kneel down so she could sit on her back and negotiate at eye level with the Portuguese governor, for lack of a seat provided for her. This episode is widely repeated in popular memory and captures her character well, but it comes from accounts gathered after the fact (chronicler Cadornega, Capuchin missionaries, court oral memory collected in the 1650s), not from an eyewitness account dated to the time of the events. To be told as a powerful piece of tradition, never as an established fact on the same footing as a battle date.
- **Imbangala practices (blood rites, ritual cannibalism)**: sources agree that Njinga underwent Imbangala initiation rites (blood oath) to seal her alliance around 1629-1631. However, the accusations of cannibalism personally attributed to her by some European chroniclers of the time (notably Father Cavazzi) are viewed by current historians as poorly substantiated charges, reflecting more the hostile and sensationalist gaze of European observers than verified facts about her (she herself reportedly told European visitors she did not eat human flesh, while tolerating the practice among some of her soldiers).
