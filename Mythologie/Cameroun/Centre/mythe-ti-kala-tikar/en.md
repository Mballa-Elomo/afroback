# "Ti kala!" The Banished Prince and the Lost Staff

Origin and foundation myth of the Tikar of the Mbam (Ngambé-Tikar, Nditam, Gâ and their sister villages), Mbam-et-Kim division, Centre Region, Cameroon. Version 1, 2026-10-16 (research file in `recherche.md`). This story completes the story of Nchare Yen (`Ouest/mythe-nchare-yen-bamoun/`): here, the same events are seen from the other bank.

> This story is a legend from oral tradition. The griot tells it as a legend, and at every step he says what historians know, what they argue about, and what no one can settle.

---

## The griot's tale

### I. A people named after an insult

Listen.

Travel north of Bafia, past the last cocoa farms, to where the forest begins to thin and the savannah comes in patches, like an open hand. Cross the Guérima on the ferry. Keep going. You come to a river the maps call the Kim, which a few kilometres further on flows into the Mbam. That marriage of two waters gave the division its name: Mbam-et-Kim. For a long time you needed a ferryman to get from one bank to the other. The bridge over the Kim has only been in service since 1996. Before it, in 1889, a German officer passing through, Curt von Morgen, described the liana bridges people here strung from one tree to another, some ten feet above the water, woven like baskets and swaying so hard that they could only be crossed one person at a time.

On the other side lies a country. Six thousand square kilometres of mingled savannah and forest, so thinly settled that there are barely two inhabitants per square kilometre, according to the historian Wang Sonne of the University of Yaoundé I, who travelled through it in the 1990s. Eleven large villages are spread across it: Nditam, Ngoumé, Gba, Kpaga, Mamblang, Ngambé, Gâ, Kong, Ina, Ouè and Beng-Beng. About twelve thousand people, he said in the year 2000. These are the Tikar of the Mbam.

Remember that name. Tikar. You may have heard it in Foumban, where they say the founder of the Bamum was a Tikar prince. You may have heard it in Kumbo among the Nso, or in Bafut, or in Bamenda, where so many royal families say: our ancestors came from Tikar country. It is a name that has travelled further than the people who bear it. A name that distant kings claim as a title of nobility.

And yet, listen to what the elders of the plain say. At the beginning, this name was not a title. It was an insult. An angry word thrown by a father at his son, before an assembly of notables, on the day everything broke. A word that meant: get out of here. Go outside. You are no longer one of us.

"Ti kala!"

A whole people that has carried, for generations, the very sentence with which its ancestor was driven away. How many peoples have managed to take an insult, turn it around, and make it their name?

But the story does not end with the insult. There will be a lost staff, a brother who loses his way looking for it, a younger brother held back by a tree whose fruit is too good, monkeys greeting a stranger. And at the end of the road, at Nditam, a man who looks at the land and says: here, I feel at home.

The Bamum have their own story, the story of Nchare Yen, which AFROBACK has already told you. The Tikar of the Mbam tell the same scattering from their own bank. And seen from their bank, the man Foumban calls its founder is only the younger brother who got lost.

Sit down. I will tell you what the elders say, then what the researchers say. And I will always tell you who is speaking.

### II. The Belaka and the beloved son

To understand the insult, you must first know the man who spoke it. And to know him, you must leave the Mbam and travel far to the north, onto the high Adamawa plateau, to the country of the Mbum, also written Mboum.

Every version I have been able to read agrees on this point: the ancestors of the Tikar were Mbum. Wang Sonne places their cradle in the region of Ngâ, near Ngaoundéré. He gives the meaning of these names in the Mbum language: Ngâ would mean "stone", and Ngaoundéré "the mountain of stone". Other authors name Nganha, a great Mbum chiefdom lying, writes the political scientist Ibrahim Mouiche, sixty-five kilometres from Ngaoundéré. The tradition of Bankim says Ngan-ha. Remember the stone, for it will come back in this story.

Who ruled the Mbum? A sovereign who bore the title of Belaka. The historian Thierno Mouctar Bah, who taught at the University of Yaoundé, describes him as a sacred ruler on whom the prosperity of the land depended. People asked him for rain when the drought went on too long. He alone could set his seat on a leopard skin. But, Bah adds, "the office was not without danger, for the Mbum practised ritual regicide". To curb tyranny, they are said to have adopted a remarkable rule, a single seven-year term: a reign of seven years and no more. Bah notes that this principle lasted several centuries, until the Fulani conquest. Keep this in mind. Seven years. We will meet that number again on the banks of the Mbam.

It is in this court that the drama breaks out. Hear it as Wang Sonne gathered it, drawing on what he calls "numerous concurring sources": the Cameroonian historian Eldridge Mohammadou, the missionary Bernard Beemster, and a colonial administrator named Charrin who, as early as 1932, had written down the traditions of the Tikar.

The Belaka had a son. His name was Klong, or Klo. He was not just any son. Wang Sonne says he was "cherished", "beloved". The one his father looked upon with pride. The one everyone thought might one day sit in his place.

And that is exactly what undid him.

For in a royal court, a king's love for a son breeds jealousy. Some of the Belaka's wives saw clearly that Klong might one day succeed his father. Tradition does not give their reasons; it gives their scheme. They spread a rumour. They went to their husband and whispered that the young prince was courting one of them. That he had even slept with her.

Imagine the shock. The favourite son, accused of taking one of the king's wives, his own father's wife. Such an accusation could cost a man a throne: in 1889, at Tibati, Curt von Morgen met an elder brother of the ruler who had been passed over for the succession, he explained, for seducing other men's wives. And the Belaka believed the rumour.

He did not make a scene at once. He did something worse: he shut the door. He stopped involving Klong in the affairs of the court. Then one day he summoned a great council of notables. All the important men of the land were there. Only one had not been invited: his son.

Klong heard of the meeting. He did not stay home and brood. He came. He walked into the council where no one expected him, and he took his place by force. Did he mean to defend himself? Tradition does not give us his words. It gives us only his father's.

The Belaka, startled to see him there in front of everyone, ordered him "sharply", writes Wang Sonne, in the Mbum language:

"Vi ti kala je!"

Get out of here. Go outside.

A silence must have fallen over the assembly. For this was not merely an order to leave the house. Klong understood, says the tradition, that the father who had loved him so much now found him unwanted in this land. He decided, says Wang Sonne, "to go bravely and seek refuge elsewhere".

And the long march began.

This, my child, is how the Tikar tell their own birth. Not through a victory. Not through a miracle. Through an injustice, a public humiliation, and the decision of a young man not to stay where he was no longer wanted. From that word, "ti kala", is said to have come the name Tikar, "which would be attached to a whole people to this day".

### III. Four mouths, one word

In 1992, a major conference on the peoples of Adamawa was held in Ngaoundéré. Among the speakers were a Catholic missionary, Father Bernard Beemster, who had lived in Bankim for ten years from 1974, and two men from Bankim, Basile Tchimi and Paul Mvouin. Their paper opens with a sentence I give you word for word: "All the Tikar know a legend which explains the birth of their people from a quarrel between the Chief (or 'Belaka') of the Mboum (east of Ngaoundéré) and one of his sons, called 'Klo'', accused of having made love with one of the king's wives."

Look closely. In Beemster's version, the prince is "accused". It does not say he was innocent, nor that he was guilty. It leaves the question open. The father, "on learning of this shame", says to him: "Ti kàlà jè", in Mbum, "get out of here", condemning his son and a few of his friends "to a life of wandering on the high plateaus of Adamawa". In time the small clan of Klo' multiplied, reached the Mbam, crossed the river and invaded the country of the Tumu at Bankim.

Now listen to a second mouth. In a book published in 2012 by CODESRIA, the Cameroonian political scientist Ibrahim Mouiche of the University of Yaoundé II reports a version he takes from Eldridge Mohammadou. Here it is no longer a son. It is a brother. A Mbum chief, jealous of the affection the people felt for one of his brothers, hurled the word "tikar", "go away from here", at him during a violent quarrel. The brother, furious, left the region. And here is the finest part: he did not leave alone. "The greater part of the population" followed him and made him their chief. "All decided, on the day of their departure, to take the name of Tikar so that the insult done to the man they had just chosen as their chief would never be forgotten."

Do you hear the difference? In the first version, a lone son is thrown out. In this one, a whole people chooses to leave with him, and takes the insult as its name, deliberately, the way you might keep a scar so as to remember. It is no longer one man's shame. It is a crowd's pride.

Listen to a third mouth, the voice of Bankim today. On 8 December 2013, at the Gaen festival, the great celebration of Bankim, His Majesty Mvouen Vincent, chief of Ngatti, gave an opening speech that a history student, Nadine Josiane Houambo Mvounga, recorded in the dissertation she defended at the École normale supérieure in Yaoundé in 2015. In this speech, the one who is driven out is not a prince. She is a princess. Her name is Wouten. Her brothers, after quarrels over the succession, bar her from the sacred house reserved for men, telling her in Mbum: "Tinkala jé", "go away from here". Wounded, Wouten leaves her home village of Ngan-ha, followed by two generals loyal to her, Tinki and Klo. "From then on they would take the name of the Tinkala people, which has today become the name of the Tikar people."

Klo is still there, you see. But he is no longer the banished son; he is a woman's general. And this version does not stand alone. On the other side of the mountains, among the Nso, Nso historians also say that their dynasty descends from a princess named Wou-Ten, who is said to have reigned at Rifem, that is, Bankim. The student from Bankim even states that "all the Tikar are unanimous" that the chiefdom of Bankim was founded by Princess Wouten. That is her voice; I give it to you as such, for the historian of the Mbam, Wang Sonne, puts Klong at the centre.

There is a fourth mouth, which I could not hear. The linguist Claude Hagège, who stayed in Bankim, published in 1969 an *Esquisse linguistique du tikar* (A Linguistic Sketch of Tikar) in which, according to Beemster, he records "four versions of the legend". I have not been able to read that book, and I would rather tell you so than invent them.

Now lay these stories side by side, like calabashes on a mat. A slandered son. An accused son. A brother too dear to the people. A sister shut out of the men's house. The person changes. The motive changes: the wives' jealousy, the brother's jealousy, the quarrel over succession. But look at what never changes.

Always, someone of royal blood is rejected by his own, at the very heart of power.

Always, a word of rejection is spoken in the Mbum language, and it always sounds the same: ti kala, tinkala, tikar.

Always, the one who is driven out does not come back to beg. He leaves, he is followed, and he founds.

That is what the myth does for those who tell it. First, it explains a name. Next, it says where the chiefs' blood comes from: from the court of a Belaka, a sacred king who made the rain fall. A Tikar chief is not just anyone; he descends, the story says, from a prince of Nganha. Finally, and this may be the deepest part, it turns a wound into a foundation. The Tikar do not say: we are the ones who conquered. They say: we are the ones who were put out of the door, and look at what we have become.

Notice, too, the place of women, which shifts from one version to another. In one, jealous wives bring about the prince's fall with a rumour. In another, a woman is the founder, and it is men who shut her out. As if the story itself were hesitating over the place of women at the heart of power. That is no accident in a society where, as Basile Tchimi, a son of Bankim, writes, some titles pass only through mothers: you can only be a notable, a "me-ki", if your mother is a princess.

### IV. Kimi, in the land of the Tumu

How long did the wandering last? Wang Sonne admits that it is "difficult to estimate the length of this movement". The little clan grew as it walked, and at last reached the upper valley of the Mbam, at the place called Kimi, which the administration would later call Bankim.

A people already lived there. They were called the Tumu.

And here again the mouths disagree, and the disagreement is precious. Wang Sonne says Klong's clan subdued the Tumu and took their lands. Beemster says it "invaded the country of the Tumu at Bankim to subdue them and end up merging with them". But the Bankim tradition recorded in 2014 by Houambo Mvounga tells another story: the Tumu were at war with their Mambila neighbours, and the newcomers helped them drive them back. After the victory, the Tumu are said to have handed them authority. The Oxford anthropologist David Zeitlyn heard a similar version: Kimi was founded when the Tumu asked the Tikar to cross the Mbam to protect them from other neighbours, the Kwanja. Conquest or alliance? Both memories live side by side.

But on one point every source agrees, and it is the most surprising. The victors took the language of the vanquished. Basile Tchimi writes it plainly: "Tikar is a tonal language. It is also the language of the Tumu." The Tikar language is not the Mbum of the ancestors who came from the north: it belongs to the Bantoid family, the language of the people of the plain. As early as 1932, the administrator Charrin had heard among the Tikar the memory of a time when their ancestors gave up their original language.

A small group takes power, and it is the one that learns the others' language. That is not how great armies behave; it is how a handful of men behave when they need to be accepted. According to Mouiche, Hagège concluded that the migrants were probably not very numerous. And memory still keeps both names. Beemster, who lived in Bankim, wrote in 1992 that the administration had given them the name Tikar, "but in reality, they call themselves Tumu".

And yet the conquest has not been forgotten. It is played out again every year. In Bankim, the greatest traditional festival is called the Gaen. Houambo Mvounga, who described it, writes that it is "a victory festival which re-enacts the invasion of the Tùmu country by the Tinkala (Tikar)". The war chiefs, the Wouampang, lead the procession from compound to compound among the great notables; behind them march the war notables, the Mé Mgbéwoui, in battle dress, with bows and arrows, spears, swords and assegais. At the height of the festival, in the royal courtyard, the chief himself, the Mveing Kimi, comes out in his ceremonial robes and performs war displays to the cheers of his people. The myth does not sleep in a book. It dances.

There are other festivals, and each says something about this people. The Great Fishing, at the end of the dry season, when the lakes have dropped: the notables who keep each lake pour palm oil or salt into it, asking the ancestors to give fish, and the size of the catch tells whether the ancestors are pleased. The Dain, the sacred drum, whose coming-out is celebrated around November, when the fields are done, beneath a great kapok tree before the palace; only initiates may touch it. The Switi, the festival of the dead: it begins at the cemetery of kings and princes, then each family visits its graves and pours nkang, maize wine, on them.

For among the Tikar, writes Tchimi, the dead have not gone away. They are watching. They can return in dreams to call to order anyone who strays, and the village chief is the most closely watched of all. The righteous ancestors live with God, on a mountain; a legend says that God passes every morning and every evening to give them food. The wicked dead torment the living for a while, until the Most High turns them into harmless termite mounds. Look at the next termite mound you pass in the savannah, and remember that.

Let us go back to Klong. He reigned, says Wang Sonne, and he died. And it is after his death that the story tightens like a knot.

For the clan had grown, and there were more and more claimants to the throne. So, still according to Wang Sonne, new rules were laid down. The most terrible of them: after seven years of reign, a chief had to be killed, with his wife's complicity, to make way for the next. And the next would be subject to the same law.

Seven years. Do you remember the Belaka and his single seven-year term? I must be honest: among the sources I have read, only Wang Sonne tells of this killing for Bankim. But the number echoes elsewhere. The list of the chiefs of Bankim, drawn up by the local genealogist Abbo Timothée Nyedjim and quoted in the 2015 dissertation, contains a period it calls precisely "the seven-year term", five chiefs of seven years each. And Thierno Mouctar Bah attributes ritual regicide and the single seven-year reign to the Mbum themselves. What no one can say is whether this law was really applied at the time of our story.

For at Klong's death, it was one of his younger brothers who was chosen. His name was Mbe.

And Mbe could count.

The years went by. Six. Then almost seven. The term was drawing near. Mbe could see the day coming when they would come for him. So, says Wang Sonne, fearing he would be removed, he decided to leave. He did not leave alone: he left "with the complicity of one of his favourite wives". And the source adds a detail that I pass on to you without comment, because I know nothing more: "She is also his sister." Notice only the terrible irony of the situation: the law wanted the wife to be an accomplice in the king's death; here, the wife is an accomplice in his escape.

The migration began, it is said, behind the site where the royal palace of Bankim stands today. And with Mbe went other heads of families, who would found the chiefdoms of Ngambé, Kong, Ouè and Ina. Eldridge Mohammadou, according to Wang Sonne, distinguished four specific migration currents. The great departure to the south had begun.

### V. The lost staff

Several people walked behind Mbe. Wang Sonne names three who matter more than the rest: two younger brothers, Nchare and Gwatou, and a younger sister, Ngonsok, also written Ngoenchok. Remember these names. Each of them will become the root of a people.

The company leaves the savannah and enters the forest, heading for the Mbam. And there, on the road, Mbe realises something terrible.

He has lost his staff of office.

A staff of office is not a stick to lean on. It is power made visible, the thing that keeps a man on the run a king rather than a mere fugitive. And Mbe, who has just escaped the seven-year law, has nothing else left to prove it.

So he turns to his younger brother Nchare and sends him to find it.

Nchare goes back the way they came. He searches. He searches for a long time. And he loses his way. He does not find the staff. Nor does he find the trail of his companions. The forest has closed behind him. So, say the Tikar of the Mbam, he resolves to stop where he is, and to the west, on the right bank of the Mbam, he founds the Bamum kingdom.

And Ngonsok, the sister? She waits. She waits for Nchare, who does not come back. "Tired of waiting", writes Wang Sonne, she takes the road again to the north-west, and founds the chiefdom of Nso.

So this is the great scattering as seen from Nditam. No race around a stone. No canoe broken by a trick. A lost staff, a brother who goes to look for it and gets lost, a sister weary of waiting. Here the kingdoms are not born of rivalry, but of going astray.

You know the Foumban version, told in AFROBACK's story of Nchare Yen: the race, the foot set on the stone, the canoe smashed on the Mapé. I will not repeat it. But lay the two stories side by side, and look.

In Foumban, in the version that the art historian Idrissou Njoya, who teaches in Foumban, gives from palace tradition, it is Nchare who crosses first and cuts off the way. The brother left on the bank, whom this version calls Morumta, goes downstream, settles at Njimbam, that is Nditam, and becomes the Mfon'mbam, the king of the Mbam. Among the Nso it is something else again: Nso historians say Ngonnso was the eldest sister, that her brothers Nchare and Mbe refused to take her because she was married, and that they cut the bridge behind them. Among the Bamum, finally, the Bamum writer Maître Aboubakar Mounpain says that Queen Yen had the boat destroyed, leaving behind two of her children, among them Fomban, who founded Ditam.

The names shift: Mbe, Morumta, Fomban, Mfombam. But look: it is always the same figure, the brother who ends up at Nditam.

And above all, look at who is the elder. In Foumban, the hero, the one who decides, is Nchare. In Nditam, the hero, the one who holds the staff, is Mbe, and Nchare is only a younger brother sent on an errand who failed to bring back what he was asked for. Each people tells the parting from its own bank, and each gives itself the better part. That is not a lie. It is how myths speak of the order of the world: who is the elder, who owes respect to whom.

Wang Sonne says so, with a hint of bitterness, in the conclusion of his paper: the elders, who led the movement, settled in the Mbam; their younger brothers founded chiefdoms at Foumban and among the Nso; and since then, Muslim and colonial influences "seem to give precedence to the younger". The Bamum became a great kingdom, known as far as Europe; Nditam remained a remote village. The myth of the lost staff recalls something else: before you were the most powerful, you were the youngest.

And here is a curious thing, which I offer only as a griot's observation, since no source puts it this way. The Cameroonian scholar Leopold Duala-M'bedy, who did fieldwork in Foumban in 1959, notes that the Bamum king is the only one allowed to touch a sacred staff said to have been brought by Nchare. The Tikar say Nchare went to look for a staff and never found it; the Bamum say their king keeps a staff that came from Nchare. No one has told me they are the same staff. But the coincidence is worth a look.

And there is an older echo still. Among the Mbum, Thierno Mouctar Bah reports, the migration had been placed under the protection of a "great fetish", and the loss of that object is said to have marked the scattering of the group. To lose the object of power is to scatter. Mbe's ancestors may already have known it.

Mbe does not go back. He does not wait for Nchare. He crosses the Mbam to the left bank. With his followers there still walks one of his younger brothers: Gwatou.

### VI. The gâ tree and the monkeys of Nditam

On the left bank of the Mbam, the company stops to rest in the shade of a great fruit tree. The elders call it gâ.

Gwatou is hungry. He tastes the fruit and finds it delicious. Mbe, the elder, is indulgent: he lets him eat, and asks him to catch up with him afterwards on his way to the Kim. Then he sets off again with his people.

Gwatou eats. He eats until he is full. Then he gets up and looks for the path his companions took. But the bush has wiped out their tracks. He cannot find them. So, Wang Sonne tells us, he goes back to the foot of the tree, since its fruit is so good, and settles there.

That is how the village of Gâ was born, once called Gahum. And since Gwatou, every chief of Gâ has borne the title of Mbonga, the chief of Gâ, and is greeted respectfully as "Mbongâ". A detail that makes people smile: the village's Catholic school is called the Bonga School, and the people who use it, says Wang Sonne, hear it as "the school of Good Welcome" ("Bon Accueil"). That is a pun, not an etymology. But it says a lot about how a village sees itself: a place where you stop because you feel good there.

What was this tree? Wang Sonne himself writes that "there is reason to wonder about the true nature of this fruit tree". No source I have read identifies it. Rather than invent a learned name for you, I leave the gâ tree to its mystery.

Mbe, for his part, goes on. He is now alone with his followers: no staff, no Nchare, no Gwatou, no Ngonsok. He crosses the Kim. He stops at the foot of a mountain, Mount Nami, a stretch of savannah not far from present-day Nditam. And there he is not alone: the land is inhabited by Balom, whom the source also calls Mebi, and by Djanti.

Tradition says Mbe drove them back "thanks above all to his long experience as a blacksmith".

Stop at that word. Blacksmith. The prince fleeing death, who has lost his staff, still carries something more precious: the knowledge of iron. Wang Sonne recalls that the Tikar of the Mbam worked iron into hoes, machetes and spears, and that the very name of the village of Kong means, in Tikar, the hoe; its chief, the Nyakong, was charged with making sure his people worked the land. And Zeitlyn, summarising Mohammadou, notes that the horsemen from the north who overturned the region in the eighteenth century travelled with "an important group of blacksmiths". The blacksmith Mbe of the myth may preserve the memory of a time when iron made the law: that is a griot's hypothesis, nothing more.

And then comes the gentlest image in the whole story. When Mbe and his company settle, monkeys perched in the branches start calling. Not cries of fear or threat. "Friendly" cries, says the tradition, as if greeting new companions. These people from the savannah, who had never lived in the forest, feel welcome. And they say: "Ndè ntè." Which means, according to Wang Sonne, "something I take pleasure in loving", or again: "this land is pleasant, I am at ease here".

From this, it is said, comes the name Nditam.

Listen carefully to what the source adds: "Ndè ntè" is in the Balom language. So the name of the village Mbe founded would come from the language of the people he drove away. As at Bankim, where the victors took the language of the Tumu, the land here keeps the words of those who were there before. And do not forget the other side of this lovely story: for Mbe to feel "at ease here", Balom and Djanti had to leave. The inventory of the myths of the Centre Region compiled for AFROBACK found no published mythology of the Balom; their version of this encounter, if they have one, has not been written down.

Wang Sonne sees one philosophy in three names. Gâ, the village where you stop because the fruit is good. Nditam, the village where the monkeys say welcome. And Ngambé, whose old name was Djouém-Flouéh, "fresh garden eggs" (aubergines); even today, when you ask someone how they are, they answer "flouéh", "I'm well". Three names that all say the same thing: this land is good, and people are welcomed here.

The founding story has never stopped repeating itself. Even in the twentieth century, Wang Sonne tells us, a prince of Gâ named Ndié went off to found Gandié on the Kim; in 1936, after a dispute with the chief of Ngambé, Prince Martin Nyandji left to create his own village, Kpaga; and Ngoumé became independent when a man named Kpo broke away from his father's family. The younger one who leaves and founds: the myth is not just a memory, it is a pattern that is still at work.

### VII. What the researchers say

Now listen to those who doubt, for doubt is also a form of respect.

The first to doubt is the very man who passed on the legend. Right after telling it, Beemster writes: "So far we have been speaking of an oral tradition. To our knowledge, no complete literary work or archaeological study corroborates this story." Ti kala is a legend. No one has found any trace of Klong, or Mbe's staff.

Then there is the name. For the Cameroonian historian Eldridge Mohammadou, whose major work, published in Tokyo in 1990, was summarised by David Zeitlyn in 1996, "Tikar" does not come from an insult. It would come from "Ti-ga": in the language of horsemen from the north, the Bare-Chamba, "ga" meant "chief". The Ti-ga were the people of the chief of the Ti. The Fulani would then have borrowed this name from the Mbum of Tibati and spread it. For Mohammadou, the Tikar kingdoms of the Mbam were born of the raids of these Bare-Chamba in the second half of the eighteenth century, and the Mbum origin is a "secondary re-interpretation". Zeitlyn, for his part, finds these invasions poorly proven. The debate is not closed.

The dates contradict each other too. The chief lists used by Mohammadou place the founding of Bankim around 1760-1780, Nditam around 1767-1781, Ngambé around 1788-1809. Mouiche, following Claude Tardits, speaks of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; the Bankim genealogy has Wouten reigning from 1201. And when Wang Sonne has the Mbum fleeing the Fulani at the beginning of the eighteenth century, he telescopes the eras: the Fulani holy war did not reach Adamawa until about 1805. No one can date Klong.

Then there is the great Tikar question, which the story of Nchare Yen introduced to you: the anthropologist David Price, who worked in Ngambé in the 1970s, found no linguistic, ethnic or truly historical evidence for calling the peoples of the Grassfields "Tikar", and Ian Fowler and David Zeitlyn see it as a claim made by dynasties rather than by peoples. Mouiche, for his part, calls it "an aberration" to speak of "Tikar tribes" for peoples who do not speak the language. Even museums get lost: in Berlin, a sword brought back by Curt von Morgen is labelled "Tikar" and located in Bamenda.

And then there is the history that hurts. In November 1889, Morgen crossed Tikar country. He writes that this great people had been subdued only the year before by the Fulani ruler of Tibati, after three years of resistance, and that its warriors now had to fight alongside their conqueror. Beemster, quoting the same author, speaks of a nine-year war waged by the Tikar of Ngambé; the number of years differs, the defeat is the same. The very name Ngambé, says Wang Sonne, is that of a chief who withstood the Fulani siege. In Bankim, people still point out the trenches dug against the horsemen. And the other face of that time is not hidden: "from Tikar country come kola nuts and slaves", writes Mouiche, and Tchimi, a man of Bankim, acknowledges that in the old days chiefs exploited and enslaved the common people.

The myth says nothing of all this. It does not need to. It says something else: who we are to one another.

### VIII. The stone and the chair

On 12 April 1964, a ceremony took place in Bankim that the ethnologist Claude Tardits reported, and that the historian Wang Sonne quotes from Paul Nkwi and Jean-Pierre Warnier. That day, the Fon of Nso and the Sultan of the Bamum, two of the most powerful rulers in western Cameroon, were seated on stones. The Tikar chief of Bankim, their host, sat on a chair. He turned to them and said: "I cannot give you chairs because you are only the sons of the King. I will enthrone you." Then, to his entourage: "Here are two children who left the country and went away as princes." Only then did he have chairs brought for them, first for the Sultan, then for the Fon, and he said to them: "You are now Kings."

Do you hear it? The stone and the chair. The mighty kings sit low, the small chief of the plain sits high, for the length of a ceremony. Wang Sonne wrote in 2000 that every new Bamum sultan must still present himself to the chief of Bankim, stop his car at the market and walk the two kilometres to the palace. And he adds that when the chief of Nditam comes to Foumban, the Sultan receives him with the honours due to a king, because Nditam is Mbe, and Mbe was the elder. He is the only one I found who says so; I give it to you as his word. The State has done its own sums: according to the official texts he cites, the chief of Bankim is only a second-class chief and the chief of Nditam a third-class one, while Foumban is a sultanate. Myth and administration do not tell the same hierarchy. The myth still tells its own.

Today, people from the plain gather in associations; a newspaper, *La Plaine Tikar*, has appeared since 1994; the Mbam'Art festival in Bafia has given their historians a platform. In Berlin, at the ethnological museum, a thumb piano called mbi, a brass bell and a photograph of a palmyra palm "in the village of Ngambe" wait under inventory numbers for someone to come and tell them where they came from.

So here is what I would like you to take with you.

One day, perhaps, someone will tell you "get out of here". A door will close, a rumour will stain you, you will be pushed out of the council where you thought you had a seat. Remember Klong. He did not stay crying at the door. He left, and those born of him turned the insult into a name still worn today. Remember Mbe, who lost his staff and did not turn back, because what he truly carried was knowledge in his hands. Remember Gwatou, who knew good land when he tasted it. And remember the monkeys of Nditam, who teach you that welcome is a power too.

The Tikar of the Mbam are not the most numerous. They are not the most famous. But when the great kings of the West want to say where they come from, it is towards their plain that they turn.

Ndè ntè. This land is pleasant. I am at home here.

---

## Structured profile

### "Ti kala!" The Banished Prince and the Lost Staff (Tikar of the Mbam)

- **Content type**: origin myth (the people's name and founding ancestor) and foundation myth (scattering of the kingdoms, founding of Gâ and Nditam), from oral tradition. Legend, not historical fact. The migrations of the Tikar's ancestors and the formation of the Mbam chiefdoms are historical, but their dates and causes are debated.
- **Peoples concerned**: Tikar of the Mbam (Nditam, Ngambé, Gâ, Kong, Ina, Ouè, Beng-Beng, Kpaga, Ngoumé, Mamblang, Gba), Tikar of Bankim (Adamawa), with the related traditions of the Bamum and the Nso.
- **Items from the Centre inventory covered**: T1 ("Ti kala!"), T2 (the lost staff), T3 (Gwatou and the gâ tree), T4 ("Ndè ntè", the monkeys of Nditam). T5 (the dragon of the spring, a tale recorded in Bankim) not included: a wonder tale outside this cycle.
- **Era**: pre-colonial (migrations dated, depending on the author, from the 16th to the early 19th century)
- **Region**: Central Africa (Centre Cameroon, Mbam-et-Kim division; Adamawa for Bankim and the Mbum country)
- **Theme**: other (origin and foundation myth, kingship, kinship between peoples)

**Timeline (history, distinct from the myth)**

- 18th century: according to Eldridge Mohammadou (1990, summarised by D. Zeitlyn, 1996), the Tikar kingdoms of the middle Mbam formed in the wake of Bare-Chamba raids; chief lists: Bankim c. 1760-1780, Nditam c. 1767-1781, Ngambé c. 1788-1809, Kong c. 1795. Other datings: 16th-17th centuries (Mouiche after Tardits); early 19th century for the settlement at Nditam (Wang Sonne after Dugast and Mohammadou 1971); 1201 for Princess Wouten in the Bankim dynastic genealogy.
- 1805-1806: start of the Fulani jihad in Adamawa; resistance by the peoples of the Banyo area, the Tikar included (Beemster after Hurault).
- 1888-1889: the Tikar subdued by the lamido of Tibati after several years of war (three according to Morgen, nine according to Beemster citing Morgen); Curt von Morgen crosses Tikar country in November 1889.
- 12 April 1964: in Bankim, the Fon of Nso and the Sultan of the Bamum, seated on stones, are "enthroned" by the Tikar chief seated on a chair (Tardits 1977, via Nkwi and Warnier, quoted by Wang Sonne).
- 1974-1996: Ngambé-Tikar becomes a district (11 March 1974), then a subdivision (1991); creation of the Mbam-et-Kim division (1 September 1992); bridge over the Kim opened (1996).

**Notable quotations (verified sources; English translations by AFROBACK from the French)**

- "Numerous concurring sources tell us that the name and the people of the Tikar were born of a quarrel between a chief (Bella Ka in Mbum) and his son Klong (or Klo), until then cherished or beloved." A. Wang Sonne, paper at Mbam'Art 2000, Bafia, 11 March 2000.
- "His father, surprised by his presence, sharply orders him in the Mbum language: 'Vi Ti Kala Je', which means in French: 'Get out of here! or go outside'." Wang Sonne, same source.
- "All the Tikar know a legend which explains the birth of their people from a quarrel between the Chief (or 'Belaka') of the Mboum [...] and one of his sons, called 'Klo'' [...]." B. Beemster, B. Tchimi and P. Mvouin, "Les Tikar de Bankim", 1993, p. 154.
- "All decided, on the day of their departure, to take the name of Tikar so that the insult done to the man they had just chosen as their chief would never be forgotten." I. Mouiche, 2012, p. 119, after E. Mohammadou 1971, p. 93.
- "I cannot give you chairs because you are only the sons of the King. I will enthrone you." The Tikar chief of Bankim to the Fon of Nso and the Sultan of the Bamum, 12 April 1964 (Tardits 1977, via Nkwi and Warnier 1982, French translation quoted by Wang Sonne 2000).
- "Ndè ntè": "Something I take pleasure in loving, or, this land is pleasant, I am at ease here" (in the Balom language, according to Wang Sonne 2000).
- "Tikar is a tonal language. It is also the language of the Tumu." Basile Tchimi, in Beemster et al., 1993.

**Debates and uncertainties**

- Who is driven out: a slandered son (Wang Sonne), an "accused" son (Beemster), a brother too dear to the people (Mouiche after Mohammadou), a princess, Wouten (Bankim tradition, Nso tradition). Hagège records four versions, not read.
- The name Tikar: the Mbum insult "ti kala" (tradition); "Ti-ga", chief of the Ti, a Bare-Chamba term passed on by the Mbum of Tibati to the Fulani (Mohammadou); an administrative name for people who call themselves Tumu (Beemster). No word-by-word gloss of the Mbum phrase was found.
- Mbum origin: asserted by every tradition, judged a "secondary re-interpretation" by Mohammadou; the Tikar speak the Bantoid language of the Tumu, not Mbum.
- Conquest or alliance with the Tumu: both memories coexist.
- The seven-year rule and the killing of the chief: told for Bankim by Wang Sonne alone; a "seven-year term" period appears in the Bankim dynastic list; a single seven-year term and ritual regicide attributed to the Mbum by T. M. Bah. Whether it applied in Mbe's time cannot be verified.
- The lost staff, Gâ and Nditam rest on a single source read (Wang Sonne, who refers to Mohammadou 1971). The gâ tree is unidentified.
- Seniority: Mbe the elder and Nchare the younger (Mbam); Ngonnso the eldest sister (Nso); Nchare the hero of the parting (Foumban). Another name for the founder of Nditam (Doundje) circulates via Wikipedia, attributed to Price, unverified.
- Length of the resistance to Tibati: three years (Morgen) or nine (Beemster). Wang Sonne places a flight from the Fulani at the start of the 18th century, which is anachronistic.
- Not consulted (library): Mohammadou 1971 and 1990, Hagège 1969, Price 1979, 1985, 1987, Charrin 1932, Mgbékoum 2006 (*Tinkala djé*), Abbo Nyedjim (*Généalogie des rois tikar de Bankim*), the newspaper *La Plaine Tikar*, the village monographs in the Ngambé-Tikar subdivision archives, the SIL Tikar dictionary (Webonary, inaccessible).

**Associated legends (explicitly marked as such)**

- Prince Klong (Klo') driven out by his father the Belaka with the cry "Vi ti kala je!".
- Princess Wouten barred from the men's sacred house, followed by the generals Tinki and Klo (Bankim version).
- Mbe fleeing ritual death at the end of a seven-year reign, with the complicity of a wife who is also his sister.
- The lost staff of office, Nchare losing his way while looking for it and founding the Bamum kingdom, Ngonsok weary of waiting and founding Nso.
- Gwatou held back by the fruit of the gâ tree, founder of Gâ and first Mbonga.
- Mbe the blacksmith, victor over the Balom and the Djanti, greeted by the monkeys, naming the place "Ndè ntè".
